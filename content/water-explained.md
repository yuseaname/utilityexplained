## Quick Answer
<p class="hub-byline" style="font-size:.9rem;color:#666;margin-top:-8px;">Edited by the Utility Explained team · Last updated August 2026</p>
<p>
Your water bill charges for three things: a fixed service charge, the water you used (billed per CCF or per gallon), and sewer/wastewater treatment, which is usually based on your water usage. One CCF equals 100 cubic feet, or about 748 gallons, so a bill showing "6 CCF" means roughly 4,488 gallons. Rates, tiers, and fees vary by utility, so use the router below to jump straight to the guide that answers your question.
</p>

## How water service works
<p>
Your utility treats water and pipes it to your meter, then bills you for what you use; wastewater leaves through a separate drain system to a treatment plant. For how those charges land on your statement, see <a href="/blog/06-water-bill-too-high/">Why Is My Water Bill So High?</a> or the <a href="/utilities-glossary/">utility glossary</a>.
</p>

## How water is measured: gallons, cubic feet, and CCF
<p>
Bills measure water by volume — gallons, cubic feet, or CCF (100 cubic feet, about 748 gallons). For the unit definitions, see the <a href="/utilities-glossary/#ccf-water">CCF (water) glossary entry</a>.
</p>

## How to read your water meter
<p>
Dial and digital meters both show cumulative usage you can verify against your bill, and most have a leak indicator. For meter-reading terms, see the <a href="/utilities-glossary/#meter-reading">meter reading glossary entry</a>.
</p>

## How to detect leaks and reduce waste
<p>
Leaks are a common cause of high water bills, and even small ones waste hundreds of gallons a month. <strong>Toilet test:</strong> add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing — if color reaches the bowl, the flapper is leaking and should be replaced. <strong>Meter test:</strong> turn off all water, then check the meter — if the leak indicator moves or the reading changes after an hour, water is flowing somewhere.
</p>
### Common leak sources
<ul>
<li>Toilet flappers (most common indoor leak)</li>
<li>Dripping faucets and showerheads</li>
<li>Leaking washing machine hoses</li>
<li>Broken sprinkler lines or irrigation valves</li>
<li>Outdoor hose bibs left partially open</li>
</ul>
<p>
Fixing leaks quickly can save thousands of gallons per month and significantly reduce your bill.
</p>

## Common misconceptions about water service
### Misconception: Running water while the dishwasher runs wastes more water
<p>
The dishwasher uses a fixed amount per cycle regardless; a running faucet adds to total usage but does not change the dishwasher's draw.
</p>
### Misconception: Low-flow fixtures sacrifice performance
<p>
Modern low-flow showerheads and faucets maintain pressure and comfort while using less water; most users notice no difference.
</p>
### Misconception: Water meters are always accurate
<p>
Meters are generally accurate but can slow or fail over time. If usage looks unusually high, request a meter test from your utility.
</p>

## Estimated readings and bill corrections
<p>
If the utility cannot access your meter, it may estimate usage, and the next actual reading corrects the bill. You can often submit your own reading online or by phone, and if a bill looks wrong, compare it to your meter and request a review. For how estimates work, see <a href="/blog/14-estimated-utility-bill-explained/">Estimated Utility Bill Explained</a>.
</p>

## Frequently asked questions
### How much water does the average person use per day?
<p>In the United States, a typical person uses 40 to 80 gallons per day for indoor activities. Outdoor watering can add significantly more.</p>
### Why is my sewer charge higher than my water charge?
<p>Wastewater treatment is expensive. Many utilities charge more for sewer service because treatment plants require significant maintenance and energy.</p>
### Can I lower my water bill if I don't have a lawn?
<p>Yes. Indoor conservation measures like fixing leaks, taking shorter showers, and running full loads in appliances can all reduce usage and lower your bill.</p>
### What is a winter average, and how does it affect my bill?
<p>Some utilities calculate sewer charges based on winter water usage, when outdoor watering is minimal. This average is used year-round to avoid charging sewer fees for water that goes to lawns or pools.</p>
### Should I worry about a small drip from a faucet?
<p>Yes. A slow drip can waste 10 to 20 gallons per day, which adds up to hundreds of gallons per month. Fixing drips is usually simple and inexpensive.</p>
