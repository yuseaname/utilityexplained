## First, What Standby Loss Actually Is

A tank water heater keeps 40–80 gallons of water hot around the clock, whether or not anyone uses it. The heat that escapes through the tank walls between uses is **standby loss** — the water heater's version of a car idling in the driveway.

{{< visual src="/images/articles/are-water-heater-blankets-worth-it/standby-loss.webp" alt="Diagram of standby loss: heat arrows leaving a stored hot water tank throughout the day." wide="true" >}}

Standby loss is why the Department of Energy's water-heating guidance treats insulation as a first-line efficiency measure: a tank that's warm to the touch is a tank that's actively losing heat, and the fix is either more insulation around it or a lower setpoint.[1] The two levers work on the same loss — a blanket and a 120°F setpoint both reduce how fast the stored water cools.

The key question is **how much insulation your tank already has**:

- **Modern tanks** (roughly the last decade) ship with factory foam insulation rated around **R-16 or better** — often R-24 or higher on ENERGY STAR models. The DOE's threshold for adding a blanket is a tank R-value under about 24, which most new tanks clear.[1]
- **Older tanks** (10–20+ years) often have thin fiberglass or minimal factory foam — R-5 to R-12 territory — and are exactly the tanks that feel warm to the touch.

## The Three Tanks That Actually Need It

The blanket question splits cleanly by tank type and location:

- **Older tanks (10+ years), thin factory foam** — the textbook case. The tank feels warm to the touch because it's losing heat through walls that were thin when the tank was built. This is where the $20–45/year band applies.
- **Electric tanks in unheated garages and basements** — even a modern tank loses more heat when the surrounding air is cold, because standby loss is driven by the temperature difference between the stored water and the room. A 50-gallon tank in a 40°F garage sheds heat much faster than the same tank in a 70°F closet, and the blanket narrows that gap.
- **Gas tanks with no factory jacket** — older gas tanks often shipped with minimal insulation around the jacket. Same rule: warm to the touch means it needs the blanket — installed carefully around the burner access, per above.

A tank that is **not** in the buy list: a modern, well-insulated tank in a conditioned space. It feels room-temperature, it already clears the DOE's R-value threshold, and the blanket's payback stretches past its remaining life.[1]

## The Math: What It Actually Saves

Here's the honest arithmetic, with every assumption stated. National average residential electricity is **18.34¢/kWh** (EIA, June 2026),[2] and the average household uses about **899 kWh/month**.[3] Water heating is typically the second-largest home energy use — roughly 18% of a typical home's energy, per DOE's water-heating guidance.[1]

**The savings range, honestly:**

- DOE and ENERGY STAR guidance puts the standby-loss reduction from a blanket at roughly **4–9% of water-heating energy** on tanks that need it.[1]
- A typical household spends about **$400–600/year on water heating** (electric at national average rates). Take the middle, **$500/year**:
  - 4% savings = **$20/year**
  - 9% savings = **$45/year**
- So the honest band is **$20–45/year** on a tank that actually needs the blanket.

**Payback on the ~$33 blanket:**

- At $45/year: 33 ÷ 45 = **~0.7 years** (about 8–9 months)
- At $20/year: 33 ÷ 20 = **~1.7 years** (about 20 months)

So the honest payback is **one to two winters** — and that's the whole verdict in one number.

{{< visual src="/images/articles/are-water-heater-blankets-worth-it/payback-timeline.webp" alt="Payback timeline from a thirty-three dollar jacket to one to two winters of savings." wide="true" >}}

**And the case where it's NOT worth it:** on a modern R-16+ tank, the blanket adds maybe 1–3% on top of factory insulation — call it **$5–15/year**:

- At $15/year: 33 ÷ 15 = **~2.2 years**
- At $5/year: 33 ÷ 5 = **~6.6 years**

A 6-year payback on a tank that may only have 5 years left is not a purchase — it's a donation. That's why the honest answer is "it depends on the tank," not "buy one."

**The gas-tank version of the math:** if your water heater runs on natural gas, the savings scale with your gas rate rather than the electric rate. A typical household's gas water-heating spend is lower than electric — often **$250–400/year** — so the blanket's band becomes roughly **$10–35/year**:

- At $35/year: 33 ÷ 35 = **~0.9 years**
- At $10/year: 33 ÷ 10 = **~3.3 years**

Same verdict, slightly longer payback: worth it on an old warm tank, not on a modern one. The gas-bill angle is covered in [Is Your Gas Bill Too High?](/blog/07-gas-bill-too-high/), which treats the water heater as one of the top gas-waste suspects.

## Which Should You Actually Buy?

- **Tank is warm to the touch, 10+ years old** → yes, buy the blanket. $33 for $20–45/year is a 1–2 winter payback.
- **Electric tank in an unheated garage or basement** → yes, even on a newer tank — cold surroundings multiply standby loss.
- **Gas tank with no factory jacket** → yes, but keep the blanket clear of the burner access panel and flue per the instructions.
- **Modern tank, R-16+, in a conditioned space** → skip it. The savings don't clear the payback horizon.
- **Not sure?** → the touch test is the honest shortcut: if the tank feels warm, it's losing heat; if it feels room-temperature, the blanket has little to grab.

And the standing rule: **insulation is a multiplier, not a substitute.** A blanket on a tank set to 140°F saves less than a tank set to 120°F with no blanket — the setpoint is free and immediate.
