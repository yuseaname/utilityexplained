## Quick Answer: What Utilities Cost in California {#quick-answer}

**Electricity:** California's residential rate averaged **29.75¢/kWh in 2026** (EIA preliminary year-to-date; 30.92¢ final for 2025) — roughly 60% above the 18.16¢/kWh national average. At the EIA national-standard 899 kWh month, that is **$267/month** for electricity, versus $163 nationally.

**Natural gas:** California residential gas averaged **$22.01/thousand cubic feet in 2025** (EIA annual) — about **$2.12/therm** after conversion, above most of the country. A standardized 75-therm heating month runs **$159**.

**Water & sewer:** there is **no reliable state average** — water is priced by your city or water district, and drought-era rate structures make the same usage cost very different amounts across town lines. Nationally the combined bill runs about $70–$90/month; [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/) decodes yours in two minutes.

Before comparing your bill to anyone else's, read the next section — in California, *who sends the bill* changes the numbers as much as any usage habit.

## Who actually serves you in California {#who-serves-you}

California re-regulated after its 2000–01 electricity crisis, so for most households there is **no shopping for an electric provider** — but the bill itself has layers that confuse people:

1. **The big three investor-owned utilities** — Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E), Southern California Edison (SCE), and San Diego Gas & Electric (SDG&E) — own the wires and send the bill for the majority of Californians. Municipal utilities (LADWP, SMUD, and others) serve their own cities with separately set rates.
2. **Community Choice Aggregation (CCA)** — in many counties, a public agency buys the *generation* on your behalf, while your investor-owned utility still delivers it and bills you. If a name you don't recognize appears on the generation line (Clean Power Alliance, Marin Clean Energy, Silicon Valley Clean Energy, and others), you're on CCA default service — and you can usually choose a greener or cheaper tier.
3. **Direct Access / third-party suppliers** — a limited set of non-residential customers can buy from third-party suppliers. Residential customers generally cannot.

Why this matters for the numbers below: CCA territories and municipal-utility cities can price generation below or above the bundled investor-owned rate. The EIA figure is a statewide blend — your own bill can sit a few cents per kWh to either side of it depending on where you live and which rate plan you're on.

## Why California rates are structurally high {#why-california-rates-are-structurally-high}

The gap between 29.75¢ and the national 18.16¢ is not one decision — it is a stack:

- **Wildfire liability and mitigation.** After the 2017–18 fire seasons, the state's wildfire fund and the utilities' undergrounding and grid-hardening programs are recovered through delivery charges on every kWh.
- **Public-policy surcharges.** Efficiency incentives, low-income discounts (including the CARE and FERA programs below), and nuclear decommissioning are collected from ratepayers rather than taxes.
- **Climate and usage math.** California's per-customer electricity sales are lower than in deep-summer states like Texas or Florida, while the grid must still serve a sharp evening peak as solar output rolls off. Fixed grid costs spread over fewer kWh push the per-kWh price up.
- **The net-metering transition.** The shift to net billing for rooftop solar moved more fixed-cost recovery onto non-solar customers — a real and contested driver in recent rate cases.

None of this is a billing error you can dispute. But two levers below — the discount programs and your rate-plan choice — move a California bill by more than any efficiency gadget.

## The discount ladder: CARE → FERA → Medical Baseline {#the-discount-ladder}

California runs the most generous utility-bill discount structure in the country, and enrollment gaps are common — many eligible households simply never apply. Check these **in order**:

1. **CARE (California Alternate Rates for Energy).** Income-qualified households get a meaningful discount — about a third off — on both electric and gas rates, applied directly to the rate you pay. Enrollment runs through your utility, and households receiving certain other public assistance may be auto-enrolled. Verify current income thresholds and terms on the [CPUC's CARE/FERA page](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/industries-and-topics/electrical-energy/electric-costs/care-fera).
2. **FERA (Family Electric Rate Assistance).** For households of three or more with income slightly above the CARE ceiling, FERA offers a smaller electric-rate discount — 18% on the CPUC's published terms. It is electric-only, not gas.
3. **Medical Baseline.** If a resident has a qualifying medical condition (life-support equipment, heat- or cooling-sensitive conditions, and others), the household receives an additional baseline allocation of electricity *and* natural gas at the lowest tier rate. Details: [CPUC Medical Baseline](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/industries-and-topics/electrical-energy/electric-costs/medical-baseline).

A household that qualifies for CARE and claims it typically saves more per year than a new refrigerator — and unlike efficiency projects, the discount repeats every month with no equipment.

## Your rate plan choice: time-of-use is now the default {#your-rate-plan-choice}

Most residential customers of the big three utilities have been moved to **time-of-use (TOU) rates**: electricity costs more during peak hours (typically late afternoon into evening) and less overnight and midday. Two consequences:

- Shifting flexible load — laundry, dishwasher, EV charging — into off-peak windows cuts your effective rate without reducing a single kWh. The full mechanics: [how time-of-use electricity pricing works](/blog/time-of-use-electricity/).
- If your usage is rigid (work-from-home, medical equipment running all day), check whether your utility still offers a tiered plan with no time component — [which rate plan fits your household](/blog/which-rate-plan-is-right-for-me/) walks the trade-off.

On the gas side, winter physics applies as everywhere: the standardized 75-therm month below doubles or triples in January for gas-heated homes. [Why your heating bill is so high](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/) breaks down the seasonal stack.

## Water and sewer in California {#water-and-sewer-in-california}

Water is priced by your city or water district — and much of the state now uses **drought budget rate structures**: each household gets a water allocation based on household size (and, in some districts, landscape area), and usage above it jumps to sharply higher tiers. Two identical houses on opposite sides of a district line can pay very different amounts for the same gallons.

Practical checks, in order:

1. **Decode your units.** Most California bills are in CCF (sometimes labeled HCF — hundred cubic feet): [CCF on a water bill, explained](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/).
2. **Find your tier breakpoints.** If your bill shows Tier 1/2/3 allocations, staying inside Tier 1 is the single biggest water saving available to you.
3. **Check for leaks at the meter.** The 10-minute meter test: [water meter running when nothing is on](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/).
4. **Read the fixed charges.** Post-drought revenue recovery has pushed base charges up sharply in many districts — your bill can rise even with flat usage. More: [why your water bill is high](/blog/water-bill-too-high/).

A $267 standardized month assumes 899 kWh at the blended state rate. Real California bills run above it mostly for three reasons: summer air conditioning in the inland counties, EV charging on a peak-heavy time-of-use plan, and electric heat or a pool pump on an unfavorable plan.

{{< faq "Why is electricity so much more expensive in California than the national average?">}}

California's 29.75¢/kWh (2026 EIA preliminary) versus the national 18.16¢ reflects wildfire-mitigation and liability costs, public-policy surcharges collected through rates, and fixed grid costs spread over comparatively low per-customer usage. It is a structural rate difference, not a billing error — which is why the discount and rate-plan levers in this guide matter more here than efficiency gadgets.

{{< faq "How much does the CARE discount save on a California utility bill?">}}

CARE cuts about a third off electric and gas rates for income-qualified households, applied directly to the rate — on the standardized 899 kWh month in this guide, that is on the order of $80–90/month at California rates. FERA offers 18% off electric for qualifying households of three or more with slightly higher income. Check current thresholds with your utility or the CPUC's CARE/FERA page, linked in the sources.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I choose my electricity provider in California?">}}

Generally no — residential service is bundled with your regulated utility (PG&E, SCE, SDG&E, or a municipal utility such as LADWP or SMUD). The choices most Californians do have are the rate plan (time-of-use versus tiered, where offered) and, in Community Choice Aggregation territories, the generation tier. That is the opposite of states like Texas, where the supply rate itself is shoppable.
