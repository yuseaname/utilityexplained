## Quick Answer

**A furnace that won't turn on is usually stopped by something in the first three checks: the thermostat (set to Heat, fresh batteries, temperature called up), the furnace's wall switch (a light-style switch that someone flipped off), or a tripped breaker.** Manufacturer troubleshooting guides put exactly these at the top of the no-heat list.[1] Work the checks in order — each takes under a minute — before paying for a service call. If the furnace powers up but never ignites, or blinks an error code, that narrows the problem considerably; the sections below cover both. And two reasons to stop immediately: a rotten-egg smell ([possible gas leak — leave and call](/blog/gas-leak-signs-what-to-do-bill-impact/)) or a carbon-monoxide alarm.

## The check order: six stops before the phone call {#check-order}

{{< visual src="/images/articles/furnace-wont-turn-on/check-order-chain.webp" alt="The furnace no-heat check order as a five-step icon chain: thermostat, service switch, breaker, air filter, furnace door." wide="true" >}}


### 1. The thermostat

Set the mode to **Heat** (not Fan or Cool) and push the set temperature a few degrees above the room temperature. If the display is blank or dim, replace the batteries — on battery thermostats, dead batteries are the single most common "furnace died" cause. If it is hardwired, check that it seats level on its base; a half-unclipped thermostat can drop its wire contacts. After any change, wait two to three minutes: most thermostats delay the call for heat to protect the equipment, and impatience at this step causes people to misdiagnose a working furnace as dead.[1]

### 2. The furnace switch

Gas furnaces need electricity for the blower and controls, and nearly every furnace has a wall switch near the unit — it looks exactly like a light switch and gets flipped by accident, by cleaning, or by a child. Make sure it is on. This is check number three on manufacturer lists for a reason.[1]

### 3. The breaker

Find the electrical panel and look for a tripped breaker labeled furnace or heat. Flip it fully off, then on. If it trips again, do not keep resetting it — repeated tripping means an electrical problem that needs a technician, and the breaker is doing its job.[1]

### 4. The filter and the limit switch

A badly clogged filter restricts airflow until the furnace's high-limit switch shuts the burners down to prevent overheating. If your filter is gray and fuzzy or you cannot remember its age, replace it — this is the cheapest fix on the list. Restricted airflow shows up elsewhere too: [weak airflow from the vents is its own diagnostic](/blog/duct-leaks-symptoms-hvac-running-all-day/), and a furnace that runs but blows cool is usually a different fault, covered in [why furnaces blow cold air](/blog/why-is-my-furnace-blowing-cold-air/).

### 5. The door switch

The blower door has a safety switch that kills power when the door is off or not fully seated. If you (or a filter change) left the door slightly ajar, the furnace will act completely dead. Push the door firmly until it latches.[1]

### 6. The vents outside

High-efficiency furnaces breathe through two plastic pipes through the wall. In winter, ice, snow drifts, or a bird's nest blocking either pipe trips the pressure switch and the furnace locks out before ignition. A walk around the house clears more no-heat calls than you would expect.

## When it powers up but never ignites {#ignition}

If the blower runs and the thermostat calls for heat but the burners never light, the ignition system is the suspect. Older furnaces use a standing pilot — if the pilot is out, the burners have nothing to ignite them, and a pilot that will not stay lit has its own causes and safety rules, covered in [pilot light keeps going out](/blog/pilot-light-keeps-going-out/). Newer furnaces use a hot-surface igniter or spark ignition, and an igniter failure needs a technician — the part is inexpensive, but it sits inside the burner compartment.[1]

One more ignition-adjacent cause worth knowing: on condensing furnaces a blocked condensate drain can trip a safety lockout. Technicians report drain clogs as a recurring winter no-heat cause; the fix is clearing the condensate trap and lines.

## Error codes: the furnace is telling you the answer {#error-codes}

{{< visual src="/images/articles/furnace-wont-turn-on/error-code-panel.webp" alt="A furnace access door sight glass revealing the blinking fault-indicator light inside the control panel." wide="true" >}}


Modern furnaces flash a light on the control board — through a sight glass on the door — in coded blinks. A steady or repeating blink pattern corresponds to a specific fault listed on a code chart pasted inside the door or in the manual: pressure switch, limit switch, ignition lockout, and so on. Reading the code before you call turns a "no heat, unknown cause" service call into "pressure switch fault," which gets the right part on the truck the first visit. Take a photo of the blink pattern and the chart.

## When to stop and get help {#when-to-stop}

Stop troubleshooting and act immediately if:

- You smell rotten eggs or gas — leave the house and call your gas company from outside. The [gas leak signs guide](/blog/gas-leak-signs-what-to-do-bill-impact/) covers what to do, including the free utility leak check.
- A carbon-monoxide alarm sounds — get everyone out and call for help; CPSC's carbon-monoxide center covers alarm response.[2]
- The breaker trips repeatedly, you see scorching, or the furnace shut down with a burning smell — that smell has its own triage in [furnace smells like burning](/blog/furnace-smells-like-burning/).

Otherwise, if one full pass through the check order does not restore heat, a service call is the reasonable next step — you have already ruled out the causes that do not need a technician.

## What the repair call is likely to cost {#costs}

No-heat repairs cluster around a few parts: a igniter or flame sensor, a capacitor, a contactor, a pressure switch, or a control board, plus the diagnostic fee. Part costs are modest; labor and the trip charge are most of the bill. Season timing matters — the first cold snap is peak demand, and wait times stretch. Regular maintenance reduces the odds of the midnight no-heat call in the first place; the [furnace tune-up cost and schedule guide](/blog/furnace-tune-up-cost-schedule/) covers what a tune-up includes and when it is worth it.

## FAQ

{{< faq "Why would my furnace suddenly stop working?" >}}
The sudden no-heat causes are almost always in the check order above: a thermostat battery died, someone switched the furnace switch off, or a breaker tripped. Power and control failures feel catastrophic but are usually trivial to restore; genuinely dead furnaces are rare compared with switched-off or locked-out ones.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How do I know if my furnace switch is on?" >}}
The furnace switch looks like a standard light switch on the wall or ceiling joist next to the unit. With the switch on and the door properly latched, the control board usually shows a light through the sight glass. No light anywhere on the furnace means it is not getting power — that is switch, breaker, or door switch territory before anything else.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can a dirty filter really stop the furnace from turning on?" >}}
It can stop the burners. Severe airflow restriction makes the heat exchanger overheat, and the high-limit switch shuts the burners down as protection. The blower may still run, which reads as "furnace on but no heat" — a symptom with its own causes in [why furnaces blow cold air](/blog/why-is-my-furnace-blowing-cold-air/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Should I keep resetting a tripped breaker?" >}}
No. Reset once. A breaker that trips again is announcing an overload or a fault on the circuit, and repeated resets push heat into wiring that is already struggling. Leave it off and call an electrician or HVAC technician — that call is cheaper than the fire a failing circuit can cause.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How long should I wait after turning the thermostat up?" >}}
Give it two to three minutes. Thermostats and furnace controls intentionally delay startup to protect the equipment, and the quiet gap after a call for heat is normal. No blower, no igniter glow, and no burner sound after five minutes means move on to the next check.
{{< /faq >}}
