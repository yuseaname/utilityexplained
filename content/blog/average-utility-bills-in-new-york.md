## Quick Answer: What Utilities Cost in New York {#quick-answer}

**Electricity:** the New York residential rate averaged **28.65¢/kWh in 2026** (EIA preliminary year-to-date; 26.64¢ final for 2025) — well above the 18.16¢/kWh national average. At the EIA national-standard 899 kWh month, that is **$258/month** for electricity, versus $163 nationally.

**Natural gas:** New York residential gas averaged **$17.58/thousand cubic feet in 2025** (EIA annual) — about **$1.69/therm** after conversion, cheaper than the ~$1.92/therm national snapshot. A standardized 75-therm heating month runs **$127**.

**Water & sewer:** a statewide number doesn't exist — New York water is priced city-by-city (and New York City's water-sewer bill is its own animal). Nationally the combined bill runs about $70–$90/month; [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/) decodes yours.

The number most New Yorkers should check first isn't on the averages table, though — it's the **supply line** of their bill. That's the one part you can act on this week.

## The two New Yorks: who delivers your power {#the-two-new-yorks}

New York's rates are really two markets averaged together:

- **Downstate** — Consolidated Edison (ConEd) covers most of New York City and Westchester; PSEG Long Island runs the Long Island Power Authority grid. ConEd's delivery territory is consistently among the most expensive in the country: dense underground infrastructure, an aging network, and long-run storm-hardening programs all sit in the delivery charge.
- **Upstate** — National Grid, NYSEG and RG&E (both Avangrid), Central Hudson, and Orange & Rockland cover most of the rest. Delivery rates are closer to (though still above) national norms, and winter heating drives the bill more than summer cooling.

Your utility is fixed by address — you cannot choose your delivery company anywhere in the state. What most New Yorkers *can* choose is the **supply** side, which is where the ESCO question comes in.

## The ESCO decision: shop your supply or stay on default {#the-esco-decision}

New York opened electric and gas supply to competition: an **ESCO (Energy Service Company)** can sell you the supply portion while your utility continues delivering it and billing you. The decision has a well-earned reputation for burning people — so use a checklist, not a doorstep pitch:

1. **Find your comparison anchor.** Your utility publishes a default **"supply price to compare"** — the rate you pay for supply if you do nothing. Any ESCO offer only makes sense measured against *that number*, not against last month's total bill.
2. **Fixed vs variable.** A fixed-rate ESCO contract prices your supply for a stated term; variable rates float monthly and are where most horror stories start. If an offer doesn't state the term and the exit rules in writing, decline.
3. **Do the math at YOUR usage.** A 1¢/therm or 1¢/kWh saving at 899 kWh is about $9/month. Add any monthly ESCO fee before deciding it's a win — many "green supply" plans carry fees that erase the spread.
4. **Know the exit.** New York rules let you drop an ESCO without a long commitment in most cases — confirm the cancellation terms before signing, and if a marketer's claims look wrong, the [Department of Public Service](https://dps.ny.gov) takes complaints.
5. **The honest default is fine.** Staying on your utility's default supply is a legitimate choice. Unlike Texas, where sitting on a holdover rate is usually a mistake, in New York the default supply price is regulated and often competitive.

If you heat with electricity or run a big EV load, also check your utility's time-of-use options before any ESCO conversation — [how time-of-use electricity pricing works](/blog/time-of-use-electricity/) — because a rate-structure change can beat any supply discount.

**The heat-pump wrinkle.** New York's climate-favorable heat-pump programs have put cold-climate heat pumps into thousands of upstate homes — and electric resistance "backup strips" come with them. In January, those strips can double or triple an electric bill overnight even though nothing is broken: the heat pump leans on resistance heat exactly when electricity demand (and some delivery pricing) peaks. If your winter electric bill exploded the year you got a heat pump, check whether backup heat is doing more than backup — [aux heat vs emergency heat, explained](/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/), and [why your heating bill is so high](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/) covers the rest of the seasonal stack.

## The tax-and-surcharge stack {#the-tax-and-surcharge-stack}

A New York utility bill carries one of the heavier tax and surcharge stacks in the country: state and local sales taxes on the energy itself, a state gross-receipts tax in some territories, the System Benefits Charge (funding efficiency and low-income programs), and various riders for renewable and clean-energy programs. On many downstate bills the combined taxes-and-surcharges block adds a noticeable percentage on top of the commodity + delivery cost.

You can't shop these away — but you should read them, for two reasons:

- **They scale with usage.** Every kWh you cut also cuts the taxes and surcharges riding on it, so efficiency savings are bigger than they look.
- **Some carry exemptions.** Low-income households may qualify for relief programs through their utility (each major New York utility runs one), and the state's HEAP program helps with winter heating costs for eligible households — apply through your county social services office.

## Water and sewer in New York {#water-and-sewer-in-new-york}

Water is set by your city, town, or water district — there is no state rate. New York City's water and sewer charge (billed by the DEP, with metered usage and a fixed multifamily charge) is a different structure from upstate municipal systems, so even within one state the "average water bill" question has no single answer.

The checks that work everywhere:

1. **Decode the units.** Most New York bills measure in CCF (hundred cubic feet): [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/).
2. **Benchmark usage.** A typical household runs about 2–4 CCF per person per month; well above that, a leak is the usual suspect ([the 10-minute meter check](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/)).
3. **Check fixed charges.** Many systems have raised base charges faster than usage rates — your bill can climb even with flat usage. [Why your water bill is high](/blog/water-bill-too-high/) walks the whole statement.

## If your bill runs above these numbers {#if-your-bill-runs-above-these-numbers}

A $258 standardized month assumes 899 kWh at the blended state rate. Real New York bills run above it mostly for three reasons: winter electric heating or heat-pump backup strips, an ESCO supply rate quietly above the default, and downstate delivery territory (ConEd country).

{{< faq "Should I switch to an ESCO in New York?">}}

Only if a written fixed-rate offer beats your utility's published supply price to compare at your actual usage, with fees included. New York's regulated default supply is often competitive, and variable-rate ESCO plans are where most overpayment stories start. Measure any offer against your utility's current default supply rate before switching, and verify cancellation terms — the Department of Public Service takes complaints about misleading marketing.

{{< faq "Why are New York electric rates so high?">}}

The 2026 EIA average of 28.65¢/kWh versus 18.16¢ nationally reflects expensive downstate delivery infrastructure (dense underground networks and storm hardening), a heavy state-and-local tax and surcharge stack, and clean-energy program riders.
