## Quick Answer

**Per the U.S. EIA's Winter Fuels Outlook (Oct 6, 2026), this winter splits cleanly by fuel: natural gas homes (about half of U.S. households) should pay about 9% LESS — roughly $640 for November through March; electric-heat homes (over 40% of households) about 4% MORE — roughly $1,196; propane homes about 3% less ($1,246); and heating-oil homes (about 3% of households, mostly Northeast) a painful 21% MORE — $2,115, on fuel averaging $5.26/gallon, up 34%.** Regionally the story bends around a strong El Niño: warmer East, much colder West — gas bills in the West actually RISE ~7% despite cheaper gas, and electric bills there rise ~9%.

January is the peak month for every fuel: gas averages $146, electric heat $270, oil $509 in January alone.

Two mechanical notes worth understanding before reading anything into these numbers. First, the EIA is explicit that these are **broad comparisons of this winter vs. last winter for typical homes on each fuel** — not a ranking of which fuel is "cheaper to have," because the housing stocks differ (electric-heat homes skew newer and milder-climate; oil homes skew old and Northeast). Second, prices drive most of this year's divergence: gas is cheaper, oil much dearer, while weather (El Niño) redistributes the regional pain.

## The Weather Caveat (Read Before Budgeting)

EIA's forecast assumes national temperatures similar to last winter, warmer in the Northeast, much colder in the West, and it flags a **strong El Niño** — which historically produces large regional deviations from any smooth forecast. EIA publishes side cases for warmer and colder winters, and updates the whole outlook monthly alongside its Short-Term Energy Outlook. Treat these tables as the center of the distribution, not a promise: a February polar vortex moves every number on this page.

## How EIA Builds This Forecast (Methodology, Honestly)

Understanding the machinery keeps you from over-trusting it. EIA starts from the October Short-Term Energy Outlook's price paths for each fuel, layers on NOAA's winter temperature forecast by region, and applies both to the per-fuel household population from its consumption surveys. The output is the expected consumption × price = expenditure table you read above, with side cases for warmer and colder winters. Three honest limitations: it models the AVERAGE home on each fuel (your house's size, envelope, and equipment move you off-center); weather forecasts at seasonal range are probabilistic (El Niño tilts odds, it doesn't issue guarantees); and prices for delivered fuels (oil, propane) vary locally far more than pipeline gas or grid electricity. Treat the table as the center of a distribution, check it monthly as EIA updates, and reconcile it against your own bill history — the method for reading YOUR bill's usage-vs-rate split is the [heating bill diagnostic](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/)'s framework.

## Your Own Bill vs. the Forecast (The Reconciliation That Matters)

The forecast number is a national average for your fuel; your bill is your house. To see where you'll land:

1. **Pull last winter's bills** (Nov-Mar). Sum the HEATING portion — for gas homes, roughly the winter total minus the ~$20-40/month summer baseline of water heating and cooking.
2. **Apply your fuel's percentage** from the headline table: gas homes ×0.91, electric-heat ×1.04, propane ×0.97, oil ×1.21 — then your region's variant from the regional tables if it differs sharply (West-region gas homes ×1.07, for instance).
3. **Adjust for your own changes:** new windows, a new thermostat schedule ([the winter settings guide](/blog/what-temperature-thermostat-winter/)), a furnace replacement, or an extra person home all day move you off last year's base by more than this forecast does.
4. **Budget the January spike:** whatever your seasonal total, January runs roughly 23% of it (gas $146/$640, electric $270/$1,196, oil $509/$2,115). Level-payment/budget billing with your utility or fuel supplier flattens exactly this spike — worth setting up in November, not in January.

## Delivered-Fuel Playbook (Oil and Propane Specifics)

Homes with truck-delivered fuel have moves pipeline and grid homes don't: **pre-buy or capped-price contracts** (locking a season price early — this year, locking BEFORE the winter was worth 21% on oil), **budget billing** with the supplier to flatten January, **group/buying-cooperative pricing** in some rural markets, and **fill timing** (spot prices tend to crest with demand in deep winter). The honest caveat for 2026-27: locks are insurance, not arbitrage — a warm winter can leave you having paid over spot. Size any lock so a mild-season regret is annoying, not painful.

{{< faq "Why is heating oil so expensive this winter?" >}}

Milder Northeast weather offsets only part of that, leaving oil-heated homes (about 3% of the U.S., mostly Northeast) facing a 21% spending increase.

{{< faq "Is this forecast reliable?" >}}
It's the government's center-of-distribution estimate, not a promise: EIA publishes warmer and colder side cases and updates the outlook monthly. The forecast assumes a strong El Niño pattern with a warmer East and much colder West — weather deviations from that script move the regional numbers substantially. Use the tables for budgeting direction, not month-by-month precision.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Which month has the highest heating bill?" >}}
January, for every fuel: EIA's forecast monthly averages peak there at $146 (gas), $270 (electric heat), $302 (propane), and $509 (heating oil). If you budget monthly, January needs roughly a third more than the shoulder months.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "When does EIA update this forecast?" >}}
Monthly, alongside each Short-Term Energy Outlook release through the heating season — the October edition is the anchor for the season, and November through February editions revise the price and weather assumptions as the winter actually unfolds. If a January cold wave breaks the forecast, the January or February update is where the revision appears; this page's tables reflect the October 6, 2026 edition.
