## Quick Answer

**Heating one room is cheaper than heating the whole house only when you actually turn the central heat down for the rest of it. A space heater running on top of your normal thermostat setting always costs more — full stop.** And even with an honest setback, whether you come out ahead depends on what your central system is: if you heat with electricity (baseboard, electric furnace), zone heating wins big and immediately; if you heat with a modern gas furnace, the math usually says the furnace wins; if you heat with a heat pump, the space heater almost never wins unless it's preventing auxiliary heat strips from running.

The one number to hold onto: a 1,500W space heater at the July 2026 national average rate costs **about $0.27 per hour to run — roughly $66 a month at 8 hours a day.** Whether that $66 is a bargain or a leak depends entirely on what it replaces.

## The Per-Hour Math That Hooks People

Every space heater sold in the United States tops out at 1,500 watts — it's a UL plug-cap convention, not a coincidence. The arithmetic people do at the store:

- 1,500W × 1 hour = 1.5 kWh
- 1.5 kWh × $0.183/kWh = **$0.27 per hour**
- "Twenty-seven cents an hour? That's nothing."

Eight hours a evening, thirty days: 360 kWh, **$65.92 a month.** For one room. That's the number that shows up on the bill in February as a mystery [winter electric spike](/blog/03-why-is-my-electric-bill-so-high/), and it's why the [space heater running cost breakdown](/blog/space-heater-running-cost/) deserves its own article. The per-hour framing is technically true and practically misleading — the same way "just $4 a day" is a $120-a-month habit.

## The Per-BTU Truth: What a Dollar of Heat Costs

A space heater is a toaster with a fan: every watt becomes heat, no more, no less. That makes its cost per unit of heat fixed and knowable. Central systems vary — a gas furnace burns fuel, a heat pump moves heat instead of making it. Here's what one million BTU (MMBTU) of delivered heat costs from each source, at the national-average rates in the header and typical equipment efficiencies:

| Heat source | Efficiency | $ per MMBTU delivered | vs. 1,500W space heater |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Space heater (any 1,500W unit)** | 100% (resistance) | **$53.70** | — |
| **Electric baseboard / electric furnace** | 100% (resistance) | $53.70 | identical |
| **Gas furnace, 80% AFUE** | 80% | $24.00 | heater is 2.2× pricier |
| **Gas furnace, 95% AFUE** | 95% | $20.21 | heater is 2.7× pricier |
| **Heat pump, COP 2.5 (cold day)** | 250% | $21.48 | heater is 2.5× pricier |
| **Heat pump, COP 3.5 (mild day)** | 350% | $15.34 | heater is 3.5× pricier |

The conversion chain, so you can check the math: 1 kWh = 3,412 BTU, so 1 MMBTU takes 293 kWh × $0.183 = $53.70. Gas: 1 therm = 100,000 BTU at $1.92, delivered at 95% AFUE means 10.53 therms per MMBTU = $20.21. Heat pump: resistance cost divided by COP.

Two honest conclusions fall straight out of this table:

1. **Against gas or heat-pump central heat, the space heater is 2.2-3.5× more expensive per unit of heat.** Zone heating with it only wins if you're cancelling MORE than that multiple in central runtime.
2. **Against electric-resistance central heat, the space heater is exactly the same price per unit of heat.** Every BTU you move from whole-house baseboards to a one-room heater is then heated at the same rate — so heating one room instead of five is a straight proportional win.

## When Zone Heating Wins

**Case 1 — You heat with electricity (baseboard, electric furnace, wall heaters).** This is zone heating's home turf. Your central and the plug-in heater cost the same per BTU, so the win comes purely from heating fewer cubic feet: one living room in the evening instead of the whole house is commonly a 40-60% cut in heating energy for those hours. Set the central thermostats to 58°F, close the doors on unoccupied rooms, and run the heater where you sit. If your winter electric heating runs $200/month, an honest evening zone routine can take $50-80 off that.

**Case 2 — Your schedule heats an empty house.** If the evening routine is one person, one room, and the alternative is whole-house comfort, the comparison isn't "heater vs. furnace" — it's "one room of resistance heat vs. the whole house's worth of furnace runtime." On an 80%-AFUE furnace in a cold climate, that can still win; on a 95% furnace it usually doesn't (see the worked example below).

**Case 3 — The heater prevents heat-pump auxiliary strips.** If your heat pump's recovery or the bedroom's cold-morning complaint triggers the electric resistance aux strips, a strategically placed heater can prevent a $53.70/MMBTU aux blast — but note this is the same price per BTU as the space heater itself, so the honest framing is: aux heat and space heater cost the same; the win is only against the aux SYSTEM overshooting the whole house. Read [aux heat vs. emergency heat](/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/) — if "AUX" is showing on your thermostat all winter, that's the bill problem, not the room temperature.

## When It Backfires

**Running the heater on top of the furnace.** Thermostat stays at 70°F, heater adds warmth to the den: you've added $66/month and saved nothing. This is the default way people actually use space heaters, and it's why [heating bills spike](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/) in January.

**Shallow setbacks.** The furnace room wins by 2.7× per BTU; if dropping the thermostat from 68°F to 64°F only cuts central runtime 15%, the heater has to more than make up a 2.7× handicap — the arithmetic can't get there.

**Open floor plans.** Zone heating works because walls and doors contain the heat. In a great-room house, "one room" is often 40% of the home's volume — the heater is heating a zone nearly as big as the furnace would.

**The drafty-room trap.** A heater in the draftiest room fights the [envelope leaks](/blog/how-to-find-and-seal-drafts-in-your-home/) instead of the thermostat. Sealing the drafts is a one-time fix that pays every hour of every winter; the heater pays by the hour forever.

## The Worked Example (Gas Furnace Home)

A 95%-AFUE gas furnace home, $150/month average winter heating bill, one person evenings 6pm-10pm:

- **Zone attempt:** thermostat 68→62°F for four evening hours (a shallow setback), space heater in the den. The setback might cut central gas use ~10% for those hours — maybe $6-8/month saved. The heater: 4h × 30d × $0.27 = **$32.40/month added.** Net: **~$25/month loss.**
- **Deeper version:** whole house to 58°F all evening (7pm-11pm), heater where the person is. Central drops maybe 20-25% for the month: ~$30-37 saved, heater costs $32.40. **Roughly a wash.**
- **The actual winner in this home:** skip the heater entirely and run the [thermostat schedule](/blog/what-temperature-thermostat-winter/) — the same setback without the resistance load saves the full $30-37.

The pattern generalizes: **on gas heat, the thermostat alone beats the heater-plus-thermostat combo.** On electric-resistance heat, the combo wins. On a heat pump, the thermostat (or a [smart thermostat](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-winter-savings/) that manages recovery) beats both.

## If You're Buying a Heater Anyway

Some rooms genuinely need supplemental heat — the office above the garage, the three-season porch — and the [honest buying criteria](/blog/42-best-energy-efficient-space-heater/) are safety features, not efficiency slogans: tip-over switch, overheat shutoff, thermostat control, and a listed (UL/ETL) product. Every 1,500W heater converts watts to heat at exactly the same 100% — "efficient" in marketing means the safety electronics and the thermostat behave, nothing more. ## The Decision Rule, Compressed

1. **Electric central heat?** Zone heat the occupied room, set back the rest. Clear win.
2. **Gas furnace?** Skip the heater; run a setback schedule. The thermostat alone wins.
3. **Heat pump?** Never supplement with resistance heat unless it prevents aux strips — and if it does, the real fix is usually a [smarter thermostat recovery](/blog/what-temperature-thermostat-winter/).
4. **Any system:** running a heater without touching the thermostat is a $66/month subscription to warm ankles.

## The Cheapest Version of This Answer: Heat the Person

Before any room-vs-house arithmetic, the cheapest heat in the building is a layer, not a load. Rough operating costs for the same evening:

| What's warm | Typical draw | Cost for a 4-hour evening |
|---|---|---|
| Whole house (gas furnace, 95%) | — | ~$1.50-2.50 at $1.92/therm |
| One room, 1,500W heater | 1,500W | ~$1.10 |
| Heated throw or blanket | ~100-200W | ~$0.07-0.15 |
| A sweater and wool socks | 0W | $0.00 |

The full comparison — including why a heated blanket targeted at the person beats a heater targeted at the room by an order of magnitude — is its own article: [electric blanket vs. space heater cost](/blog/electric-blanket-vs-space-heater-cost/). The short version: resistance heat warming 150 pounds of human is always cheaper than resistance heat warming 1,500 cubic feet of air.

## Frequently Asked Questions

{{< faq "How much does it cost to run a space heater for a month?" >}}
At the national average of 18.3¢/kWh, a 1,500W heater costs $0.27 per hour — $2.19 for an 8-hour evening, about $66/month at 8 hours a day. A heater cycling on its thermostat runs less; a heater in a drafty room runs nearly continuous.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Is a space heater cheaper than gas central heating?" >}}
No — not per unit of heat. Resistance heat at national averages costs about $53.70 per million BTU against $20-24 for a modern gas furnace. Zone heating with a space heater only wins on gas if the central setback you pair it with cancels more furnace runtime than the heater's 2.2-2.7× per-BTU handicap.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Do space heaters use a lot of electricity compared to other appliances?" >}}
They're the biggest plug-in load in most homes: 1,500W is roughly a dishwasher and a half, or fifteen LED light fixtures, running continuously. Eight evening hours is 12 kWh — about 360 kWh a month, which at 18.3¢ is more than many households' entire monthly lighting and electronics combined.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What's the cheapest way to heat one room?" >}}
In order: heat the person (blanket, heated throw — pennies per hour), then seal the room's drafts, then a thermostat-cycled heater in a closed room. Heating the person is 10-50× cheaper per hour than heating the room; see the [electric blanket vs. space heater comparison](/blog/electric-blanket-vs-space-heater-cost/) for the numbers.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Should I close vents in unused rooms to save money?" >}}
Generally no for forced-air systems — closing too many vents raises duct pressure, makes the blower work against restriction, and can unbalance the system (the same physics as a clogged filter, which our [heating bill diagnostic](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/) covers). Closing doors and using a setback thermostat is the safer lever.
{{< /faq >}}
