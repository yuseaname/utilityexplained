## Quick Answer

**Most homes leak air in the same ten places, and the fixes are cheap: sweeps, weatherstripping, caulk, gaskets — ENERGY STAR puts sealing plus insulation at up to 10% of the total annual energy bill.** That figure is ENERGY STAR's own program estimate for the two measures together — treat it as a labeled estimate, not a bill coupon.[1] This article covers the sealing half: where the gaps are, what each size calls for, what each fix costs.

**Find before you buy.** The tour below costs an evening and one windy day and ends with a written list of the gaps your home actually has — buy from that list. The thermal camera waits for round two, if the hand and the smoke pen leave questions behind.

Your hand and a twelve-dollar smoke pen find every gap — no thermal camera required. That is also this article's boundary: when a gap hides where hands can't reach, [the DIY energy-leak checklist](/blog/47-check-energy-leaks-home-diy/) runs the instrument-based audit — a different tier of mystery, a different tool.

## The Detection Kit

**Three instruments, one of them strapped to your wrist.** The hand test runs on a windy day, indoors: back of the hand, slow sweep along door frames, window trim, outlet plates, baseboards, hatch edges. Slow beats fast — a quick palm reports its own motion, not the draft. The smoke pencil (or a stick of incense) turns a hunch into an observation: fans off, watch the smoke stream bend or jerk near a suspect edge. The night flashlight trick needs a helper and darkness — they walk a flashlight along the outside of a closed exterior door while you watch from the dark inside; any stripe of light that reaches you used the same path winter air takes. The kit prices out at $0–15: the incense is already in the pantry, and the smoke pen is the only purchase these methods can justify.[4]

## The 10-Spot Tour

**Ten stops cover the leak list federal guidance walks through — the DOE- and FTC-standard locations, dressed for a Saturday.**[2] Cold air arrives low and warm air leaves high, so the order runs basement to attic, exterior walls first. Detect as you go, and write down what you find.

{{< visual src="/images/articles/how-to-find-and-seal-drafts-in-your-home/ten-spot-house-map.webp" alt="Two-story house cross-section with ten numbered leak points from the attic hatch down to the rim joist, outlets, windows, doors, fireplace, and vents." wide="true" >}}

### 1. The attic hatch

**Detect:** Back of the hand along the hatch trim on a windy day — colder-than-the-room air means the attic above is connected. **Seal with:** An insulated hatch cover, $25–60.[4] **Renter-ok:** No — the attic belongs to the building, not the lease.

### 2. The rim joist and top plate

**Detect:** Slow palm along the seam where the foundation meets the framing, basement side; cold corners upstairs often trace back here. **Seal with:** Caulk or foam matched to the gap size — both sit on the FTC's standard fix list.[2] **Renter-ok:** Partial — open basement seams, yes; finished walls belong to the owner.

### 3. Exterior-wall outlets and switches

**Detect:** Back of the hand flat on the plate — a chill means the box behind it shares air with the wall cavity. **Seal with:** Foam gaskets behind the plates, $5–15 for a fifty-pack; breaker off, screws kept.[4] **Renter-ok:** Yes — fully reversible.

### 4. Window sashes and tracks

**Detect:** Smoke pencil at the meeting rail and both side channels, fans off — a bending stream is a finding. **Seal with:** V-strip where surfaces close against each other; rope caulk on panes you never open. $5–15.[4] **Renter-ok:** Yes, with a paint-lift caveat on older trim.

### 5. Door bottoms

**Detect:** The night trick — a helper circles the closed door outside with the flashlight; stripes of light mark your gap. **Seal with:** A sweep at $8–15, or a weighted stopper at $15–25 with zero installation.[4] **Renter-ok:** Yes.

### 6. Baseboards and floor edges

**Detect:** Smoke an inch above the baseboard along exterior walls — a flickering stream means floor-edge leakage. **Seal with:** A caulk bead at the wall-floor line, or press-in rope caulk, $8–15.[4] **Renter-ok:** Yes — the rope-caulk way.

### 7. The fireplace damper and flue

**Detect:** Palm an inch above a closed damper on a cold day — moving air with the damper shut means the seal has quit. **Seal with:** A flue plug for a chimney you never light, $12–60 — **and it comes out before any fire, every time.**[4] **Renter-ok:** No — landlord sign-off first.

### 8. The dryer vent exterior flap

**Detect:** From outside, watch the flap settle after a drying cycle — a lint-wedged flap hangs open. **Seal with:** Nothing — clear the lint and restore the closing action; terminations are on the never-seal list below. **Renter-ok:** Partial — it is an exterior wall.

### 9. Pipe, wire, and duct penetrations

**Detect:** Palm work under sinks, behind the refrigerator, along the basement ceiling — wherever plumbing, wiring, or ducts punch through. **Seal with:** Caulk for hairlines, foam for voids — match the product to the hole.[2] **Renter-ok:** Partial — accessible seams, yes; wall interiors are maintenance.

### 10. Bathroom and kitchen exhaust fan housings

**Detect:** Fan off, a tissue held at the grille — flutter means air is shortcutting the housing. **Seal with:** A bead of caulk where the housing meets the ceiling — never at the roof or wall cap outside. **Renter-ok:** Partial — usually a maintenance ticket, not a DIY cut-in.

## The Renter's Toolkit

**Everything worth carrying into a rented home is reversible — or it is not in the kit.** Four classes do nearly the whole tour:

- **Press-in rope caulk ($8–15):** the universal joint for fixed gaps — sash edges, baseboard runs, trim joints. Thumb it in; peel it out at move-out.[4]
- **Adhesive V-strip or D-profile ($5–15):** for surfaces that close against each other. It bonds only to clean, dry surfaces — wipe before you stick.[4]
- **Weighted fabric stoppers ($15–25):** zero install, zero hardware, zero lease questions — laid against the offending door each night.[4]
- **Foam outlet gaskets (fifty-pack, $5–15):** breaker off, screws into a cup, gasket seated, everything buttoned back exactly as found.[4]

The skip list is firmer: anything permanent — expanding foam, committed caulk beads, attic-hatch covers, chimney flue plugs — waits for the owner's sign-off. Reversibility is not a style preference in a lease; it is the design brief.

## What Not to Seal (the section that keeps you safe)

**Some openings in your home are equipment, not defects.** Sealing the ones below saves pocket change and creates real hazards — the trade never comes out ahead.

- **Weep holes behind siding.** They drain rain out of the wall; block them and the wall keeps the water.
- **Combustion-air openings near gas appliances.** Furnaces and water heaters breathe through those grilles on purpose; a flame that loses its designed air supply finds air somewhere else. That intake is the starting fact of every gas bill — [why a gas bill runs high](/blog/07-gas-bill-too-high/) begins there.
- **Dryer and exhaust terminations.** Moist air and lint have to exit; a flap that will not close wants cleaning, never a cover.
- **Attic ventilation.** Soffit and ridge vents carry moisture and heat out of the attic assembly — ventilation, not drafts; they do not owe you stillness.

**Anything the appliance manual says feeds combustion air never goes on the sealing list — not with foam, not with a rag, not "just for the winter."**

## The Free 80% (before any purchase)

**Run the free list first — many "drafts" are operation, not construction.**

- **Lock every window fully.** An unlatched sash rests on its weatherstrip instead of compressing it.
- **Latch the doors you use most.** The latch pulls the door into its seals; a merely pulled-to door drafts around the latch side.
- **Close the fireplace damper** when no fire is lit. An open damper is a flue, and a flue drafts.
- **Re-seat the attic hatch.** Square in the frame, pressed down — a half-seated hatch leaks along its whole trim.
- **Repair a torn sweep before replacing it.** A tightening and a straightening beat a shopping trip.

None of it costs a dollar, and it retires a fair share of "drafty house" verdicts on its own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

{{< faq "Do door draft stoppers actually work?" >}}

Yes — narrowly, and at the exact job they are built for. A weighted stopper blocks the strip of cold air sliding under a closed door, which is the draft you feel at ankle height, and it does so with zero installation. What it cannot do: seal the gap itself (that is the sweep's job), stay put when the door swings, or reach drafts born at windows, outlets, or the attic hatch. A bridge, not a repair — fine as one, disappointing as the other.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How do I find drafts in an apartment I can't modify?" >}}

The same three instruments travel: hand, smoke pencil, night flashlight. So does most of the fix list — press-in rope caulk, adhesive weatherstripping, weighted stoppers, and foam gaskets are all removable, which is the renter's line between fixing and modifying. Run the free list first; latches and dampers misbehave in apartments exactly as they do anywhere. The attic-hatch and flue classes wait for the conversation with the owner.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Is air sealing worth it before winter?" >}}

The timing logic holds: the detection evening is weather-agnostic, but a sealed gap earns its keep all heating season — and again in summer against cooled air. Weigh it with the labels attached: ENERGY STAR's up-to-10% is a program estimate for sealing plus insulation, not a bill coupon.[1] Sequence it — free list tonight, ten-spot tour on the next windy day, shopping from the written list, and the 25C credit page before you file.[3]
