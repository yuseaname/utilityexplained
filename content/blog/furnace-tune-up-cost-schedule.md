## Quick Answer

**A professional furnace tune-up typically runs about $80 to $200, with most homeowners paying in the $100-$200 range** [1]. You generally want it once a year, ideally in the fall before heating season starts, so problems get caught before you need the heat [3]. The visit covers checking combustion, cleaning or flagging filters, testing safety controls, and looking for wear — but it is not repair work. If the technician finds broken parts, your bill will be separate from the tune-up price.

## What a Furnace Tune-Up Actually Includes

A tune-up is preventive maintenance, not a fix-it service. A good technician spends 45-90 minutes looking at the parts that wear out or become dangerous. Here's what a typical visit includes, based on contractor checklists and ENERGY STAR's maintenance guidance [3]:

| Check | What the tech does |
|---|---|
| Combustion check | Tests gas (or oil) pressure and burner operation; looks for improper combustion that can waste fuel or create carbon monoxide [3] |
| Heat exchanger | Inspects for cracks or corrosion — a cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide, so this is the safety-critical part [3] |
| Filter | Checks, cleans, or replaces the filter; a dirty filter raises energy costs and can damage equipment [3] |
| Blower | Inspects the blower motor and fan, cleans if needed, checks airflow |
| Flame sensor | Cleans or tests the flame sensor, which keeps the burner running safely |
| Venting | Checks the flue/exhaust pipe for blockages or leaks, so combustion gases leave the house properly [3] |
| Safety controls | Confirms limit switches, thermostats, and ignition systems start and shut down correctly [3] |

Ask for the checklist when you call. Most reputable companies will tell you exactly what their tune-up includes before you book, and honest ones will show you what they found when they're done [1].

## Gas vs. Oil vs. Electric: Cost Differences

What you pay depends partly on what kind of furnace you have. Gas and oil units burn fuel, so they need combustion checks; electric units are simpler, with no burner, no flame, no venting to check — so their tune-ups tend to be cheaper [1][2].

| Furnace type | Typical tune-up cost | Why the difference |
|---|---|---|
| Gas | $80 - $170 | More moving parts: burner, ignitor, gas valve, heat exchanger [1][2] |
| Oil | $100 - $200 | Also needs fuel-line, tank, and nozzle checks; oil soot cleanup takes time [1][2] |
| Electric | $70 - $130 | Fewer combustion parts; tech mainly checks heating elements, blower, and controls [1][2] |

Understanding your equipment starts with knowing how efficient it is — see our [AFUE vs SEER vs HSPF vs COP](/blog/afue-vs-seer-vs-hspf-vs-cop/) guide. The single biggest reason the ranges vary, though, is where you live. Labor rates differ by region, and HVAC companies charge $75-$125 an hour labor on average [1]. Rural areas often run cheaper than big metro zones [2].

## Maintenance vs. Repair: Knowing the Difference

The tune-up price covers the check and the routine cleaning — not replacement parts. If your technician finds something broken, that's a repair, booked separately, and repairs typically cost $100 to $600 depending on the part [1].

That distinction matters because it changes what you're agreeing to. A tune-up at $150 should not quietly become a $450 repair bill without you approving the work first. A fair process: the tech finishes the inspection, shows you what's wrong, gives you a repair estimate, and waits for your yes. If a company won't do that, find another one.

A tune-up is also not a replacement recommendation. Some companies use an annual visit to pitch a whole new furnace. Unless your unit is very old, incredibly inefficient, or failing safety tests, a $150 tune-up should keep it going rather than pushing you into a $5,000+ replacement [1].

## Is a Maintenance Plan Worth It?

Many HVAC companies sell annual maintenance plans for roughly $150 to $500 a year, which works out to around $10-$30 a month [1]. In exchange, you typically get the tune-up, the inspection, a filter replacement, and sometimes priority scheduling or a small discount on repairs [2].

Are they worth it? It depends:

- **Worth it if your system is older or you know you'll skip maintenance otherwise.** A plan forces you to do the once-a-year visit, and one caught problem usually pays for years of plans.
- **Worth it if a single tune-up costs $150+ in your area anyway.** If the a-la-carte tune-up costs $150-$200, a plan at $150-$200 a year doesn't cost more, and it usually bundles extras like the filter.
- **Skippable on a new furnace with a long warranty.** Many new furnaces come with 10-year parts warranties that don't require annual service, though a warranty does not cover every problem.
- **Read the fine print first.** Some plans cover only one visit a year, while others promise two (often furnace in fall, AC in spring). Some include parts, some just a discount rather than full coverage [2].

And never sign up for a ten-year contract you can't cancel. A monthly $10-$30 charge adds up fast if you never use it [1].

## One Caution: Watch for the Upsell

The most common furnace scam is the **heat exchanger crack claim**: the tech points at a hairline mark, declares the exchanger cracked, and quotes you several thousand dollars to replace the whole furnace, because a cracked exchanger can leak carbon monoxide.

Legitimately, cracked heat exchangers are a serious safety problem — ENERGY STAR warns that a cracked heat exchanger or dirty burner causes improper burner operation, which can create carbon monoxide hazards [3]. But that's exactly why you should ask to see it yourself. A real crack is usually visible, and a reputable tech will show you with a camera or flashlight. Before approving expensive work:

- Ask the tech to **show you the crack** — an honest pro can point exactly at it.
- If they can't or won't, get a second opinion before spending thousands.
- Ask whether they did a **carbon monoxide test** around the unit; that result matters more than a blurry photo.

Same logic applies to any big-ticket claim during a routine visit: filters, duct cleaning, and "urgent" part replacements can wait for a second quote. Walk out of any company that pressures you to decide that day [1].

## When to Schedule — and a Few Ways to Save

Schedule your furnace tune-up **in the fall, before heating season starts** — ideally September or October. ENERGY STAR specifically recommends having a contractor do annual pre-season check-ups, because contractors book up once cold weather hits [3]. Calling in August or early September gets you the best timeslots and usually the same price.

Ways to keep costs sane:

- **Replace your filter yourself** between visits. A basic filter costs $10-$30, and a dirty filter raises energy costs and can damage equipment — so this one task does a lot of work for you [1][3].
- **Bundle with your AC check.** If you have central air, many companies discount a combined heating-and-cooling plan versus two separate annual visits [2].
- **Ask for the price on the phone.** If they can't give you a range over the phone, they're likely to upsell you in person [1].

If the tune-up finds nothing wrong, consider it money well spent: you now know your furnace will likely start all winter. If it finds something, you found it in September instead of January, when emergency repair rates apply [1].

Even with an efficient system working perfectly, the context helps: the average U.S. home uses about 899 kWh of electricity a month [4], and U.S. residential electricity averaged about 18.34 cents per kWh as of mid-2026 [5] — so heat is one of the biggest levers on your bill, which makes a cheap yearly check a reasonable insurance policy. Natural gas prices have also stayed relatively low through 2026, with wholesale spot prices around $1.86 per million Btu near mid-2026 [6] — but a furnace that runs inefficiently because it's never been serviced burns more of whatever fuel you use, whatever the price.
