## Quick Answer

**Standing water in a water meter box is common and usually harmless — rain and irrigation runoff account for most wet meter boxes, and the water normally drains or seeps into the soil within a day or two of a storm.** [1] The 2-minute check that separates weather from a leak: dry spells and sprinkler schedules aside, look at the meter's **low-flow indicator** (the small spinning dial). Spinning with everything in your house shut off points to a leak on *your* side of the meter. Not spinning, but the box keeps refilling or the water stays consistently high for days — that points to the *utility's* side, and that call is theirs to answer, at their cost. [1]

This page is the decision tree: rain vs. refill, the indicator test, and who to call tonight. For the deeper diagnostics this tree routes into — [why the meter runs when no water is used](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/) and [how to read the leak indicator](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/) — follow those next; for who pays for which side of the pipe, the [before-vs-after-the-meter responsibility guide](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/) has the boundary map.

## The 2-minute diagnostic: rain, refill, or leak

{{< visual src="/images/articles/water-meter-box-full-of-water/decision-tree.webp" alt="Decision tree diagram for water in a meter box: did it rain or are sprinklers running leads to wait-and-watch, continuous refill with a still meter leads to calling the utility, and a spinning indicator with all fixtures off leads to a private-side leak." wide="true" >}}

Work top to bottom; stop at the first yes.

1. **Did it rain in the last 1–3 days, or are sprinklers running near the box?** Most standing water in meter boxes is rain runoff entering through the lid, or irrigation overspray pooling in a box that sits in a low spot. Mark the water level (a stone on the meter cap, or photograph it) and re-check tomorrow — rain water drains or seeps away on its own. [1][2]
2. **Is the water level rising, or staying flat?** Flat or falling after weather = wait it out. Rising on a dry day with the sprinklers off = go to step 3.
3. **Shut off every fixture in the house and watch the meter's low-flow indicator.** Spinning or ticking with nothing running = water is moving through the meter — a leak on **your** side, somewhere between the meter and your faucets. Still as stone while the box refills = water is arriving **before** the meter — the utility's side. [1]

The indicator is the instrument that settles it; [how to find and read it](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/) takes two minutes if you've never looked. And if you want the isolation version of the test — house valve closed vs. open, which narrows *your-side* leaks to the yard run vs. the house — that's the [meter-still-moving walkthrough](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/).

## Why meter boxes collect water even without a leak

{{< visual src="/images/articles/water-meter-box-full-of-water/why-boxes-fill.webp" alt="Cutaway illustration of a meter box in soil showing three water-entry paths: rain through the lid, irrigation overspray from a nearby sprinkler head, and groundwater pushing up through saturated soil." wide="true" >}}

A meter box is a buried hollow with a loose-fitting lid — essentially a low spot by design. Three weather-and-landscape causes fill it with no leak anywhere:

- **Rain runoff.** The most common cause; boxes in low-lying yards or at the bottom of a slope collect surface water that seeps through the lid joint. It drains or seeps into the soil after the storm passes. [1]
- **Irrigation overspray.** A sprinkler head aimed anywhere near the box will top it up on every cycle. If your box is wet only on watering days, that's the answer — adjust the timer or the head, and the problem is gone. [1]
- **Groundwater and soil type.** In heavy clay or a high water table, the box acts as a drain sump: saturated ground pushes water in, and slow soils take days to pull it back. Long dry spells are the only thing that empties some boxes — drainage is entirely dependent on the soil. [2]

None of these three need a phone call. The diagnostic above exists to prove the absence of a leak so you can file the water under weather and move on.

## The two leak cases — and who owns each

The indicator test sorts a real leak into one of two ownership buckets, and the buckets have different phone numbers:

| Indicator (all fixtures off) | Water in box | What it means | Who you call |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spinning / flowing | Any | Leak on the **customer side** — the pipe from the meter to and inside your house | Plumber (your cost); document everything for a [leak-adjustment claim](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/) |
| Still / stopped | Refilling or staying high on a dry day | Leak on the **utility side** — the main, the connection, or the meter fittings themselves | The water utility's emergency line (their cost) |

[1]

Two practical notes before you dial:

- **The meter and its fittings belong to the utility.** Water pooling specifically around the meter connections — or a box that won't stop filling with the indicator still — is nearly always a utility work order, not a plumbing bill. Don't excavate, don't tighten anything on the meter; tampering with it is prohibited nearly everywhere. The ownership boundary, and what to do when the utility says "not ours," is covered in the [responsibility guide](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/).
- **Photograph the box and the meter before and after.** If this turns out to be a bill event, the timestamped photos are the case file for both the boundary determination and the [leak-adjustment request](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/). [1]

## "Who do I call tonight?" — the urgency ladder

{{< visual src="/images/articles/water-meter-box-full-of-water/urgency-ladder.webp" alt="Three-rung urgency ladder for a wet meter box: monitor at home after rain, call the utility business line for a still-meter refill, and call the utility emergency line for water surfacing or flowing at the box." wide="true" >}}

Most wet boxes are a tomorrow problem. The exceptions, in escalating order:

1. **Monitor at home** — water after rain or on watering days, indicator still. No call; re-check in 24–48 hours.
2. **Utility business-hours line** — dry weather, box keeps refilling, indicator still. Ask for a "meter box leak investigation" and reference the still indicator; utilities handle district-side seepage as routine maintenance. [1]
3. **Utility 24-hour emergency line, tonight** — water *surfacing* around the box (flowing, bubbling, or eroding soil), a frost-split box in freezing weather, or any suspicion the leak is before the meter and growing. Utility-side leaks waste treated water continuously; utilities want these calls at 2 AM. [1]

If the surfacing water is on your side of the meter — indicator spinning, and the wet area is between box and house — the emergency is yours: shut the main valve at the box if it turns, and start the plumber calls.

## The freeze-season version of this problem

Winter is also when the pipes *leading to* the box are most at risk — if you're preparing for a hard-freeze forecast, the [keep-pipes-from-freezing ladder](/blog/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/) covers the whole house side, from dripping the right faucet to heat cable on the chronic problem run.

Box flooding clusters twice a year: spring rains, and freeze-thaw cycles. In cold-winter areas, standing water in a meter box is a freeze risk to the meter and the service-line fitting — water that freezes in the box can split fittings and the box itself, turning weather into a genuine leak. If your box stands wet through a hard-freeze forecast, that upgrades step 2 above to a same-week call: many utilities will pump or insulate a flood-prone box on request. A broken lid — the other freeze casualty — is also utility property to replace; don't improvise a cover that seals moisture in. [2]

The cheap long-term upgrade for box-and-yard-run worries is a strap-on meter monitor: it reads the meter continuously and flags slow flow weeks before any box tells you — [point sensors vs. whole-home monitors](/blog/point-sensor-vs-whole-home-water-leak-when-to-upgrade/) compares the options.

{{< faq "Is it normal to have water in my water meter box?" >}}

Often, yes. Rain runoff and irrigation overspray fill meter boxes routinely, and utilities themselves publish that standing water is "not unusual" — it normally drains or seeps into the soil after a storm. What is not normal is a box that keeps refilling on dry days with the sprinklers off, or water surfacing and flowing around the box. The low-flow indicator separates the cases in two minutes. [1]

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How do I know if the water in my meter box is a leak?" >}}

Shut off everything in the house and watch the meter's low-flow indicator. Spinning with no water in use means flow through the meter — a leak on your side of it. A still indicator with the box still filling means the water is arriving before the meter — the utility's side. Rain and irrigation cause everything else, and drain away on their own. [1]

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Who do I call about a flooded meter box?" >}}

If the meter's indicator is still and the box refills without rain: the water utility — the box, lid, meter, and fittings are theirs, and district-side seepage is their maintenance item. If the indicator is spinning: a plumber, because the leak is in your pipe. For water surfacing or flowing at the box, call the utility's 24-hour emergency line the same day, either way. [1]

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Will a flooded meter box freeze and damage the meter?" >}}

It can, in hard-freeze climates — standing water in the box can split the meter fittings or the box during a freeze-thaw cycle. If your box stands wet ahead of a hard freeze, call the utility's business line that week and ask them to pump or insulate it; many do this on request. A cracked lid is also theirs to replace. [2]

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I pump the water out of my meter box myself?" >}}

You can bail or pump clear standing water to see the meter — inspectors and homeowners do this routinely for readings. What you should not do is repair, tighten, or seal anything on the meter or its fittings, or improvise a gasketed lid: the assembly belongs to the utility, and sealing moisture in accelerates the freeze damage you're trying to avoid. [1][2]

{{< /faq >}}

## Most Wet Boxes Are Weather. Prove It, Then Act.

The indicator dial in your meter box is the cheapest diagnostic instrument you own: two minutes with every fixture off sorts rain from your-side leak from utility-side leak, and each of those three answers has a different — and cheap — next step. Photograph the box, run the test, and file the water under weather unless the dial says otherwise.

**Where to go next:** run the 2-minute diagnostic the next time the box is wet; if the indicator spins, [isolate the leak](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/) the same day; if the box refills with a still meter, call the utility with that exact observation — "still indicator, box refilling, no rain" — and let the boundary guide handle anything they dispute.
