## Quick Answer

**Yes — but only if you actually program it.** The U.S. Department of Energy estimates you save about **1% on heating for every degree-F of setback you hold for 8 hours**. An 8°F overnight setback, held every night, typically trims **5–10% off heating** — roughly **$50–100 a year** at average U.S. rates. The device itself saves nothing; the schedule does.

## What the evidence actually shows

Three numbers anchor every honest answer to this question:

- **The DOE rule of thumb:** roughly 1% of heating (or cooling) costs per degree of setback, sustained for about 8 hours a day. Set back 10°F from 10 PM to 6 AM and the math points to ~10% savings on the heating portion of your bill.
- **ENERGY STAR's recommended schedule:** an 8°F setback for 8 hours in winter, a 7°F setup in summer — and it certifies smart thermostats it estimates save about **8% of heating and cooling costs** (around $50 a year for a typical home).
- **Independent field studies of learning thermostats** (Nest, ecobee) have measured **10–12% savings on heating and about 15% on cooling**, averaged across thousands of real homes.

Stack those together and a realistic expectation is **5–12% off your heating bill** — meaningful on a $1,000–1,500 winter heating season (EIA averages $950–1,100 for gas-heated homes, more for electric resistance and heat-pump homes), but not the "cut your bill in half" some ads imply.

## The catch: most people never program them

Here is the uncomfortable part. Savings come from the **schedule**, not the plastic on the wall. Surveys by energy researchers (including work summarized by ACEEE and DOE field studies) have repeatedly found that **roughly a third to half of programmable-thermostat owners never set a schedule** — they use it as a manual dial, which saves nothing over a plain thermostat.

A programmable thermostat cuts your heating bill only when all three of these are true:

1. **Your schedule is genuinely regular.** You're out of the house or asleep at predictable times on most days.
2. **The setback is aggressive enough to matter.** A 2°F dip saves ~2%; ENERGY STAR's 7–8°F is where real money shows up.
3. **You stop fighting it.** Every manual override erases a slice of the schedule's benefit. If household members constantly bump the temperature, the program might as well not exist.

If those conditions don't describe your household, a **learning thermostat** that builds its own schedule (and adjusts when it detects nobody home) is the design that solves the human problem — that's what the field studies above actually measured.

## The recovery myth: "reheating the house wastes what you saved"

The most common reason people skip setbacks is the belief that reheating a cold house in the morning burns more fuel than maintaining a steady temperature. **It doesn't.** Heat loss from your house scales with the indoor–outdoor temperature difference. When the house sits colder overnight, it loses heat more *slowly*, and the morning recovery is a short, one-time burst — not a penalty. The physics was settled decades ago and DOE's 1%-per-degree guidance already accounts for it.

Two genuine exceptions (below) involve equipment that responds badly to deep, fast recoveries — not the physics of your house.

## Realistic savings, by household

| Household situation | Realistic annual savings | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Regular 9-to-5 schedule, 8°F night setback | **$60–110/yr** (5–10% of heating) | The textbook case the DOE math describes |
| Same schedule + proper daytime away setback | **$90–150/yr** | Adds the 8-hour workday setback |
| Irregular schedule, learning thermostat | **$50–140/yr** (8–12% of heating+cooling) | What independent smart-thermostat studies measured |
| Schedule set once, then overridden daily | **~$0–20/yr** | The device is now a manual dial |
| Already keeping the house at 60°F around the clock | **~$0** | You're already past what setbacks offer |

Figures assume U.S. average heating costs; scale up if you heat with electric resistance or live in a cold climate, down if you live in a mild one.

## Two systems where deep setbacks backfire

- **Heat pumps with electric auxiliary (strip) heat.** A big morning recovery can trigger the expensive resistance backup, erasing days of savings. Use small setbacks (2–4°F), or a thermostat with adaptive recovery that brings the house back slowly on the compressor alone. (If you have a heat pump, see our [heat pump running guide](/blog/heat-pump-running-all-day-in-winter/).)
- **Radiant floor and other slow-response systems.** Concrete mass holds heat for hours; aggressive schedules overshoot and undershoot. These systems often do best with gentle 2–3°F adjustments.

For conventional furnaces and boilers — the majority of U.S. homes — no such caveat applies.

## A schedule that actually saves money

This is the ENERGY STAR-shaped template, adjusted for an 8-hour workday:

| Period | Winter | Summer |
|---|---|---|
| Wake (6–8 AM) | 68°F | 76°F |
| Day, nobody home (8 AM–5 PM) | 62–64°F | 80–82°F |
| Evening (5–10 PM) | 68°F | 75°F |
| Sleep (10 PM–6 AM) | 60–62°F | 78–80°F |

Copy that into the thermostat once, then leave it alone for a winter. That discipline — not the hardware — is what shows up on the bill. If you want the full picture of where your heating dollars go, start with [Heating & Cooling explained](/heating-cooling-explained/).

## Programmable vs. smart: which one to buy

- **Programmable ($25–60):** the right choice if your week is predictable and you'll genuinely set it once. Payback is typically one heating season.
- **Smart/learning ($130–250):** worth it if schedules vary, if you want remote control, or if you know you'll never program anything. The measured 8–12% savings shorten payback to 2–3 heating seasons, and utility rebates (often $50–100) cut the upfront cost further.

Either way, check your utility's rebate page before buying — most major utilities run thermostat rebates year-round.

## The honest bottom line

A programmed setback is one of the few home changes that costs almost nothing, works while you sleep, and returns 5–10% of your heating bill every winter you keep it. The failure mode isn't the equipment — it's the setup step nobody does. Budget twenty minutes with the manual tonight, or buy the kind that programs itself.
