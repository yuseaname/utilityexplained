## Quick Answer

**A gas bill must be interpreted from the account's meter information, billing period, usage unit, rate schedule, and itemized charges—not from a generic national price, therm conversion, or seasonal bill table.** DOE materials describe regulated and deregulated gas-market structures, but the controlling terms for a household are the serving utility's current tariff and bill.[1]

Keep the statement and tariff together when reviewing a charge or comparing periods.

## Start With the Account Details

Record these items before comparing a bill:

1. Service address, account class, and rate-schedule name.
2. Billing-period start and end dates and total billed days.
3. Current and prior meter readings, read status, and unit shown.
4. Total billed usage and any conversion factor shown by the utility.
5. Each itemized charge, credit, tax, rider, or one-time item.

Utilities can bill gas use in different units and may apply account-specific conversion or rate rules. Use the unit and calculation shown in the provider's own materials rather than assuming CCF, MCF, and therms are interchangeable in every circumstance.

## Line-Item Walkthrough: What Each Charge on Your Gas Bill Means

A typical regulated gas bill includes several distinct charge categories. The table below summarizes what each line covers and how it is calculated. Your utility’s exact labels and amounts will differ — check your actual statement.

| Charge | What It Pays For | Typical Calculation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Customer charge** | Billing, meter reading, account maintenance | Flat monthly fee (often $7–$15) | Charged every month regardless of usage; covers fixed administrative costs.[3] |
| **Supply charge (commodity charge)** | The natural gas itself | Price per therm × therms used | In deregulated markets, you may choose a supplier; the utility still delivers the gas.[1] |
| **Delivery charge (distribution charge)** | Pipelines, mains, and local infrastructure that bring gas to your home | Flat fee + per-therm charge, or per-therm only (typically $0.10–$0.50/therm) | You pay this to the local utility even with a third-party gas supplier; covers maintenance and pipeline operation.[3] |
| **Riders/surcharges** | System upgrades, environmental programs, pipeline safety, securitization | Dollar or percentage add-on to usage or fixed charges | Named items like "Infrastructure Adjustment," "Energy Efficiency Rider," or "PCA" (Purchased Gas Adjustment). These fluctuate by tariff.[3] |
| **Taxes and fees** | State and local taxes, gross receipts tax, franchise fees | Percentage of charges or fixed amount | Tax line items vary by location; the utility passes through government-imposed charges.[1] |

## Therm/CCF Conversion: A Worked Example

Gas is measured by volume (cubic feet) but billed by energy content (therms). If your meter reads in CCF (hundred cubic feet) and your bill charges by therm, the utility applies a conversion factor.

**The conversion:** 1 CCF of natural gas contains roughly 1.027 therms of energy, on average. The exact factor varies by gas quality and is shown on your bill.

**Worked example:**

1. Meter reading shows you used **60 CCF** this month.
2. Your bill states a conversion factor of **1.027 therms/CCF**.
3. Billed therms = 60 CCF × 1.027 = **61.6 therms**.
4. If your supply rate is $1.40/therm, the supply charge = 61.6 × $1.40 = **$86.24**.

If your utility bills directly in therms (from an energy-reading meter), no conversion is needed — the meter already reads in therms.

## Seasonal Usage Context

Natural gas consumption is highly seasonal because space heating is the dominant load. According to EIA RECS data, space heating accounts for 60–80% of annual household gas use in cold climates, with water heating adding 15–25% and cooking/drying taking the rest.[4]

In a typical cold-climate household, winter monthly usage can be **80–150 therms**, while summer usage falls to **10–30 therms** (water heating and cooking only). That means a January bill may be 5–10 times higher than a July bill for the same home.

## Compare Like With Like

When a total changes, compare a similar billing period and record:

- the number of billing days;
- billed gas use and read status;
- weather and occupancy changes;
- rate-schedule or tariff changes; and
- credits, past-due amounts, or one-time items.

The total amount due alone cannot identify whether a change came from usage, a rate, or a non-usage charge.

## When to Contact the Utility

Contact the provider if a meter reading, usage unit, rate schedule, or line item does not match the statement or current tariff. Ask for the account-specific explanation, the tariff version, and the provider's review or dispute process. Keep copies of the bill and your contact record.
