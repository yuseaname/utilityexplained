## The quick answer

A water service charge is the fixed monthly fee on your bill whether you used almost no water or a lot - it does not change with the gallons on the meter, and utilities set it by meter size, not by use. [3][4] It exists because the costs of keeping you connected - the meter, the bill, the mains held ready - do not shrink when you conserve. What you can lower is the usage charge, the per-unit part, billed in units like CCF; one CCF equals 748 gallons. [1]

## The problem: a charge even when you used almost no water

You did not water the lawn. You were out of town. The usage on your bill looks tiny. And yet there it is: a water service charge, base charge, customer charge, or meter charge.

It feels like you are paying for nothing. You are not paying for nothing, but you also should not have to guess what the fee means. This guide explains what the fixed fee is, what it pays for, and how to sanity-check it. For the big picture of how water bills are built, start with our pillar, Water Service Explained.

## What a water service charge is (and what it is not)

The fixed fee goes by different names: service charge, base charge, customer charge, meter charge, and sometimes minimum charge. The key idea is not the label. The key idea is what it does: it is not a measurement of water you used. Your usage is measured separately, usually in gallons or CCF - one CCF equals 748 gallons. [1] If you keep seeing CCF on your bill, see what CCF means on a water bill.

## What the fixed fee usually covers

Think of the fixed fee as the cost of having an active account and a working connection. Even if your usage is low, the system still exists for you. Cleveland Water describes its two components plainly: a fixed charge, based on the size of your water meter, and a consumption charge, based on how much water you use - with rate revenue funding treatment plants, water main replacement, and smart meters. [4]

What the fixed side is tied to, and what it helps cover, commonly includes: meter costs (the meter itself, testing, replacement) [4]; billing and customer service (statements, payment processing); operations and maintenance of mains, valves, and pumps [4]; and readiness costs - the system is sized and staffed so water is there when you open the tap.

Some utilities also list separate fixed fees for fire line, hydrant, or infrastructure. Those are still fixed-fee ideas, just broken into multiple lines.

## What real utility fixed charges look like (2026)

Fixed charges are set locally, and the spread is wide. Three municipal utilities, from their own current rate schedules:

- Louisville Water Company (KY) - monthly service charge of 17.82 dollars for a five-eighths or five-eighths by three-quarters meter, 38.24 dollars for 1 inch, and 122.37 dollars for 2 inches, per its 2026 tariff effective January 1, 2026. The commodity charge is tiered at 1.70 dollars per 1000 gallons for the first 2000 gallons and 4.70 dollars for the next 8000. [2]
- Philadelphia Water Department (PA) - monthly water service charge of 5.96 dollars for a five-eighths meter, 9.28 dollars for 1 inch, and 21.55 dollars for 2 inches, effective September 1, 2025. The regulation states it plainly: water charges shall consist of a service charge and quantity charge, and the type and size of the meter shall determine the service charge. [3]
- Cleveland Water (OH) - monthly fixed charge of 9.90 dollars in 2026 for the common residential five-eighths to 1 inch meter, and 19.45 dollars for 1.5 to 2 inch meters, on a schedule published year by year through 2029. [4]

Notice the pattern all three share: same city, bigger meter, bigger fixed charge - before a single gallon is counted. A larger meter can deliver more water at once, costs more to install and maintain, and its fixed charge is set higher to match. Philadelphia even has a process for customers who discover they are over-metered: apply, downsize the meter, and the lower charge applies. [3]

## Service charge vs usage charge on the bill

The service charge usually appears in a mini-itemized list. Look for a line that is the same amount each month, mentions base, service, customer, or meter, and does not change when your gallons or CCF change.

A worked example using Louisville rates [2]: a household with the standard meter uses 6,000 gallons in a month. That is 2,000 gallons at the first tier and 4,000 at the second: 3.40 plus 18.80 equals 22.20 dollars of usage charge, plus the fixed 17.82 dollars. Total water charges: 40.02 dollars. The fixed 17.82 would appear even if the usage were zero.

If sewer rides on the same statement, do not be surprised if it looks bigger than the water service charge - sewer is often the larger of the two. That is covered in how sewer charges work on your water bill. If your total jumped and you are hunting the cause, start with why your water bill suddenly increased.

## How to sanity-check the charge

### One: confirm it is fixed

Compare two bills with different usage. If the line item stays the same, it is a fixed fee.

### Two: check whether the charge scales with meter size

Many utilities tie the service charge to meter size - Louisville runs from 17.82 dollars at five-eighths to 122.37 dollars at 2 inches in the residential-to-small-commercial range alone. [2] Check which size appears on your bill and whether it matches the meter at your property.

### Three: separate water from sewer

Many water bills are two services on one statement: a water fixed charge plus a sewer base fee. Look for the headings that separate Water and Sewer before comparing months.

## What you can and cannot lower

You can usually lower usage charges by reducing gallons and CCF: fix leaks, adjust irrigation, shorten showers. Summer spikes usually come from outdoor use, so that is the first place to look.

You usually cannot lower the base or service charge without an account or meter-size change. If the fixed charge jumped year over year, check the utility current rate schedule first - adopted rate increases (Cleveland publishes its schedule year by year) explain most jumps, and a wrong meter size on the bill is a billing error worth disputing. [4]

If you suspect a hidden leak, use your meter as a truth source: see water meter leak indicator explained. If the bill climbs every summer, see why water bills are higher in summer.

## Common misconceptions

- The service charge means they estimated my usage. No. Estimated usage is about meter reads. The service charge is a fixed fee.
- If I used zero water I should pay zero. It feels fair, but utility costs split into fixed system costs plus variable usage costs - and bills are structured the same way. [3][4]
- The fixed fee is the same everywhere. It is not: 5.96 dollars a month in Philadelphia versus 17.82 dollars in Louisville for the same standard meter. [2][3]

## Next steps if it still feels wrong

1. Confirm the account status: active, vacant, or seasonal. Some cities have special rates for vacant properties.
2. Confirm the meter size. If the fixed fee is tied to meter size, make sure the size on your bill matches what is installed - Philadelphia runs a process for over-metered customers to downsize and drop to the lower charge. [3]
3. Separate water from sewer. Many water bills are really two services on one statement.

## FAQ

What is a water service charge? A fixed fee that helps cover maintaining the water system and keeping your connection and account active, even if you use very little water. [3][4]
Why do I pay a base charge every month? To cover fixed costs like meters, billing, and maintenance; usage is billed separately based on gallons or CCF. [4]
Is the service charge based on how much water I use? No. It is a fixed amount that does not change with usage; the usage charge changes based on what the meter records. [3][4]
Can I lower my water service charge? It is set by the utility for a given account type or meter size. You can usually lower the usage portion of the bill by reducing consumption and fixing leaks. [4]
