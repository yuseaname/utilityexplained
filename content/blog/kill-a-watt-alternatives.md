## Quick Answer

**The P3 Kill A Watt P4400 — the classic $25-ish plug-in usage meter — is still the default way to measure one appliance, and it is buyable again after a stock wobble.** But "Kill A Watt" is one product in a four-way toolbox: the **P4456 Kill A Watt Flex** (same idea, newer features, when in stock), an **energy-monitoring smart plug** (measures in an app, over time, from anywhere), the **Emporia Vue 3** (every circuit at once, panel install), and **your utility's hourly data** (free, whole-home only). The right pick depends on whether you want a one-weekend answer or a permanent meter — the comparison below settles it in two minutes.

## First, What the Kill A Watt Actually Is

A Kill A Watt is a pass-through meter: appliance plugs into it, it plugs into the wall, and a small LCD shows volts, amps, watts, and — the number that matters — **cumulative kWh**. Run it for a day or a week, multiply the kWh by your rate, and you have that appliance's real cost, from your actual usage, instead of a generic internet table. P3 International has made the family for over two decades,[1] which is why the name became generic — like "Crescent wrench" for an adjustable wrench.

The family tree, briefly, because the listings get confusing:

## The Four Ways to Measure One Appliance (or More)

### 1. The Kill A Watt — the weekend answer

**Best when: you want one number for one appliance, once, with zero setup.**

Strengths: instant, cheap, app-free, works in any outlet, survives decades. Limits: it answers one outlet at a time; you have to physically check the display; and it can't meter anything hardwired or 240-volt (dryers, ranges, water heaters, well pumps). If the P4400 listing you find is out of stock or the price has spiked, the P4456 Flex is the same instrument with modern conveniences:

### 2. An energy-monitoring smart plug — the permanent meter

**Best when: you want to watch a device over months, from your phone, maybe on a schedule.**

A metering plug (Kasa KP115, Tapo P110M, Emporia) is a Kill A Watt that lives in the app: real-time watts plus day/week/month kWh history, remotely, forever — and it can also switch the device off automatically, which is where savings actually happen. The trade: it needs Wi-Fi and a tap or two of setup, and it holds your appliance on a relay — for compressors (fridges, window ACs) a brief measurement is wiser than permanent duty. ### 3. The Emporia Vue 3 — every circuit at once

**Best when: the suspect list is longer than the outlet count, and you own the home.**

A panel-mounted monitor meters up to 16 circuits directly — including the 240-volt loads no plug can touch (dryer, range, water heater, AC) — with no appliance-by-appliance patrol. The costs: panel install (electrician-recommended) and circuit-level rather than device-level naming. The whole trade-off is covered in [the best home energy monitor 2026](/blog/best-home-energy-monitor-2026/); the short version: if you would buy three or more plug meters to chase a bill mystery, the Vue 3 is the same money answering the whole panel.

### 4. Your utility's hourly data — the free floor

**Best when: the question is "when is the house using power," not "which device."**

If you have a smart meter, your utility portal already graphs whole-home hourly usage for free. It can't split a chest freezer from a wine fridge — but combined with the breaker-panel method (compare hourly lines before and after flipping one breaker), it gets surprisingly far at $0. ## The Math You'll Do Exactly Once

{{< visual src="/images/articles/kill-a-watt-alternatives/weekend-audit.webp" alt="Line illustration of a power strip with three devices and one plug-in meter, arrows flowing toward a clipboard checklist." wide="true" >}}

Whatever tool you pick, the arithmetic is the same. The June 2026 national average residential price was **18.34¢/kWh**,[5] and the average household buys about **899 kWh/month**[6] — call it a $165 monthly bill at the national average. So:

- A garage refrigerator metering at 90 kWh/month costs 90 × $0.1834 ≈ **$16.50/month** — about $200/year, which is exactly the kind of number that justifies a meter and maybe retires a fridge.
- An entertainment cluster idling at 60 W around the clock is 60 W × 730 h ≈ **44 kWh/month ≈ $8/month** — worth a scheduled smart plug, not worth panic.

One measurement beats any generic table, because nameplate watts and real duty cycles differ — [how many watts does a refrigerator use](/blog/how-many-watts-does-refrigerator-use/) exists because that question has honest ranges, not one answer.

## Which One Should You Actually Buy?

- **One suspect appliance, one-time question** → Kill A Watt P4400 (or P4456 Flex if the P4400 listing is dry). ~$25–40 once.
- **Suspect you'll want to schedule or watch long-term** → metering smart plug, ideally a 2-pack.
- **Whole-house mystery, own the home, multiple suspects including 240V loads** → Vue 3 and an electrician's hour.
- **Budget zero** → utility portal + breaker-panel method.

And the standing rule for every tool on this page: **measuring is not saving.** The meter produces the number; the habit change, the scheduled plug, or the retired second fridge produces the lower bill. If no one will act on the reading, spend the $25 on nothing.
