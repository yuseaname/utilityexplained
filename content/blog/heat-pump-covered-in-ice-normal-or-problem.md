## Quick Answer

**Light frost on an outdoor heat pump in cold, humid weather is normal — the unit is an air conditioner running in reverse, and its outdoor coil runs below freezing while it harvests heat. The system knows this and runs a periodic defrost cycle (typically 5-15 minutes, steam pouring off the unit, sometimes the fan stops).** The line between normal and problem: frost that **clears** during defrost cycles is the system working; ice that **thickens over days**, forms a **solid glaze on the case and fan guard**, or comes with the unit running nonstop without warming the house means the defrost system (timer, sensor, reversing valve, or board) has failed — and that failure quietly triggers the expensive electric backup heat covered in [aux heat vs. emergency heat](/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/).

One rule above all others: **never chip, scrape, or pour hot water on a frozen heat pump** — the coil fins bend at a touch and refrigerant leaks end the season.

## Why a Heat Pump Frosts at All (The 60-Second Physics)

In heating mode, the outdoor coil is colder than the outside air — that's how it absorbs heat from it. Whenever that coil is below 32°F while the air is humid (which is most of the cooling-fog weather between 20°F and 40°F, plus any precipitation), moisture from the air freezes directly onto the coil surface. Frost is the expected byproduct of the machine doing its job; a thin, even, white coat during those conditions is normal operation.

The problem with frost is what it does next: it insulates the coil from the air, cutting the heat the pump can harvest. So every heat pump carries a defrost system. On a timer/sensor schedule (commonly a check every 30-90 minutes of runtime, defrost when the coil sensor says frost is present), the system reverses to cooling mode for 5-15 minutes — the outdoor coil runs hot, the frost melts off as visible steam, and the electric backup heat inside keeps the house from cooling during the reversal. Then it snaps back to heating. If you've ever seen your outdoor unit steaming like a kettle on a January morning with the fan stopped: that was a healthy defrost.

## The Checklist: Normal Frost vs. Failed Defrost

| Sign | Normal (defrost working) | Problem (defrost failing) |
|---|---|---|
| **Coverage** | Light, even frost on coil faces; clear or slightly frosted grille | Solid glaze encasing the case, fan guard, and refrigerant lines |
| **Pattern over days** | Frost appears and clears; unit never stays iced after cycles | Ice thickens day over day; never fully clears |
| **Fan behavior** | Fan may pause during defrost, runs otherwise | Fan blocked by ice, or blade scraping ice — shut it down at the breaker |
| **Steam events** | Regular steam-offs in humid cold weather | Never steams; frost just accumulates |
| **Indoor symptoms** | House holds temperature | House losing ground, "AUX" showing on the thermostat constantly, bills climbing |
| **Timing** | Frost worst in 25-40°F humid weather, sleet, freezing rain | Ice persists even in dry cold, or forms a uniform block |

The indoor symptom row is the expensive one. When the outdoor coil is insulated by ice, the compressor can't supply the house's heat, and the thermostat calls the electric resistance backup — heat at roughly triple the compressor's cost per BTU. A failed defrost system can add hundreds of dollars to a cold-month bill before anyone looks outside. ## What Actually Breaks (and What Each Costs to Fix)

1. **Defrost sensor or thermostat** (the coil-temperature probe that says "frost present"): the most common failure, a $15-50 part plus service labor. If the unit NEVER defrosts, this is the first suspect.
2. **Defrost control board / timer:** the schedule-keeper. A board failure shows as no defrost initiation, or defrosts that never terminate (unit stuck blowing cold air outside for an hour+).
3. **Reversing valve:** the four-way valve that flips between heat and cool modes — the mechanism defrost depends on. If the valve sticks, you'll also notice heating performance collapse, not just ice.
4. **Low refrigerant:** frost behavior changes with charge — often a frost pattern concentrated on part of the coil only, or ice where it never formed before. Refrigerant work is strictly a licensed technician's job (EPA rules), and leaks found early are cheaper than leaks found by a dead compressor.
5. **Snow burial and airflow block:** not a defrost fault — drifts against the coil, leaves in the fall, a deck rail built too close. Keep 18-24 inches of clear space around the unit and clear snow below the coil (many units are mounted on risers for exactly this).

## Winter Rules for Heat Pump Owners

1. **Don't chip, scrape, or hose it.** Bending fins or cracking a line ends the season. If ice must go manually, a gentle room-temperature water rinse from a hose at midday is the ceiling of DIY — never hot water (thermal shock) and never force.
2. **Watch the AUX indicator during cold snaps.** AUX heat during a defrost cycle is normal and brief. AUX running for hours on end in ordinary cold means either a failed defrost, or a thermostat recovery pattern that needs the [heat-pump setback strategy](/blog/what-temperature-thermostat-winter/).
3. **Keep the coil area clear.** Snow below the unit, leaves around it in fall, and that "temporary" tarp over the top for two winters — all of them choke the airflow that both heating and defrost need.
4. **Let it run.** Heat pumps are designed to run long, steady cycles in cold weather; that's efficiency, not a malfunction — the full explainer is [heat pump running all day in winter](/blog/heat-pump-running-all-day-in-winter/). Short rapid cycling is the pattern that indicates trouble (the furnace-side version of that logic: [short cycling](/blog/why-does-my-furnace-keep-turning-on-and-off/)).

## The Defrost Cycle, Step by Step (So You Can Watch It Honestly)

Knowing the sequence turns a weird-looking winter event into a checkable process:

1. **Frost builds** on the outdoor coil during heating in humid cold (normal).
2. **The control checks in** on its timer/sensor schedule — typically every 30-90 minutes of compressor runtime.
3. **The reversal:** the reversing valve flips the system to cooling mode. The outdoor coil becomes the hot condenser; the indoor coil briefly becomes the evaporator.
4. **The backup engages:** because the house is now being "cooled," the electric resistance backup fires to hold indoor temperature — this is the aux-heat blip on your energy reports, and it's the expensive 5-15 minutes a failing defrost multiplies.
5. **The melt:** frost and thin ice slide off the coil as liquid and steam; the fan stays off so the coil can heat thoroughly (a running fan would throw cold air and slow the melt).
6. **The snap-back:** the valve returns to heating, the outdoor fan restarts, and the system resumes — usually with a soft whoosh and a puff of vapor.

Any step that doesn't happen — no reversal (valve), no heat at the coil (refrigerant), no termination back to heat (board/sensor) — produces one of the failure signatures in the checklist table above.

## Cold-Climate Units vs. Older Heat Pumps

Older and standard heat pumps defrost on fairly crude timers and lose meaningful capacity below roughly 25-35°F, which is where ice problems and aux-heavy bills concentrate. Modern cold-climate heat pumps carry variable-speed compressors, better defrost controls driven by actual coil sensors, and maintain capacity at much lower temperatures — part of why the DOE comparison our [space heater guide](/blog/42-best-energy-efficient-space-heater/) cites shows modern heat pumps cutting heating electricity use roughly 65% versus resistance heat. ## Service Now vs. Emergency Later (The Scheduling Honesty)

A defrost repair in November is a scheduled appointment at weekday rates. The same repair discovered during a January ice storm — aux heat running flat-out, house losing temperature, every HVAC company in the county on emergency queue — is a premium-priced visit with a wait, plus the interim aux-heat cost on your bill. This asymmetry is why the "ice thickening over days" row in the checklist deserves a phone call the day you notice it, not the day the house gets cold.

## Frequently Asked Questions

{{< faq "Is it normal for a heat pump to have ice on it in winter?" >}}
Yes — light frost on the outdoor coil in cold, humid weather (roughly 20-40°F, sleet, freezing rain) is the machine working as designed: the coil runs below freezing to absorb heat, and moisture freezes onto it. The built-in defrost cycle clears it every 30-90 minutes with a burst of steam. Ice that thickens over days or encases the case means the defrost system has failed.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How often should a heat pump defrost in winter?" >}}
Typically a defrost check every 30-90 minutes of compressor runtime in frosty conditions, with actual defrost cycles running 5-15 minutes as needed. Units in sleet or freezing rain defrost more often. What matters isn't the exact count — it's that frost never accumulates beyond what each cycle clears.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why is my heat pump steaming in winter?" >}}
That's the defrost cycle: the system briefly reverses to cooling mode, the outdoor coil runs hot, and the melting frost boils off as visible steam — usually with the outdoor fan paused. Steam events are the sign defrost is WORKING. A unit that never steams while frost accumulates is the failing one.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I pour hot water on my frozen heat pump?" >}}
No. Hot water can crack components, and any chipping or scraping bends the aluminum fins that cover the coil — they deform at a touch, and blocked airflow becomes its own permanent efficiency loss. If manual ice removal is unavoidable, a gentle room-temperature hose rinse at midday is the only method close to safe; recurring ice means a defrost repair, which fixes the cause.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Does a frozen heat pump use more electricity?" >}}
Markedly more — not from the ice itself but from what it causes: a frost-blocked coil can't harvest heat, so the thermostat calls the electric resistance backup, which produces heat at roughly one-third the compressor's efficiency. Failed defrost plus aux heat is a classic hidden driver of the winter bill spikes in [why is my heating bill so high](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How do I know if my heat pump is a cold-climate model?" >}}
Check the model number's spec sheet for capacity retained at 5°F — cold-climate units publish it prominently (often 70-100% of rated capacity), while standard units quietly don't. Age is a decent proxy: units shipped after the cold-climate product category matured carry variable-speed compressors and sensor-driven defrost, while pre-2015-era units are mostly timer-defrost designs that ice and aux-heat harder below 30°F. The spec plate on the outdoor unit has the model number that gets you the sheet.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How much does a defrost repair cost?" >}}
The two most common items are modest: a defrost sensor is a $15-50 part, and the diagnostic-and-clean visit around it typically lands in the same band as a standard heating service call — the [furnace tune-up cost and schedule](/blog/furnace-tune-up-cost-schedule/) guide's ranges cover it. The expensive tail is a stuck reversing valve or a refrigerant leak repair, which is why the diagnostic step exists: pay for the diagnosis, then decide with numbers in front of you.
{{< /faq >}}
