## Quick Answer

**Most often it's a running toilet — do the 10-minute dye test now.** Add a few drops of food coloring to the toilet tank, wait 10 minutes without flushing, and look at the bowl: if color appears, the flapper is leaking and every minute counts against your meter.[2]

The test is free, the leak hides inside a closed tank where nothing looks broken, and EPA tells homeowners to test toilets for silent leaks this way at least once a year because flappers wear out.[2]

Then run the 3-step meter check to confirm water is (or isn't) moving somewhere else:

1. **Shut off every faucet, ice maker, dishwasher, washing machine, and irrigation zone.**
2. **Find your water meter** (curbside pit or basement) and note the reading — or watch the small leak-indicator dial.
3. **Wait.** EPA's protocol: read the meter, read it again two hours later with no water use in between. If the reading changed, you have a leak in the home.[2]

Dye test clean and meter dead-still? Work down the ranked causes below. Bill jumped one month over the last? [Why water bills suddenly increase](/blog/why-did-my-water-bill-suddenly-increase/) is the faster path.

## The Causes, Ranked by Likelihood

Ranked by how often each explains a high bill — and how cheap the confirming test is.

### 1. A leaking toilet

A worn flapper lets tank water seep into the bowl around the clock — silent, invisible, adding thousands of metered gallons before anyone notices. In most U.S. cities sewer is billed on metered water, so the leak hits the bill twice. EPA advises replacing flappers at least once every five years.[2]

**How to confirm:** the 10-minute dye test above — food coloring in the tank, no flush, color in the bowl means a leak.[2] Full walkthrough and repair options: [is your toilet running? The simple leak test](/blog/is-your-toilet-running-leak-test/).

### 2. Irrigation and outdoor use

EPA puts outdoor use above 30 percent of household water use on average and as much as 60 percent in arid regions — and up to half of outdoor water is lost to wind, evaporation, and runoff from inefficient irrigation.[1]

**How to confirm:** run each irrigation zone and walk it — broken heads, misting, pooling, sidewalk spray. Run one cycle at dawn while watching the meter: creep during irrigation-only windows puts the loss outdoors. Deep dive: [why water bills climb in summer](/blog/why-is-my-water-bill-higher-in-summer/).

### 3. A rate, tier, or billing change

Your bill can climb with zero change in gallons. Utilities raise rates, restructure tiers, add surcharges, adjust sewer formulas — and under tiered rates, modest extra usage can land your household in a much more expensive block.

**How to confirm:** put the current statement beside last year's same-month statement and compare the usage line and the rate lines separately — usage up is a leak-or-behavior problem; usage flat with a higher total is a rate problem. Check the tariff, tier breakpoints, and any one-time charges. Full method: [tiered water rates explained](/blog/tiered-water-rates-explained/).

### 4. Meter error or estimated reads

Meters are usually right, but estimated reads, misreads, and failing meters happen.

**How to confirm:** check the bill for an "estimated" or "customer read" label, then compare the bill's ending meter figure to the physical meter — they should match within a dial or two. See [how to read a water meter (and know if it's accurate)](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/). If they don't reconcile, ask the utility for a meter test (questions below).

### 5. A longer billing period

Bills don't always cover 30 days — cycles run ~28 to ~35 days, and "more days at the same daily rate" reads as a spike on paper.

**How to confirm:** divide billed gallons by the service-period days printed on the statement and compare with last bill's daily figure. Daily use flat, total up? That's the calendar, not a leak.

### 6. Seasonal and household changes

Houseguests, more showers, a new appliance, a pool, evaporative cooling — usage moves with the calendar. Outdoor use drives the biggest seasonal swing.[1]

**How to confirm:** compare daily use (not totals) against the same month last year, then account for every household change. If daily use is far above the benchmark below with no lifestyle change to point at, go back to cause #1.

### 7. Water-softener and well-pump cycles

A softener stuck in regeneration, or a well pump cycling continuously, wastes water steadily and quietly. EPA notes softeners can be water-intensive and should be serviced annually.[2]

**How to confirm:** watch the softener through a full regeneration — continuous trickle-to-drain is the tell. For a well, watch the pressure tank for short-cycling.

## Benchmark Your Usage Before You Assume a Leak

EPA puts average use at 82 gallons per person per day at home, indoor and outdoor combined.[1] If your metered use runs far above the household figures below with no change in household or habits, start at ranked cause #1.

## Work the Checks in Order

Ranked list hasn't closed the case? Walk these three checks in order — one afternoon, zero dollars.

{{< visual src="/images/articles/06-water-bill-too-high/water-diagnosis-strip.webp" alt="Three-step process to investigate a high water bill: compare bills, test the meter, and isolate a valve." wide="true" >}}

### Check 1: Compare the bill line by line

Put the current statement beside a comparable earlier bill and record:

| Bill detail | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Service period | Start/end dates and total billing days |
| Meter/read status | Actual, estimated, customer-submitted, corrected, or another label |
| Usage | Billed quantity, unit, and any provider conversion |
| Charges | Usage, fixed, sewer, tax, credit, and one-time lines |
| Rate plan | Tariff, tier, season, and effective date |

A higher total alone does not identify the cause — separate usage, rate, and period. The utility controls the bill's units, rate structure, read status, and adjustment policy. For the sewer line especially, see [how sewer charges work on your water bill](/blog/how-sewer-charges-work-on-your-water-bill/).

### Check 2: Test the meter and document conditions

Follow your utility's meter-access and safety instructions. With no water in use, read the meter — EPA's protocol: read now, again two hours later; a changed reading means a leak[2] — or watch the leak-indicator dial. Start with the [water meter leak indicator](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/) (two minutes), then the full [water meter running when no water is used](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/) guide. Record the date, the observation, anything using water at the time, any visible moisture.

### Check 3: Isolate the source

Close the shutoff valve at each fixture one at a time and re-check the meter; when the meter stops, you've found the branch. Review fixtures and appliances only within the manufacturer's instructions and what you can access safely. For rental or shared service, notify the owner or manager in writing about use, repairs, and billing. For suspected concealed leaks or plumbing outside safe scope, contact a qualified professional.

## Ask the Utility Specific Questions

If the checks point to the bill rather than the plumbing, ask:

1. What usage unit, service period, and meter/read status produced this bill?
2. Did the tariff, tier, sewer method, or any rate component change?
3. Does the account contain an estimate correction, credit, or one-time charge?
4. What documentation does the provider require for a bill or meter review?
5. What current leak-adjustment or payment rules, if any, apply to this account?

Keep the bill, tariff, meter observations, photos, and repair records together — they support a precise review request.

## FAQ

{{< faq "Will a running toilet really raise my bill that much?" >}}

It can. The average household's leaks waste 9,400 gallons a year, most of it from silent leaks like worn toilet flappers that run around the clock.[1] Every leaked gallon is metered, and where sewer is billed on metered water, the leak raises both line items.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How do I tell a leak from a rate change?" >}}

Read the bill's two halves separately: the usage line (gallons or CCF) and the rate lines. Usage up → work the leak checks, dye test first. Usage flat but the total up → compare tariff, tier, and surcharge lines with last year's statement. See [tiered water rates explained](/blog/tiered-water-rates-explained/).

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "The dye test is clean but the meter still moves. Now what?" >}}

Water is flowing somewhere else — irrigation lines, service-line leaks, or a stuck softener are the usual suspects. Work the isolation procedure in [water meter running when no water is used](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/).

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I get a high bill adjusted after a leak?" >}}

Many utilities offer a one-time leak adjustment if you document the repair, but policies are local. Ask your provider what documentation it requires — before paying a bill you believe a leak caused, and before assuming any adjustment exists.

{{< /faq >}}
