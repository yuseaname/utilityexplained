## Quick Answer: What Utilities Cost in Florida {#quick-answer}

**Electricity:** the Florida residential rate averaged **14.99¢/kWh in 2026** (EIA preliminary year-to-date; 15.33¢ final for 2025) — below the 18.16¢/kWh national average. At the EIA national-standard 899 kWh month, that is **$135/month** for electricity, versus $163 nationally.

**Natural gas:** Florida residential gas averaged **$25.48/thousand cubic feet in 2025** (EIA annual) — about **$2.46/therm** after conversion, among the higher state gas prices. A standardized 75-therm heating month runs **$184**. (Most Florida households don't have gas heat — this line matters less here than in northern states.)

**Water & sewer:** there is **no reliable state average** — water is priced city-by-city in Florida. Nationally the combined bill runs about $70–$90/month; [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/) decodes yours.

One thing to know before comparing bills with friends in other states: Florida's *rate* is moderate but its *usage* is extreme. The annual bill is a rate story in winter states and a usage story here.

## Why there's no provider shopping in Florida {#why-there-is-no-shopping-in-florida}

Florida is a fully regulated, single-utility-territory state. Who serves you is decided by your address:

- **The big investor-owned utilities** — Florida Power & Light (the largest, covering much of the peninsula), Duke Energy Florida, and Tampa Electric (TECO) — set rates through cases before the [Florida Public Service Commission](https://www.floridapsc.com).
- **Municipal utilities** — JEA in Jacksonville, OUC in Orlando, and many city systems — set their own council-approved rates, often below the IOUs.
- **Rural electric cooperatives** cover the remaining territory.

No retail suppliers, no ESCOs, no plan marketplace — the idea of "switching providers" that works in Texas or New York simply doesn't exist here. That removes an entire category of bill mistakes (bad plans, holdover rates) but also removes rate shopping as a savings lever.

What remains are three levers, and they're all usage-side or structure-side.

## The three levers that actually work {#the-three-levers-that-work}

**1. Attack cooling load — it is the bill.** In most Florida homes the air conditioner is the single largest consumer by a wide margin, running much of the year. The ranked moves: thermostat discipline (each degree of cooling costs real money), [what temperature to set your thermostat in winter — and the summer mirror](/blog/what-temperature-thermostat-winter/), filter changes (a clogged filter makes the unit run longer), and [fixing an AC that runs but doesn't cool](/blog/ac-running-but-not-cooling/) before it burns kWh pointlessly. If your unit short-cycles, [AC short-cycling causes and fixes](/blog/ac-short-cycling-causes-fixes/) explains why that pattern inflates bills.

**The pool pump is Florida's hidden second appliance.** Pools are everywhere in Florida — and a single-speed pump running 8–12 hours a day draws more electricity than most households expect, often out-drawing the refrigerator many times over. Two moves, in order: cut runtime to the minimum that keeps water clear (test down in 30-minute steps), and if the pump is old and single-speed, a variable-speed replacement is one of the few big-ticket purchases that reliably pays for itself on a Florida bill — it qualifies for federal tax credits in many cases and cuts pump energy by half or more. Run the numbers for your own pool with the [electricity cost calculator](/calculators/electricity-cost/) before buying anything.

**2. Use your utility's own programs.** Florida's major utilities run free in-home or online energy audits, thermostat-cycle programs, and peak-time credits. These are the closest thing to "shopping" available — same wires, same rate, lower net cost. Check your utility's efficiency page; enrollment is usually a form.

**3. Read the bill's fee block.** Florida bills stack a customer charge, storm-protection plan charges, and fuel-related lines. If your bill jumps and usage didn't, the answer is usually in that block, not the thermostat. [Hidden fees on your utility bill](/blog/hidden-fees-utility-bill/) maps the common ones, and [delivery charge vs supply charge](/blog/delivery-charge-vs-supply-charge-utility-bill/) explains the split.

## Storm surcharges and fuel clauses {#storm-surcharges-and-fuel-clauses}

Two Florida-specific lines confuse people more than any others:

- **Fuel clauses.** Florida utilities pass fuel costs through with regulator approval, adjusted as prices move. When natural gas (the fuel behind much of Florida's generation) spikes, the fuel line rises even though your usage didn't — and it falls when fuel eases, which is part of why 2026-to-date rates eased.
- **Storm restoration and storm-protection charges.** After major hurricanes, restoration costs are recovered over time through a temporary surcharge; separately, multi-year storm-hardening programs (undergrounding, pole upgrades) carry their own charge lines. These are PSC-approved, itemized, and — importantly — not usage you can control.

The practical takeaway: when your bill jumps and the usage graph is flat, compare the surcharge block line-by-line against the previous bill before touching the thermostat.

## Water and sewer in Florida {#water-and-sewer-in-florida}

Water and sewer are set by your city or water district — two neighbors across a city line can pay very different rates for the same usage. Florida-specific wrinkles:

1. **Irrigation is the swing factor.** In many Florida utilities, irrigation tiers or separate irrigation meters price outdoor water well above indoor rates — a billing surprise northern transplants don't expect.
2. **Decode the units first.** Most Florida water bills measure in CCF (hundred cubic feet) or gallons: [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/).
3. **Benchmark usage.** A typical household runs about 2–4 CCF per person per month indoors; well above that, check irrigation first, then leaks ([the 10-minute meter check](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/)).
4. **Watch fixed charges.** Many systems have raised base charges faster than usage rates — your bill can climb even with flat usage. [Why your water bill is high](/blog/water-bill-too-high/) walks the whole statement.

{{< faq "Why is my electric bill so high in Florida if the rates are below average?">}}

Florida's 14.99¢/kWh (2026 EIA preliminary) is below the 18.16¢ national average — but near-continuous air conditioning in a humid climate drives usage far above the 899 kWh standardized month for much of the year. High Florida bills are usually a kWh story, not a rate story: thermostat settings, filter condition, duct leaks, and an aging unit running long cycles.

{{< faq "Can I choose my electric provider in Florida?">}}

No. Florida is a fully regulated, single-utility-territory state — your provider (FPL, Duke Energy Florida, TECO, a municipal utility, or a cooperative) is determined by your address, with rates set through the Florida Public Service Commission. There is no retail supplier marketplace as in Texas or New York; the savings levers are usage, your utility's efficiency programs, and the rate options your own utility offers.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What is the storm surcharge on my Florida utility bill?">}}

Two related charges appear on Florida bills: temporary storm-restoration surcharges that recover hurricane repair costs over time, and ongoing storm-protection plan charges funding grid hardening like undergrounding. Both are approved by the Florida Public Service Commission and itemized on the bill — they rise and fall independent of your usage, so check them first when a bill jumps without a usage change.
