## Quick Answer

Summer electric bills spike because air conditioning drives **40–50%** of home energy use during hot months. The biggest cost-cutting levers: raise the thermostat above **78°F**, shift laundry and dishes to off-peak hours, and seal duct leaks. Together these can cut cooling costs by **15–30%**.

<h2 id="cooling-load">AC is the #1 summer bill driver</h2>
<p>
              Air conditioning is the single largest driver of summer electric bills. A central AC
              can draw 2,000 to 5,000 watts while running (U.S. EIA, 2025). Run it several hours a
              day and you can add hundreds of <a href="/blog/what-is-a-kilowatt-hour">kWh</a> to the bill — which is the most common reason your
              <a href="/blog/03-why-is-my-electric-bill-so-high/">electric bill is so high in summer</a>.
            </p>
            <p>
              Humidity makes this worse because the system must remove moisture as
              well as cool the air. That extra latent-load work increases runtime
              even if the thermostat setting stays the same.
            </p>
            <p>
              Window units and portable ACs also add significant kWh, especially
              if multiple units run at once. Each unit can draw 500 to 1,500 watts,
              and older units often run at low <a href="/blog/afue-vs-seer-vs-hspf-vs-cop">SEER</a> efficiency.
            </p>
            <p>
              If your AC runs continuously but the home still feels warm, the system
              may be undersized, low on refrigerant, or short-cycling. See our guide
              on <a href="/blog/13-ac-short-cycling-causes-fixes/">AC short-cycling causes and fixes</a> to rule that out first.
            </p>

<h2 id="thermostat">Thermostat settings: the cheapest AC control</h2>
<p>
              A lower thermostat setting increases AC runtime. Setting the thermostat
              to 72°F instead of 76°F can raise energy use by <strong>10–20%</strong> depending on
              your home and climate.
            </p>
            <p>
              Sunlight and heat gain also matter. South- and west-facing windows can
              add significant heat in the afternoon. Closing blinds or using light
              curtains can reduce the load.
            </p>
            <p>
              Cooking indoors adds heat too. Using the oven frequently in summer can
              cause the AC to run more to counteract the extra heat.
            </p>
            <p>
              Using the microwave or outdoor grill for some meals reduces the
              indoor heat load on very hot days.
            </p>

<h2 id="maintenance">AC maintenance keeps efficiency high</h2>
<p>
              Dirty filters and blocked vents make the AC work harder. Replace filters
              monthly during peak season or as the manufacturer recommends. Keep
              outdoor condenser coils clear of debris so the system stays near its rated <a href="/blog/afue-vs-seer-vs-hspf-vs-cop">SEER</a> efficiency.
            </p>
            <p>
              If the system is older or low on refrigerant, it may run longer than
              normal. A maintenance check can identify issues that quietly raise
              power use.
            </p>
            <p>
              Also check that supply vents are not blocked by furniture or curtains. Poor
              airflow forces longer runtimes to reach the same temperature.
            </p>

<h2 id="time-of-use">Time-of-use rates can double peak-hour costs</h2>
<p>
              Many utilities charge higher rates during late afternoon and early
              evening in summer. That is when AC use often peaks. Even if kWh stays
              the same, the cost per kWh can be <strong>2–3x higher</strong> during those hours.
            </p>
            <p>
              <a href="/blog/08-time-of-use-electricity/">Time-of-use rate plans</a> reward pre-cooling your home earlier in
              the day and shifting laundry, dishwashing, and pool pumps to off-peak
              hours.
            </p>
            <p>
              <a href="/blog/tiered-electricity-rates-explained/">Tiered rate plans</a> work differently — they charge more as you cross kWh
              thresholds within a billing cycle, so any month you cross into a higher
              tier the marginal kWh cost jumps.
            </p>

<h2 id="humidity">Humidity makes the AC run longer</h2>
<p>
              High humidity makes your home feel warmer, so the AC runs longer to
              reach the same comfort level. In very humid climates, a dehumidifier
              can improve comfort but it also uses electricity. The combined load can
              raise kWh by <strong>20–40%</strong> if both run frequently.
            </p>
            <p>
              If you use a dehumidifier, run it during off-peak hours if your
              rate plan allows. Empty the tank regularly to keep it running efficiently.
            </p>

<h2 id="appliance-heat">Appliances that add hidden heat</h2>
<p>
              Refrigerators, dryers, and ovens release heat into your home. When it
              is hot outside, that extra heat makes the AC work harder. Running the
              dryer at night or air-drying clothing can reduce both heat and kWh.
            </p>
            <p>
              Lighting also adds a small amount of heat. Switching to LEDs reduces
              both energy use and heat output.
            </p>

<h2 id="billing-cycle">Billing cycle length skews summer comparisons</h2>
<p>
              A longer billing cycle can make a summer bill look worse than it is.
              Compare kWh per day before drawing conclusions — a 33-day cycle will
              always be higher than a 28-day cycle even at the same daily usage.
            </p>
            <p>
              Also check for any seasonal rate adjustments, which are common in
              summer when demand is higher. If your bill includes a fuel or supply
              adjustment line, that can shift the total even if kWh stays the same.
            </p>

<h2 id="utility-programs">Utility programs that lower summer bills</h2>
<p>
              Some utilities offer summer demand-response programs that reward you
              for reducing usage during peak hours. These programs can lower costs or
              provide bill credits, depending on the provider (state public utility
              commission).
            </p>
            <p>
              If you qualify, time-of-use education programs can help you shift
              energy use to cheaper hours. Check your utility website for available
              programs and eligibility.
            </p>
            <p>
              If your utility offers a smart thermostat rebate, it can be a low-cost
              way to automate higher settings when you are away without sacrificing
              comfort when you are home.
            </p>

<h2 id="daily-data">Use daily usage data to find AC spikes</h2>
<p>
              Daily or hourly usage charts make it easier to see when the AC runs the
              most. If the spikes align with afternoon hours, shading windows or
              pre-cooling earlier in the day may help.
            </p>
            <p>
              If usage is high overnight, check whether the thermostat is set too
              low or if the AC runs continuously because the home is not sealing
              properly.
            </p>
            <p>
              A quick experiment is to raise the thermostat by one degree for a
              week and compare daily kWh. Even small changes reveal how sensitive
              your usage is to temperature settings.
            </p>

<h2 id="insulation">Insulation stops attic heat from reaching you</h2>
<p>
              Attics can reach 130–150°F in summer. If insulation is thin, that heat
              transfers into your living space and increases cooling demand by
              <strong>15–25%</strong> in many homes. Sealing attic access points and adding
              insulation reduces heat gain.
            </p>
            <p>
              For renters, using insulated curtains or reflective window film can
              reduce heat gain without permanent changes.
            </p>
            <p>
              If you notice rooms that are consistently warmer, check for missing
              weatherstripping around doors or gaps around window frames.
            </p>

<h2 id="quick-fixes">Low-effort fixes to cut summer bills</h2>
<ul>
              <li>Set the thermostat a few degrees higher when away.</li>
              <li>Use ceiling fans to feel cooler without lowering the AC.</li>
              <li>Seal air leaks around doors and windows.</li>
              <li>Clean or replace AC filters regularly.</li>
              <li>Use blinds on sunny windows in the afternoon.</li>
            </ul>
            <p>
              Small changes can cut cooling runtime, which is the biggest source of
              summer kWh. For the full playbook, see our <a href="/blog/how-to-lower-electric-bill-complete-guide/">complete guide to lowering your electric bill</a>.
            </p>
            <p>
              If you have rooms you rarely use, close the vents and keep the doors
              shut so the system focuses on the spaces you actually occupy.
            </p>

## Summer Bill Increase Causes

| Cause | Typical Increase | How to Fix | Cost to Fix |
|-------|:---:|-----------|:---:|
| AC running more | 30–100% | Raise temp 2–3°F | $0 |
| AC inefficiency | 20–40% | Replace filter, service | $5–150 |
| Pool pump running | $30–80/mo | Reduce to 4–6 hrs | $0 |
| Dehumidifier | $15–40/mo | Use only when needed | $0 |
| Extra refrigerator | $15–25/mo | Unplug if rarely used | $0 |
| Fans left running | $5–15/mo | Turn off when leaving | $0 |
| Window AC units | $20–60/unit | Use central AC efficiently | $0 |
| Rate plan mismatch | 10–30% | Switch to TOU plan | $0 |

*Next step: Pull up your last three bills and divide kWh by days in the cycle. If your summer daily usage is more than 50% above your spring daily usage, the AC is the prime suspect — start with the thermostat, filter, and duct-sealing fixes above.*

{{< faq "Is it cheaper to leave the AC running all day?" >}}

Usually no. It is more efficient to raise the thermostat when you are away and cool the home when you return. A constant-on strategy usually costs **$30–$80/month** more than a setback strategy.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Do window units cost less than central AC?" >}}

It depends. Window units can be cheaper if you only cool a small area, but they are less efficient for whole-home cooling. A 10,000 BTU window unit at 10 EER costs more per kWh cooled than a modern central system at 16 SEER.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why is my bill higher even with the same temperature setting?" >}}

Hotter weather, higher humidity, and longer daylight hours can increase runtime. Rate changes, a longer billing cycle, or crossing into a higher tier can also raise the total even if kWh stays flat.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How much can raising the thermostat by 1 degree save?" >}}

About **3–5%** of cooling cost per degree in most homes. Raising from 72°F to 78°F typically saves **$36–$72/month** during peak summer.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why does my AC run but the house still feels warm?" >}}

Common causes: undersized system, low refrigerant, dirty coils, blocked ducts, or the system is short-cycling. A service tech can confirm which one it is. See our guide on <a href="/blog/13-ac-short-cycling-causes-fixes/">AC short-cycling causes and fixes</a>.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Does closing vents in unused rooms save energy?" >}}

Usually no. Central AC systems are balanced for full airflow. Closing vents raises duct pressure, can leak air at joints, and in some systems makes the AC run longer — costing more, not less.

{{< /faq >}}
