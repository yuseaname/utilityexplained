## Quick Answer

**A ductless mini-split moves heat between an outdoor unit and one or more indoor units without large air ducts; central air uses ductwork to distribute conditioned air.** The better fit depends on the home's calculated load, room layout, existing duct condition, equipment match, and installation plan—not a generic price or savings table.[1][2]

Ask for written proposals that identify the exact model numbers, load calculation, scope of electrical and duct work, airflow plan, and commissioning steps.

## What Each System Requires

### Ductless mini-split

DOE's Building America evaluation describes ductless mini-split heat pumps as systems with an outdoor unit connected to indoor units by refrigerant lines and controls.[1] A proposal should state the location and capacity of each indoor unit, line-set routing, condensate handling, electrical work, controls, and the rooms each unit is intended to serve.

### Central air

Central air uses an indoor air handler or furnace coil and ductwork to distribute air. For a ducted proposal, request an inspection of existing ducts and a statement of any repairs, sealing, redesign, or airflow verification included in the scope.[2]

## Compare the Home, Not Just the Equipment Label

DOE guidance on HVAC sizing emphasizes calculated heating and cooling loads, equipment selection, air distribution, and blower capacity.[2] Stage count, a published efficiency rating, or a brand name cannot by itself predict comfort or operating cost in a particular home.

Compare these items across proposals:

| Proposal item | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| Load calculation | What heating and cooling loads were used? |
| Equipment | What exact indoor and outdoor model numbers are matched? |
| Air distribution | How will ducts, returns, airflow, or indoor-unit placement be evaluated? |
| Installation | What electrical, drainage, line-set, duct, permit, and finish work is included? |
| Commissioning | What measurements will be verified after installation? |
| Operating estimate | Which local rates, weather assumptions, and equipment data support it? |

## Room-by-Room Control and Duct Condition

A ductless design can provide separate indoor units for the areas included in the design. That does not make a whole-home outcome automatic: placement, capacity, doors, layout, controls, and the calculated load still matter.[1][2]

If a home already has ducts, do not assume they are either unusable or problem-free. Ask for evidence from the inspection and for the exact corrective work, if any, included in the quote.

## Cost and Bill Claims Need Local Inputs

There is no defensible universal installed-price, maintenance-cost, noise, or bill-savings figure for this comparison. Costs can include equipment, electrical work, line-set routing, duct work, permits, site conditions, controls, and labor. Operating cost depends on the selected equipment, the home's load, use patterns, weather, and the applicable utility rate.

Ask contractors to show assumptions behind any estimate. Treat a quote as a scoped project proposal, not proof of a guaranteed bill result.

## Questions Before You Decide

- Can I receive the heating and cooling load-calculation summary?
- What rooms and conditions is this proposal designed to serve?
- What exact equipment is included, and is it manufacturer-matched?
- What duct, airflow, drainage, electrical, and permit work is included or excluded?
- How will the installer verify operation after installation?
- What maintenance tasks does the manufacturer require for this equipment?
