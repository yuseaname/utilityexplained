## Quick Answer

**Set the thermostat to 68°F while you're awake and at home, and 7-10°F lower — 58-61°F — for the eight hours you sleep and any stretch nobody's home.** That single pattern is worth roughly 10% off your heating bill under the Department of Energy's long-standing rule of thumb (about 1% per degree of setback held for 8 hours). For vacations or an empty house in a hard-freeze climate, the floor is **55°F** — low enough to save, high enough to keep pipes safe, per the American Red Cross's frozen-pipe guidance. The one big exception: **heat pumps** should use milder 2-4°F setbacks or a smart thermostat's staged recovery, because a deep recovery can trigger expensive auxiliary heat.

If you're choosing hardware to automate this, that's our [smart thermostat comparison](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-winter-savings/); this page is the free part — what the schedule should actually say.

## The Two Numbers That Matter

Everything in winter thermostat strategy reduces to two settings and a discipline:

1. **68°F occupied.** Not a magic constant — it's the balance point most households find comfortable in sweaters-and-slippers clothing. If 66°F works for you, you save a bit more; if the household genuinely needs 71°F, that costs a bit more. The bill difference between 68°F and 70°F held all winter is real but modest (rule of thumb: ~2-3% of heating per 2°F); the BIG money is in the setback hours, not the occupied setpoint.
2. **7-10°F lower for 8 hours.** Overnight while sleeping (blankets do the compensating) and during the workday in an empty house. This is where the ~10% lives, and it's the setting most households never program.

## Why the Setback Saves (It's Not "Reheat Eats the Savings")

The persistent myth: "the furnace works so hard warming the house back up that you save nothing." It's false for a physical reason worth 30 seconds: **heat loss from your house is proportional to the indoor-outdoor temperature difference.** Drop the inside from 68°F to 58°F on a 28°F night and the rate of heat loss drops by a quarter for those hours. The house genuinely banking less lost heat IS the savings; the morning recovery burns extra fuel for 30-45 minutes, but it's buying back a fraction of what the setback hours banked. Net: positive, reliably.

The DOE's rule of thumb — roughly 1% of heating energy per degree of setback per 8 hours — has survived decades of field testing. Manufacturer studies land in the same neighborhood: Nest's own published averages run around 10-12% on heating for homes that adopted schedules (manufacturer-reported; our [smart thermostat comparison](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-winter-savings/) quotes the claim wording precisely).

## The Exception That Pays for a Smart Thermostat: Heat Pumps

If you heat with a heat pump, deep setbacks can backfire, and this is the single most expensive thermostat mistake of the season. The physics: below a certain outdoor temperature the compressor alone can't recover a 10°F drop quickly, so the system calls in **auxiliary electric resistance strips** — heat at one-half to one-third the compressor's efficiency. A deep setback that pays back $2 overnight can trigger a $1.50 aux blast every morning recovering it. Marginal becomes losing.

The right pattern on a heat pump:

1. **Mild setbacks — 2-4°F** — for sleep and away periods.
2. **Or let a smart thermostat stage the recovery**: modern units start the warm-up early and slow, finishing on the compressor alone. This is the #1 heat-pump-specific reason to upgrade hardware; the [thermostat tier comparison](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-winter-savings/) flags which models handle it well.
3. **Never use "Emergency Heat"** for warming the house faster — that's a maintenance mode, not a turbo button. The full explanation is in [aux heat vs. emergency heat](/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/), and a heat pump that runs aux constantly all winter is usually a [bill problem](/blog/why-is-my-heating-bill-so-high/) with a specific fix.

## The Full Settings Table

| Situation | Setpoint | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Home, awake (day/evening) | **68°F** | Comfort baseline in winter clothing |
| Home, asleep | **60-62°F** | Blankets compensate; full 8-hour setback window |
| Away at work (empty house) | **58-61°F** | Same math as overnight; nobody's comfort to protect |
| Away for the weekend+ | **60°F** | Recovery on return; pipes not a concern yet |
| Vacation / vacant in freeze climate | **55°F floor** | Red Cross frozen-pipe guidance: don't go lower than 55°F while away in winter — this protects plumbing, and it's also why our [frozen-pipes ladder](/blog/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/) says "same temperature day and night" during extreme events |
| Someone home all day (infants, night shifts, remote work) | 68°F held | A held comfortable temp beats griefing a schedule — program the setback into the hours that ARE empty |
| Heat pump (any of the above) | Setback only 2-4°F, or staged recovery | Avoid aux-strip recovery; see above |

Special populations: infants' rooms are often recommended slightly warmer than the hallway (around 68-72°F for sleep safety), and elderly or medically sensitive households should treat 68°F as a floor, not a target. No energy saving is worth a hypothermia risk — the setback pattern presumes blankets and healthy adults.

## Special Cases Worth Knowing

**Recovery timing.** A thermostat that starts recovery at 6:00am for a 6:30 wake time runs the system at max exactly when the house is coldest (pre-dawn). Smart thermostats back-calculate the start time so the warmup finishes — not starts — at wake time. Manual schedules should do the same: set recovery 30-45 minutes before you need warmth, not at the moment you need it.

**Zoned homes and one cold room.** If the schedule serves the hallway thermostat but the bedroom runs cold, the fix is a remote sensor (ecobee/Nest both include one in their premium tiers — see the [comparison table](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-winter-savings/)), not a whole-house setpoint bumped for one room's sake. And a genuinely cold room often has a draft to fix first: [how to find and seal drafts](/blog/how-to-find-and-seal-drafts-in-your-home/).

**"Same temperature day and night" during a polar vortex.** When the forecast is a hard multi-day freeze, the Red Cross guidance shifts: hold a steady temperature around the clock (and open sink-cabinet doors on exterior walls). The pipe-protection value outranks the setback savings for those few days — the full ranked list is in [how to keep pipes from freezing](/blog/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/).

## What Each Degree Actually Costs (and Saves)

The occupied setpoint conversation deserves real numbers, because "turn it down two degrees" is advice without a price tag until you attach one. Using the same rule of thumb — about 1% of heating energy per degree, sustained — applied to a $150/month winter heating bill:

| Change | Monthly effect | Season (5 months) |
|---|---|---|
| Hold 70°F instead of 68°F | +$2-3 | +$10-15 |
| Hold 66°F instead of 68°F | -$2-3 | -$10-15 |
| Add overnight 10°F setback (from none) | -$12-15 | -$60-75 |
| Add workday setback for empty house too | another -$8-12 | another -$40-60 |

The pattern is unambiguous: the occupied setpoint is fine-tuning; the setbacks are the instrument panel. A household that holds 70°F around the clock spends more than one at 68°F with disciplined setbacks — by a factor of three or four on the difference.

**The humidity footnote (why 68°F feels different in January than October):** drier air makes skin feel cooler at the same temperature, which is why the same 68°F that was fine in autumn feels thin in deep winter — the air is drier because cold outdoor air carries less moisture indoors when heated. Adding humidity doesn't change the thermometer, but it changes comfort at a given setpoint. We frame this honestly per our editorial standard on climate devices: a humidifier is a comfort purchase with climate-dependent results, not a heating-bill fix — but if it lets you hold 66°F comfortably instead of bumping to 69°F in January, it has paid its keep. Pets in the "what about the dog all day" question: healthy adult dogs and cats are comfortable well below human preferences — the 58-61°F away setpoint is fine; very young, old, or short-coated animals are the exceptions worth a floor of ~64°F.

## Frequently Asked Questions

{{< faq "What is the best temperature to set your thermostat in winter?" >}}
68°F when you're awake and home, and 58-61°F for the eight hours you sleep or the hours the house is empty. The setback — not the 68°F itself — is where roughly 10% of heating savings comes from (about 1% per degree held 8 hours, per the Department of Energy's rule of thumb).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Is it cheaper to leave the thermostat at one temperature all the time?" >}}
No — that's the myth. Heat loss scales with the indoor-outdoor temperature difference, so a house held at 58°F overnight loses heat slower than one held at 68°F. The morning recovery burns extra fuel, but far less than the setback hours saved. Constant-temperature is simpler, not cheaper.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What temperature should I set when on vacation in winter?" >}}
55°F, and no lower. That's the Red Cross's frozen-pipe floor for an empty house in freezing climates: low enough to save meaningfully, high enough that plumbing in exterior walls stays out of the danger zone. Ask a neighbor to check during a hard freeze, and see the full freeze-protection ladder before you go.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Does thermostat setback work with a heat pump?" >}}
Differently. Deep 8-10°F setbacks can force the heat pump's auxiliary resistance strips during morning recovery, and strips run at one-half to one-third compressor efficiency — the setback savings get eaten. Use mild 2-4°F setbacks, or a smart thermostat that stages recovery on the compressor. Details: [aux heat vs. emergency heat](/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How many degrees should I turn the thermostat down at night?" >}}
Seven to ten degrees is the standard recommendation — 60-62°F from a 68°F baseline — held for the ~8 hours of sleep. Every degree of that range is worth roughly 1% of your heating bill.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Does lowering the thermostat really save that much money?" >}}
On a $150/month winter heating bill, the full pattern (68°F occupied, 10°F overnight setback, away-period setbacks) typically returns 8-13%, or $12-20/month — about $60-100 across a heating season. Larger in colder climates, smaller in mild ones; the percentage holds, the base bill moves.
{{< /faq >}}
