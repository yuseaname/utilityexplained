## Quick Answer

**The standard rule across most US utilities: the utility owns and repairs the pipe from the water main up to (and usually including) your meter; you own everything from the meter's outlet to and inside your house.** A leak before the meter is the utility's problem and cost; a leak after it is yours — including the stretch of underground service line that runs under your own yard, which surprises most homeowners. If water is still moving through your meter with everything in the house shut off, the leak is almost certainly on your side. [How to run that test](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/).

But knowing the rule is the easy half. The hard half is the boundary case — a leak at the meter box itself, a utility that says "not ours" to a pipe you believe is theirs, a bill for water you never used. This page covers the rule, the boundary map, and the escalation path with the evidence to bring. For the diagnosis steps that get you here, start with the [leak indicator guide](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/); for what a mystery leak does to the bill, see [why water bills suddenly increase](/blog/why-did-my-water-bill-suddenly-increase/).

## The boundary map: where "yours" starts

Every property with a municipal water connection has the same four-part anatomy. Responsibility flips exactly once, at the meter:

| Segment | What it is | Who owns/repairs it |
|---|---|---|
| Water main | The utility's pipe under the street | Utility |
| Connection + service line to the meter | The pipe from the main to your meter box (often at the property line, curb, or easement) | Utility (in most jurisdictions) |
| **The meter** | The meter body itself and its fittings | Utility |
| Service line from meter to house + all indoor plumbing | The pipe under your yard, into and through your house | **You** |

{{< visual src="/images/articles/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/boundary-map.webp" alt="Photograph of a residential service-line trench running from a curbside meter to a house, overlaid with the four water-service segments and the point where responsibility flips from utility to homeowner." wide="true" >}}

Three wrinkles worth knowing before you point a finger:

- **"Before the meter" is not the same as "before your property line."** In most jurisdictions the utility's responsibility ends at the meter, even when the meter sits at the street — meaning the entire yard run is yours. But some utilities own further (to a curb stop valve), and some municipal codes push responsibility back to the property line even without a meter there. The only authoritative answer is your own utility's service-line policy, usually a one-page document on its website.
- **The meter box itself is a gray zone.** The lid, box, and fitting repairs are commonly utility territory even when the surrounding pipe is yours. A meter box standing full of water is often the utility's maintenance issue, not a plumbing emergency — the [full rain-vs-leak decision tree](/blog/water-meter-box-full-of-water/) sorts which — but the pipe joints on either side of the meter follow the ownership rule above.
- **Private wells and HOA/community systems rewrite everything.** With a well, you own all of it. In an HOA or shared system, the bylaws decide. Ask for the service-territory map in writing.

## The 10-minute test that settles "which side is it on?"

You rarely need to excavate to know which side of the meter a leak is on. The meter is the boundary and the instrument:

1. **Shut off your main shutoff valve** (usually where the line enters the house, or at the meter box if there's a customer-side valve).
2. **Watch the meter's leak indicator or register dial.** If it keeps moving with your side isolated, water is flowing *between the main and your shutoff* — the utility side of your isolation point. [How to find the leak indicator](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/).
3. **Reverse it:** house valve open, all fixtures off, meter still moving — the flow is inside or under your house.
4. **Photo everything:** the meter reading, the moving indicator, the valve position, the wet spot or box condition. Timestamped photos are your entire case file if the boundary is disputed later.

{{< visual src="/images/articles/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/shutoff-test-flow.webp" alt="Photographic two-panel water-meter and shutoff-valve test: a meter still moving with the house valve closed points to the utility side, while a meter stopping at the house valve points to the homeowner side." wide="true" >}}

If the meter stops the moment you close your house valve, the mystery flow is on your side of that valve — under the yard or in the house. That's when the [meter-vs-main-valve isolation walkthrough](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/) narrows it further, and when you start collecting plumber quotes rather than utility claims.

## What "yours" costs when it's the underground service line

The bill homeowners don't see coming: the yard run of pipe is your responsibility even though it's buried under ground you might think of as the utility's corridor. When it leaks or freezes:

- **Typical repair cost:** professional main-line repair averages around **$990, with most projects between $350 and $1,665** depending on depth, length, and whether the line is copper, galvanized, or plastic; plumber labor runs **$75–150/hour**. [4][5]
- **Excavation is the cost driver.** The pipe is cheap; the digging and restoration are not. Trenchless options exist in some cities but are not universal.
- **Homeowners insurance usually does not cover the line itself.** Standard policies typically exclude service-line repair; some insurers sell it as a cheap add-on rider. If you have a long yard run of aging pipe, that rider is worth a phone call — ideally before the leak, not after. [6]
- **Sewer laterals are a separate but parallel trap.** The same before/after logic applies to the sewer side, with the same insurance exclusion. [How sewer charges work](/blog/how-sewer-charges-work-on-your-water-bill/) explains which side of that bill you can influence.

## When the utility says "not ours" — the escalation path

Utilities state their own rule, and their frontline answer is not always the last word — especially when the leak sits in the boundary's gray zones. The differentiation gap this page exists to fill:

1. **Ask for the determination in writing, with the boundary cited.** "Customer-side" on the phone is not a determination; a written reference to the tap or meter point is. Most utilities have a field supervisor who makes formal boundary calls.
2. **Present your meter evidence, not your opinion.** The timestamped photos from the 10-minute test show exactly where flow was isolated. If the meter was replaced or re-read recently, note it — meter work is sometimes the cause of fitting leaks that appear days later.
3. **Ask about leak-adjustment policy.** Many utilities will credit part of the bill for water lost through an underground leak you repaired — typically one billing period's excess over your average, once, after proof of repair. This is the money conversation, and it's separate from who fixes the pipe. ([The full letter-template process](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/).)
4. **State PUC/public-works complaint** is the backstop if the utility refuses to engage: most states let you file without a lawyer, and the threat of a regulator reading the file often unblocks a stalled determination.

## Season's warning: the freeze months are when this page matters most

Service-line breaks cluster in the freeze months — deep-january cold snaps, thaw cycles, and the first hard freeze after a dry autumn. If you're reading this in fall: the cheapest preparation is knowing where your meter, curb stop, and house shutoff are, that they turn, and where your boundary sits on the utility's map. A [meter-mounted flow monitor](/blog/point-sensor-vs-whole-home-water-leak-when-to-upgrade/) catches a slow underground leak weeks before the bill does — and weeks before the burst.

{{< faq "Is the water meter itself my responsibility?" >}}

Usually no — the meter body and its fittings belong to the utility, and tampering with it is prohibited in nearly every jurisdiction. But the pipes on either side follow the ownership boundary: the utility's pipe up to the meter, yours from the meter onward. If the meter itself leaks at its fittings, report it to the utility; don't repair it yourself.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What if the leak is exactly at the meter box?" >}}

This is the classic gray zone. The box, lid, and meter fittings are commonly utility-maintained even when the customer-side pipe nearby is yours. Photograph the condition, run the shutoff test, and let the utility make the first determination — boundary cases at the box are resolved by their field supervisor, not by who excavates first.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Does homeowners insurance cover a leaking service line under my yard?" >}}

Typically no. Standard homeowners policies generally exclude the service line itself, though resulting interior damage (a collapsed slab, for example) may be covered. Several insurers offer inexpensive service-line riders; if you have an aging line and a long yard run, price the rider before the leak.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I get the water bill credited for a leak I repaired?" >}}

Often yes, partially. Many utilities have a leak-adjustment policy: with proof of repair, they credit the excess usage above your historical average for one billing period. The request usually must be made in writing within a set window. Ask for the policy by name when you call — "leak adjustment," not "bill dispute."

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Who do I call first — utility or plumber?" >}}

Run the 10-minute shutoff test first, then: utility-side evidence (meter keeps moving with your house valve closed, or the leak is clearly before the meter) → utility. Your-side evidence (meter stops at the house valve, or wet spot between meter and house) → plumber for the yard run, or [check the leak-adjustment angle](/blog/why-did-my-water-bill-suddenly-increase/) on the bill while the repair is scheduled.

{{< /faq >}}

## The Boundary Is Documentable

The rule is simple; the disputes are not. The homeowners who win boundary calls are the ones who arrive with timestamped meter photos, a written determination request, and the utility's own service-line policy printed out. Ten minutes of documentation beats an hour of phone tag — and the meter, which belongs to the utility, is the instrument that usually settles the case in your favor.

**Where to go next:** run the shutoff test today and photograph the meter state; if it's your side, [isolate the source](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/) and collect quotes; if it's theirs or ambiguous, request the written boundary determination and ask about the leak-adjustment policy in the same call.
