## Quick Answer

**Yes, you can dispute a utility bill — and doing it right takes under an hour.** The process works for electric, gas, and water, and it doesn't require a lawyer. It requires a little evidence and a clear, formal request.

- **Verify the meter reading yourself today** — take a dated photo of your meter and compare it to the number on the bill
- **Call the utility and open a formal dispute** — ask for a case or reference number and keep it
- **While the dispute is open, pay the undisputed portion** — this keeps your service safe and your position strong
- **Unresolved after 30 days? Escalate to your state Public Utility Commission** — it's free, and utilities take PUC complaints seriously

If your bill is wrong, the worst thing you can do is ignore it. The second-worst is to pay it in full and hope for a credit later. The right move is to dispute it formally, in writing, with evidence — and keep paying what you actually owe while you do.

## Before You Call: Build Your 10-Minute Evidence File

A dispute without evidence is just an argument. A dispute with evidence is a case. Before you call, spend ten minutes assembling the four things below — you'll use them in the call, the letter, and if you escalate.

**1. A dated photo or video of your meter, taken today.** This is your most powerful evidence. Read your meter and photograph it with the date visible (a newspaper, your phone's timestamp, or a handwritten note in the frame all work). Not sure how? See our guides for [electric meters](/blog/how-to-read-electric-meter/), [gas meters](/blog/how-to-read-gas-meter/), and [water meters](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/).

**2. Your last 12 bills.** You want two things: your usage history and your charge history. A sudden spike in usage with no change in your household is a red flag. A jump in the *rate* or *fees* with flat usage is a different problem. Save them as PDFs.

**3. The exact charge line you are disputing.** A utility bill is not one number — it's a stack of line items: usage, delivery, taxes, fees, surcharges, and sometimes third-party charges. Name the specific line. If you're unsure what a line means, our [electric bill line-item breakdown](/blog/electric-bill-breakdown-understanding-line-items/) walks through each one.

**4. A one-sentence summary of what is wrong.** "My bill says I used 1,200 kWh but my meter reads 800" is a dispute. "My bill is too high" is not. Billing systems are built to handle specific errors, not general complaints.

**Which line is wrong matters.** There are three categories, each with a different fix: **usage** (the reading or estimate doesn't match reality — submit your own reading, request a reread), **rate** (you were charged the wrong tier or plan — ask for a rate review), or **fee** (a charge you don't recognize — ask for an itemized explanation). Identify your category and you've done most of the work.

## The Call: What to Say and What to Get

Once your evidence file is ready, make the call. Keep it calm, specific, and formal. The goal is not to win the argument — it's to open a *formal dispute* and get a *case number*. That number is your proof the dispute exists, and it's what you'll reference if you escalate.

**A script that works:**

> "I'd like to open a formal billing dispute on my account. My account number is [number]. I believe the [usage/rate/fee] on my [date] bill is incorrect. My meter reads [reading] as of today, and I have a dated photo. Can you open a dispute, give me a case or reference number, and note the account?"

**Three magic phrases:**

- **"Formal billing dispute"** — signals you want the official process, not a casual adjustment
- **"Case or reference number"** — forces them to create a trackable record
- **"Please note the account"** — puts your request on the record

**What to ask for before you hang up:** the case or reference number and the name or ID of the person you spoke with; whether the bill is based on an [estimated or actual reading](/blog/14-estimated-utility-bill-explained/) (if estimated, ask for an actual reread); a meter reread or billing audit; how long the review will take; and whether the disputed amount is placed on hold. Write down the date, time, and the answers — this record is your protection if the utility later says the dispute never happened.

## Keep Paying the Undisputed Part

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that protects you. A dispute is **not** a payment holiday. If you stop paying entirely, you risk disconnection — and a utility can generally disconnect for nonpayment even while a dispute is pending, unless your state pauses it. See [when the utility can shut off your service](/blog/can-utility-shut-off-service/) for the full picture.

The safe move: pay the portion you don't dispute, and withhold only the disputed amount. This keeps your account current on everything except the contested line, making disconnection far less likely. It also makes you look reasonable — and a reasonable customer with evidence is hard to dismiss.

**How partial payment is applied matters.** Ask how they apply a partial payment. Some apply it to the oldest charges, some to the current bill, and some to the disputed amount first — which would defeat the purpose. If they apply it to the disputed line, ask them to apply it to the undisputed portion instead, and get that noted on the account.

If the utility refuses a partial payment or insists the full amount is due, say so in writing and keep your evidence. That refusal is exactly what a state commission wants to hear about.

## Common Dispute Types and What Wins Each

Not all disputes are equal. Some are almost automatic wins with the right evidence; others require a formal meter test. Here's the practical breakdown:

| Dispute type | What it looks like | What wins it |
|---|---|---|
| **Estimated read too high** | Bill says "estimated" and the number is far above your normal usage | Your own dated meter photo + submitting a self-read; ask for an actual reread. See [estimated bills explained](/blog/14-estimated-utility-bill-explained/) |
| **Sudden spike, no usage change** | Usage jumped 2-3x with no new appliance or occupants | Compare last 12 bills; check for a missed read or catch-up from a prior estimate. See [sudden spike guide](/blog/30-sudden-spike-in-electricity-bill-no-usage/) |
| **Post-move-in catch-up bill** | You moved in and got a huge bill covering the previous occupant's usage | Ask for [proration](/blog/20-utility-bill-proration-explained/) to your move-in date; provide your lease or move-in date |
| **Deposit or billing-cycle quirk** | A deposit, minimum bill, or cycle-length change inflated the amount | Ask for an itemized explanation; confirm the billing cycle length and deposit terms |
| **Unauthorized third-party supplier charge** | A charge from a company you never signed up with (slamming/cramming) | Dispute in writing immediately; this is a fast-track issue at most state PUCs — report it there directly |
| **Smart-meter accuracy** | You suspect the meter itself is over-reading | Request a formal meter test. Utilities generally must test meters on request, and accuracy standards exist — but the process and who pays vary. |

One honest note on smart meters: if the test comes back within tolerance, the utility will usually hold you to the reading. That's why your own dated photo and usage history matter — they're your evidence before you spend time on a formal test.

## Escalate to the State PUC or Commission

If the utility deadlocks, or 30 days pass with no resolution, escalate. Your state's Public Utility Commission (PUC) — sometimes called the public service or corporation commission — regulates utilities and can order bill corrections, credits, and payment plans.

**When to escalate:** the utility refuses to open a dispute or won't give you a case number; the dispute has been open roughly 30 days with no resolution; the utility won't correct an error you can prove with evidence; or you suspect slamming or cramming.

**How to file:** In most states, you file online through the commission's website, and it's free. You'll provide your account info, the utility's name, a description of the problem, and your evidence. Keep your case number from the utility — the commission will ask for it.

**What happens after you file:** The commission notifies the utility, and the utility must respond formally — usually within a set number of business days. The commission then reviews and can order a correction, a credit, a meter test, or a payment plan. The general pattern is that utilities take PUC complaints seriously, because unresolved complaints count against them.

**Possible outcomes:** a corrected bill, a credit, a formal meter test, a payment plan, or an order that the utility stop a disputed collection. The commission is the backstop — and it's free, which is why it's the right escalation for a consumer.

## If the Utility Is a City or Municipal Provider

One important exception: if your water (or sometimes electric) is provided by a city or municipal utility, the state PUC may not have jurisdiction. Municipal utilities are often governed by a city council, a utilities board, or a local commission instead.

If that's your situation, your alternatives are:

- **The city council or utilities board** — attend a meeting or file a written complaint; these bodies oversee the utility and can pressure it to correct errors
- **The state attorney general's consumer protection office** — handles unfair or deceptive billing practices and can intervene even where the PUC can't

Start by asking your utility who regulates it. If they say the PUC doesn't oversee them, ask for the name of the governing board or council. That answer tells you exactly where to escalate.

## Sample Dispute Letter and Phone Script

Here's a fill-in template you can adapt. It's generic on purpose — it works for electric, gas, or water, and it doesn't rely on any specific state law. Fill in the brackets, attach your evidence, and send it by email (with a read receipt) and by certified mail if you want a paper trail.

**Sample dispute letter:**

> [Your Name]
> [Your Address]
> [City, State, ZIP]
> [Date]
>
> [Utility Name]
> [Billing Department]
> [Utility Address]
>
> **Re: Formal Billing Dispute — Account #[Your Account Number]**
>
> To Whom It May Concern:
>
> I am writing to formally dispute the [usage/rate/fee] charge on my bill dated [bill date], in the amount of $[disputed amount]. I believe this charge is incorrect for the following reason: [one or two sentences — e.g., "my meter reads X as of today, but the bill reflects Y"].
>
> I have attached the following evidence: [list — e.g., a dated photo of my meter, my last 12 bills, my lease showing my move-in date].
>
> I request that you: [requested action — e.g., "reread my meter and correct the bill," "review the rate applied to my account," "remove the unauthorized charge"]. Please respond in writing within [number, e.g., 15] business days.
>
> I have paid the undisputed portion of my bill and will continue to do so while this dispute is open. If this is not resolved, I will escalate to [state PUC name / city utilities board / state attorney general].
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your Name]
> [Your Phone Number]
> [Your Email]

**Phone script (for the follow-up call):**

> "I'm calling about case number [number] I opened on [date]. I'm following up on my formal dispute of the [usage/rate/fee] charge of $[amount]. I've attached my evidence — my dated meter photo and my last 12 bills. Can you confirm the status, and when will I have a written response?"

Keep every response in writing. If they resolve it, get the resolution in writing too. If they don't, you have everything you need to escalate.

## FAQ

{{< faq "Can the utility disconnect me while I'm disputing the bill?" >}}
Generally, yes — a dispute is not a payment holiday. If you stop paying entirely, the utility can pursue disconnection for nonpayment. The safe move is to pay the undisputed portion and withhold only the disputed amount. Some states pause disconnection during a good-faith dispute, but don't rely on it. See [when the utility can shut off your service](/blog/can-utility-shut-off-service/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How long does the utility have to respond to my dispute?" >}}
It varies. Many utilities aim to respond within a few business days to a couple of weeks, and state PUCs typically require a formal response within a set number of business days after you file a complaint. If you've heard nothing after 30 days, escalate.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Do I have to pay late fees on the disputed part?" >}}
Not necessarily. If the disputed amount is later found to be an error, most utilities will remove any late fees on that portion. The safest approach is to pay the undisputed part on time and ask, in writing, that no late fees accrue on the disputed amount while it's under review.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What if the meter test says my meter is fine?" >}}
Then the utility will generally hold you to the reading, and your dispute likely won't succeed on accuracy grounds. That's why your own dated photo and usage history matter — they're your evidence before you request a formal test. If the test is fine but your usage genuinely changed, look for a leak, a running appliance, or a new device.
{{< /faq >}}
