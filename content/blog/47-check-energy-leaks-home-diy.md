## Quick Answer

**A do-it-yourself assessment can help document possible air leaks, but it is not a substitute for safe electrical, combustion, structural, moisture, or HVAC work.** DOE’s DIY guidance says to look for leaks around windows and doors and to plug or caulk holes or penetrations for faucets, pipes, electric outlets, and wiring with appropriate materials.[1]

Do not rely on generic savings claims or a one-size-fits-all repair list. First document the location, condition, and safe scope of any suspected issue.

## Build a Room-by-Room Record

Walk through the home and note visible gaps, damaged weatherstripping, drafts, moisture, and comfort concerns. Record the date, room, location, photo if appropriate, and whether the condition involves a movable joint, penetration, ventilation component, electrical item, combustion appliance, or building damage.

Focus on locations identified in DOE guidance, including windows, doors, faucets, pipes, outlets, wiring penetrations, cracks, and holes in exterior materials.[1]

| Location | What to record | Follow-up question |
|---|---|---|
| Windows and doors | Seal condition, operability, visible gap, moisture | Is weatherstripping or repair appropriate for this assembly? |
| Pipe, wire, and outlet penetrations | Location, gap, material, signs of damage | What material and safety steps does this location require? |
| Attic, basement, or crawlspace | Access, insulation, moisture, exposed equipment | Is professional assessment needed before work? |
| HVAC/combustion area | Unusual operation, damage, odor, venting concern | Should a qualified professional inspect it? |

## Match the Fix to the Location

DOE explains that weatherstripping can be used to seal air leaks around movable joints such as windows or doors.[3] Select materials and methods that match the installed assembly and follow manufacturer instructions. For rental housing, obtain the property owner or manager’s approval before altering the unit.

Do not cover required ventilation, alter gas equipment, work on energized electrical components, disturb suspected hazardous material, or perform structural repairs as a DIY air-sealing project.

## Know When a Professional Assessment Is Better

DOE describes a professional home energy assessment as a first step for understanding energy use and choosing measures that improve efficiency and comfort.[2] Seek qualified help when there is persistent moisture, visible building damage, an electrical concern, combustion safety issue, inaccessible location, or uncertainty about the correct repair method.

Keep the assessment notes, bills, repair records, and any contractor findings together. That record is more useful than assuming a particular project will produce a specified bill reduction.
