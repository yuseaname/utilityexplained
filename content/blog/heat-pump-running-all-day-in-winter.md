Your heat pump has been running for hours. The house is warm, but the system never seems to shut off, and the compressor noise is starting to feel like a countdown to a repair bill. Before you panic: long runtime can be normal, because heat pumps move heat instead of making it. Still, there is a real line between normal and a symptom. This guide draws that line.

## The short answer

A heat pump can run for long stretches in winter because it is extracting heat from cold outdoor air. Longer runtime does not always mean a problem, especially during freezing weather.

If the home never reaches setpoint or the air feels cold all day, it is time to dig deeper.

## Normal vs symptom: the verdict table

Match what your system is doing against the left column first. Only the right column means trouble:

| ✅ NORMAL — no action needed | ⚠️ SYMPTOM — dig deeper |
|---|---|
| Runs 30–60 minutes, then off for 10–15 minutes (35–50°F weather) | Short cycles of 5–10 minutes, on and off repeatedly |
| Runs nearly continuously at 15–35°F, house *holds* the setpoint | Runs continuously but the house temperature keeps *dropping* |
| Runs continuously below 15°F with aux heat contributing — expected in deep cold | Air from the vents feels cold for long stretches, not just during brief defrost |
| Brief cool air from the vents every so often — a defrost cycle clearing ice | The outdoor unit stays iced over for hours, or ices up again right after defrosting |
| Louder, steadier operation during the coldest days of a cold snap | Your electric bill spikes far beyond your typical winter pattern with no weather explanation |

One row deserves emphasis: **runtime alone is not a symptom.** "Off" is what a struggling system does when it can't keep up — a heat pump that cycles briefly, blows tepid air, and never satisfies the thermostat is in worse shape than one that simply runs and runs while the house stays warm.

If any right-column pattern sounds like yours, skip to [When to call a professional](#when-to-call-a-professional).
## Why heat pumps run longer in cold weather

Heat pumps are most efficient in mild temperatures. When it is cold outside, they can still heat, but they need more runtime to deliver the same warmth.

Think of it like carrying buckets in the snow. You can still move water, but it takes longer. Each hour of cold weather, your home loses a certain amount of heat to the outdoors, and the heat pump has to replace exactly that much. Colder air means each pass moves less heat per minute, so the system runs longer to move the same total. That is why longer cycles can be normal — and why the same system that cycles on and off in October runs nearly continuously in January.

If you want the efficiency basics, see [HVAC efficiency ratings explained](/blog/afue-vs-seer-vs-hspf-vs-cop/).

## The balance point: where the heat pump needs help

Every heat pump has a **balance point**: the outdoor temperature where the heat it can deliver exactly matches the heat your home is losing. Above that temperature, the heat pump keeps up on its own. Below it, the heat pump literally cannot move heat indoors fast enough to replace what's escaping, no matter how long it runs — and the thermostat brings in backup (aux) heat to close the gap.

This is the concept that explains most "it's running all day" panic:

- On the normal side: a heat pump running continuously near its balance point is doing exactly what it was designed to do. The runtime is long because the margin between delivered heat and heat loss has narrowed, not because anything is broken.
- On the symptom side: if the system can't reach setpoint even with aux heat contributing — or aux heat seems to run constantly rather than just during the coldest hours — the balance point may sit above where it should, often because of sizing, duct losses, or a heat-loss problem the building owner can actually fix.

For gas-fired homes there's a second meaning of balance point: on a dual-fuel setup it's the temperature where the thermostat switches from heat pump to furnace. How to set it — and why your local electric-to-gas price ratio decides it — is covered in [our heat pump vs gas furnace guide](/blog/48-heat-pump-vs-gas-furnace-savings/).

## Defrost cycles and cool air

In freezing weather, moisture in the outdoor air freezes on the outdoor coil. Ice on the coil blocks airflow and stops the heat-transfer process, so the system periodically enters a **defrost cycle** to clear it.

Here's the part that startles people: to melt that ice, the system briefly reverses operation — it runs in cooling mode indoors and pushes heat to the outdoor coil. During those few minutes, the air from your vents can feel cool, and the outdoor fan stops while steam or mist rises off the unit.

That brief cool air is normal. A few signals distinguish routine defrost from trouble:

| Normal defrost | Not defrost |
|---|---|
| Cool vents for roughly 5–15 minutes, occasionally | Cold air for an hour or more |
| Steam or mist off the outdoor unit, then it clears | The outdoor unit stays iced over for hours |
| Happens every hour or two in freezing weather | Happens constantly, in mild weather too |

If it happens constantly — especially in weather where the coil shouldn't be icing — it may point to a low refrigerant charge, a stuck defrost sensor, or an airflow problem, not the weather.

## Aux heat and emergency heat

Many heat pumps have auxiliary heat that kicks in when outdoor temperatures drop below the balance point or when the system needs help catching up — after a big setback, for instance, or during a cold snap.

Two things matter for your runtime question. First, aux heat running briefly during the coldest weather is expected and doesn't mean the heat pump is failing. Second, aux heat is typically electric resistance heat, which costs noticeably more per hour to run than the heat pump itself — so if it runs constantly, your bill will say so before anything else does.

If you see "AUX" or "EMER" on your thermostat often, learn what those modes mean here: [aux heat vs emergency heat](/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/).

## Airflow and filter checks

Restricted airflow makes any heating system work harder. A clogged filter or blocked vent can force longer runtimes and weaker warmth.

- Replace filters on schedule — check monthly during heavy-use months, minimum every 3 months.
- Keep return vents clear of furniture, curtains, and dust.
- Make sure supply vents are open.

If runtime got longer gradually over a season, do these three checks before anything else — they're the cheapest fixes and the most common cause.

## Heat loss and duct leaks

If your home loses heat quickly, the heat pump will run more often to keep up. Air leaks, thin insulation, and leaky ducts are common causes. This guide helps you spot duct issues: [duct leaks symptoms](/blog/duct-leaks-symptoms-hvac-running-all-day/).

Duct losses are also why some homes skip ductwork at replacement time — see [ductless mini-split vs. central air](/blog/ductless-mini-split-vs-central-air/) for how a zoned system changes the runtime equation.

## When to call a professional

Work down this list; if you reach the bottom without an explanation, book a technician:

1. **Filter and vents** — replace the filter, open every supply, clear every return. (Free, 10 minutes.)
2. **The verdict table** — if you match *any* right-column pattern, don't wait for it to become an emergency.
3. **The defrost test** — watch one full defrost cycle. Cool vents for minutes with steam off the unit is normal; an outdoor unit that stays iced solid for hours is not.
4. **Aux heat frequency** — if "AUX" shows daily in weather you know the system used to handle on its own, that's a capacity or refrigerant question, not a thermostat question.
5. **Call a pro when**: the home never reaches setpoint on a mild day, the outdoor unit ices repeatedly right after defrosting, the breaker trips during heating, the air stays cold through an entire cycle, or your winter electric use jumps far past previous winters with similar weather. Refrigerant charge, defrost controls, and reversing valves are not DIY territory.

One more flag: a system that suddenly starts running *differently* — new noises, new cycling pattern, new cold-air stretches — deserves a call even if each symptom alone looks marginal. Change is the symptom.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Heat pumps should cycle like a furnace."** They often run longer by design — steady long runs are how they hold temperature efficiently.
- **"Long runtime means it is failing."** Not always. In cold weather it can be the system doing its job.
- **"Defrost means the system is broken."** Defrost is a normal winter function; constant defrost is not.
- **"Aux heat appearing means I need a new heat pump."** Aux heat below the balance point is by design. Aux heat running constantly in mild weather is the thing to investigate.

If your system turns on and off too quickly, the opposite pattern is its own issue: [AC short cycling explained](/blog/13-ac-short-cycling-causes-fixes/).

## FAQs

Quick answers to common heat pump runtime questions.

{{< faq "Is it normal for a heat pump to run all day in winter?" >}}

It can be normal during very cold weather. Heat pumps often run longer to maintain the set temperature. The concern trigger is not the runtime itself — it's a home that loses ground to the cold, or aux heat that never stops.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why does my heat pump blow cool air sometimes?" >}}

During defrost cycles, the system briefly reverses to melt ice off the outdoor coil and can send cooler air indoors for a few minutes. Cold air for hours is a different problem.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What does AUX heat mean?" >}}

AUX heat is backup heat — usually electric resistance — that helps the heat pump when outdoor temperatures drop below the balance point or the system needs to catch up quickly.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What is a heat pump balance point?" >}}

The outdoor temperature where the heat pump's maximum output exactly matches your home's heat loss. Below it, the heat pump alone can't keep up and backup heat fills the gap. Long runtimes near the balance point are normal.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "When should I be concerned about nonstop runtime?" >}}

If the home never reaches setpoint, vents blow cold for long stretches, the outdoor unit stays iced over, or bills spike beyond your normal winter pattern, it's time to check further.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can duct leaks make a heat pump run longer?" >}}

Yes. Leaky ducts waste heated air and force the system to run longer to maintain comfort — and they can push the effective balance point higher.

{{< /faq >}}
