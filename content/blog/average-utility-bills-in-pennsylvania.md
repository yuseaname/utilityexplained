## Quick Answer: What Utilities Cost in Pennsylvania {#quick-answer}

**Electricity:** the Pennsylvania residential rate averaged **21.77¢/kWh in 2026** (EIA preliminary year-to-date; 19.93¢ final for 2025) — above the 18.16¢/kWh national average, and rising faster than it. At the EIA national-standard 899 kWh month, that is **$196/month** for electricity, versus $163 nationally.

**Natural gas:** Pennsylvania residential gas averaged **$15.04/thousand cubic feet in 2025** (EIA annual) — about **$1.45/therm** after conversion, one of the cheaper gas states (Pennsylvania sits on the Marcellus shale). A standardized 75-therm heating month runs **$109**.

**Water & sewer:** there is **no reliable state average** — water is priced city-by-city or by authority in Pennsylvania. Nationally the combined bill runs about $70–$90/month; [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/) decodes yours.

Pennsylvania is one of the few states where you can shop **both** your electric and gas supply — and the PUC publishes the number you need to beat. That's the core of this page.

## Price-to-compare, explained {#price-to-compare-explained}

Pennsylvania deregulated electric generation while keeping delivery regulated. Your bill splits into:

- **Delivery** — PPL Electric (central and northeast), PECO (Philadelphia area), Duquesne Light (Pittsburgh), and the FirstEnergy companies (Met-Ed, Penelec, Penn Power, West Penn) own the wires. Delivery rates are set by the PUC and you cannot shop them.
- **Generation/supply** — the shoppable part. If you do nothing, your utility buys default supply for you and passes the cost through at a state-published rate: your **price to compare**.

The price-to-compare is the single most useful number on a Pennsylvania bill, because every competitive offer is only meaningful against it. The official comparison tool is the PUC's [papowerswitch.com](https://www.papowerswitch.com) — offers are listed with term, rate type, and renewable content, filterable against your utility's current default.

How to use it without getting burned:

1. **Find your current price to compare** — it's printed on your bill (usually in the supply/generation section) and on the shopping site for your utility.
2. **Compare like with like** — a supplier's fixed ¢/kWh against your default ¢/kWh, at your annual usage. A 1¢/kWh saving at ~10,800 kWh/year is roughly $108/year.
3. **Read the term and the fee** — enrollment is free and delivery is unaffected; the catch is contract length, early-exit fees, and whether a low teaser rate is variable after month one.
4. **Dropping a supplier is easy** — you can return to default supply at any time; the utility remains your provider of last resort. No interruption of service occurs during any switch.

## Fixed vs variable offers {#fixed-vs-variable-offers}

Pennsylvania's supplier market offers the full menu — and the same trap Texas teaches:

- **Fixed-rate, fixed-term** — the rate holds for the contract (commonly 6–24 months). This is the product to compare against your price to compare. The risk is timing: lock when the default is low and a later default drop can leave you above it — but at least you know your number for the term.
- **Variable/month-to-month** — floats with the supplier's pricing. Occasionally useful for a short stay in a rental, dangerous as a permanent arrangement; introductory rates frequently step up after the first month or two.
- **Green/renewable-content offers** — same comparison logic, different generation mix. Verify the renewable claim on the offer sheet if that's the reason you'd pick it.

A Pennsylvania-specific note: default-supply prices change on a schedule (commonly twice a year in most territories, on set dates). Mark your utility's default-price adjustment months — right after an adjustment is when shopping comparisons are freshest, and when a previously good fixed contract may have turned expensive.

## Gas shopping and the heating season {#gas-shopping-and-the-heating-season}

Pennsylvania is also a gas-shopping state: where you have natural gas service (and your gas utility participates), you can buy the gas commodity from a licensed supplier while the utility delivers it. The same price-to-compare logic applies to gas — your utility publishes a default gas cost rate, and the PUC lists gas offers on its shopping site.

The stakes are higher here than the electric side, for one reason: **winter**. A gas-heated Pennsylvania house burns the large majority of its annual gas between November and March — [why gas bills spike in winter](/blog/gas-bill-too-high/) — so a supply rate that's 20¢/therm above default costs real money across exactly the months you can't avoid. Pennsylvania's $1.45/therm average (2025 EIA) is already among the nation's cheapest; a bad supplier choice can quietly erase that home-state advantage.

## Water and sewer in Pennsylvania {#water-and-sewer-in-pennsylvania}

Water and sewer are set by your city, borough, or water/sewer authority — two neighbors across a municipal line can pay very different rates for the same usage. Pennsylvania-specific wrinkles:

1. **Authorities, not utilities.** Much of the state is served by independent municipal authorities whose rate cases don't run through the PUC — rate changes hit harder and faster when infrastructure bills come due.
2. **Stormwater fees.** A growing set of municipalities (Philadelphia's is the best-known) bill a separate stormwater charge based on impervious surface — a line item homeowners don't expect. [Hidden fees on your utility bill](/blog/hidden-fees-utility-bill/) covers the general pattern.
3. **Decode the units first.** Most Pennsylvania water bills measure in CCF (hundred cubic feet) or gallons: [what CCF means on a water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/).
4. **Benchmark usage and check fixed charges.** A typical household runs about 2–4 CCF per person per month; well above that, a leak is the usual suspect ([the 10-minute meter check](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/)). And many systems have raised base charges faster than usage rates — your bill can climb even with flat usage. [Why your water bill is high](/blog/water-bill-too-high/) walks the whole statement.

## If your bill runs above these numbers {#if-your-bill-runs-above-these-numbers}

A $196 standardized month assumes 899 kWh at the blended state rate. Real Pennsylvania bills run above it mostly for three reasons: winter electric heating or heat-pump backup strips, a supply contract that's drifted above the current default, and an aging refrigerator or well pump running constantly.

{{< faq "What is the price to compare on my Pennsylvania electric bill?">}}

It's your utility's current default supply rate — the generation cost you pay if you never shop. Every competitive supplier offer in Pennsylvania should be compared against it (printed on your bill, or via the PUC's papowerswitch.com for your utility). If a fixed offer beats it at your annual usage after fees, switching saves money; if not, staying on default supply is the right call, and returning to it is always allowed.

{{< faq "Why is gas so cheap in Pennsylvania compared to other states?">}}

Pennsylvania sits on the Marcellus and Utica shale plays — much of the nation's natural gas is produced in or near the state, which trims the transportation cost built into residential prices. The 2025 EIA average of $15.04/Mcf (about $1.45/therm) is well below the ~$1.92/therm national snapshot. Delivery charges and the utility's commodity rate still set your final price — which is why comparing gas supplier offers against your utility's default matters more than the state average.
