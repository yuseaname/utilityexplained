## Quick Answer

**A break in the water main under the street is the utility's pipe and the utility's repair — you report it, you do not pay for it. Your responsibility starts at your own service line: the pipe that branches off the main, runs under your yard, and feeds your house.** Utilities across the country draw the line in slightly different places — Denver Water puts property-owner responsibility from the tap at the main through the meter pit[1]; Toronto splits at the property line[2]; Columbus uses the curb box[3] — but the shape is the same. A main break will not run water through your meter, so it does not directly raise your bill; the follow-on issues (a boil-water advisory, brown water, or a freeze-broken service line on your side) are where the money questions live. For the full boundary map and the meter test that settles "which side is it on," see [who is responsible for a water leak before or after the meter](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/).

## Whose pipe is it: the three-way split {#boundary}

{{< visual src="/images/articles/water-main-break-who-pays-what-to-do/boundary-diagram.webp" alt="Cross-section showing the utility water main under the street, the service line branching off through the yard, and the shut-off valve and meter at the boundary before the house." wide="true" >}}


A water system reaches your kitchen faucet through three pieces:

1. **The main** — the larger pipe under the street or easement, owned and repaired by the water utility. A break here is their crew, their cost, their emergency line.
2. **Your service line** — the smaller pipe from the main to your house. This one is yours in most US utilities, including the whole yard stretch that surprises homeowners[1][5]. Henderson, NV states it plainly: the homeowner maintains the water service lateral from the connection at the City's water meter, and pays all costs for breaks on that side[5].
3. **Everything inside the house** — yours, plumbers' territory.

The exact dividing point varies by utility — at the main tap, at the meter or curb box, or at the property line[1][2][3] — but the pattern holds: street side is theirs, yard side is yours. When you report a suspected break, a utility crew determines which side the leak is on; Columbus notifies the property owner with a door hanger when the repair is the owner's responsibility, with a required repair window and a licensed-contractor requirement[3].

## What a main break looks like from your driveway {#signs}

{{< visual src="/images/articles/water-main-break-who-pays-what-to-do/discolored-water.webp" alt="Two glasses of water compared: clear water beside brown sediment-stirred water typical after a main break." wide="true" >}}


You rarely see the pipe. You see the symptoms:

- **Water surfacing where it should not be** — bubbling or ponding in the street, a sunken or heaved patch of pavement, or an unexplained wet strip in a yard during dry, freezing weather. Columbus's guidance: water spraying or ponding during periods of no rain or snowmelt may be a break — report it[3].
- **Pressure drop or no water** — a sudden loss of pressure across the whole house (not one faucet) often means a main or service-line event upstream.
- **Discolored water** — brown or yellow water after pressure changes means the flow stirred up iron and sediment in the mains. It is usually not a health emergency, but it is the classic after-effect of a break or hydrant work.
- **In winter, the spike in breaks is real** — ground freeze pushes break rates up exactly when a service line under your own yard is also at risk. The freeze months are when both [water meter boxes fill with water](/blog/water-meter-box-full-of-water/) and service lines split.

## What to do, in order {#what-to-do}

1. **Report it.** Call the utility's emergency or 24-hour line — main breaks are treated as priority emergencies, and utilities ask for immediate reports[3]. Do not assume a neighbor already called.
2. **Protect your own house if the problem might be your side.** Know where your main shut-off valve is (typically on the foundation wall where the service enters, before the meter). Toronto's guidance: if your shut-off is not working, the city can turn water off at the property line — for a fee — while a plumber handles the private side[2].
3. **Follow the advisory.** After significant breaks or pressure loss, utilities may issue a boil-water advisory until tests clear. Use bottled or boiled water for drinking and cooking until the utility lifts it — their notice, not the water clearing visually, is the all-clear.
4. **Flush your lines when told to.** The standard post-break instruction is to run cold taps until the water clears, which moves stirred-up sediment out of the mains and your plumbing. Avoid running hot taps first, so sediment does not get drawn into the water heater.
5. **Document.** Photos of flooding, dates, utility reference numbers — useful for insurance and for any [bill adjustment conversation](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/) if your side of the pipe turns out to be involved.

## Your bill questions, answered straight {#bill}

**Will a main break raise my water bill? No.** Water from a main break never passes through your meter — the meter only records what flows into your house. You are not charged for the utility's lost water.

**But my usage looked weird after the break.** Pressure fluctuations can stir odd readings and estimated bills; compare the bill against [how to read your water meter](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/) directly. If the meter itself was damaged by freezing or the event, that is a utility conversation — in many utilities the meter belongs to the property owner but is maintained by the utility for normal wear[1].

**What if it was actually my service line that broke?** Then the water did run through your meter, and the bill impact is real — a burst line can lose water fast. Two mitigations exist. First, many utilities offer leak adjustments (credits for the lost volume once the leak is repaired); Portland, for example, lets customers apply for an adjustment after burst-pipe leaks and will also review sewer charges, because winter water use sets some households' year-round sewer billing[4]. Our [water bill leak adjustment guide](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/) walks through the paperwork. Second, if you let faucets drip to prevent freezing, that deliberate use is typically not adjustable[4] — the tradeoff was cheap insurance.

**Does homeowner's insurance cover a service-line break?** Sometimes, separately. Denver Water notes water damage may or may not be covered by a typical policy and directs homeowners to verify with their agent[1]; many insurers sell specific service-line coverage as an add-on. The pipe repair itself is often your cost either way.

## How utilities prioritize repairs {#repairs}

Breaks queue by severity, not by call order. Columbus repairs by a priority system weighing the leak's severity, location, and critical facilities served — hospitals and schools rank higher; busy-road repairs get scheduled for off-hours[3]. Translation: a gushing six-inch main on an arterial road gets a crew tonight; a weeping leak on a quiet cul-de-sac may wait days. Your role is to report accurately (location, whether water is surfacing, whether anyone is out of water) and to keep the report number.

## FAQ

{{< faq "Who do I call for a water main break?" >}}
Your water utility's emergency or 24-hour line — the number on your bill or utility website. Many cities also accept reports through 311. Call immediately for surfacing water, a pressure collapse, or flooding; crews determine which side of the boundary the leak is on[3].
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Is brown water after a main break safe?" >}}
Discolored water is usually stirred-up sediment — iron and manganese — rather than contamination, but the safe rule is to follow the utility's advisory. Run cold taps until the water clears, and use boiled or bottled water for drinking if a boil-water advisory is in effect. The utility's test results, not the color, end the advisory.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Am I responsible for the pipe under the street?" >}}
No. The main under the street belongs to the utility, along with the repairs[1][2][3]. Your responsibility starts at your service line — check your specific utility's boundary (main tap, meter, curb box, or property line), because the exact handoff point varies.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Can I get a bill credit after a water main break?" >}}
Not for the utility's break — that water never touched your meter, so there is nothing to credit. Credits apply when the leak was on your side: repair it, then request a leak adjustment, and ask whether sewer charges can be reviewed too[4]. See the [leak adjustment walkthrough](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why do so many mains break in winter?" >}}
Cold drives both failure modes: ground freeze shifts and loads aging pipe, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles flex joints. The same cold threatens your service line and meter set — which is why [letting faucets drip in hard freezes](/blog/how-much-does-dripping-faucet-add-to-water-bill/) and knowing your shut-off valve location are standard winter prep.
{{< /faq >}}
