## Quick Answer

**The fastest water-bill savings are free: find and fix leaks, run full loads, and stop outdoor waste.** The average family spends about $1,300 per year on water, but EPA WaterSense estimates that upgrading to WaterSense-labeled fixtures and ENERGY STAR certified appliances can save an average of $500 per year.[1] Leak repairs alone can save a family of four up to $200 annually.

Work through the actions below in order — free behaviors first, then low-cost repairs, then upgrades with the best payback.

### Level 2: Low-Cost Leak Repairs ($5–$50)

Leaks are the most common driver of unexpectedly high water bills. Household leaks average about 9,400 gallons a year (EPA WaterSense) and can run far higher in leak-prone homes, costing $50–$200+ annually — the [high-water-bill diagnostic](/blog/06-water-bill-too-high/) shows how to find them.

#### The 10-Minute Leak Check

1. **Read your water meter.** Record the reading. Don’t use any water for 2 hours. Read again. If the number changed, you have a leak.
2. **Check toilets.** Drop a dye tablet (or food coloring) into the tank. If color appears in the bowl without flushing, the flapper is leaking. Replacing a flapper costs $5–$15 and saves up to $50/year per toilet.
3. **Check faucets.** A drip at 1 drop/second wastes 3,000 gallons per year. An o-ring or washer replacement costs under $5.
4. **Check outdoor spigots.** A single outdoor leak can waste 10+ gallons/hour.

| Action | Cost | Typical Savings | Payback Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toilet flapper replacement | $8–$15 | $30–$50/yr | <6 months |
| Faucet washer/o-ring | $1–$5 | $10–$30/yr | Instant |
| Outdoor spigot washer | $3–$8 | $10–$100/yr | <2 months |
| Hose bib replacement | $15–$30 | $20–$80/yr | <6 months |
|---|
| **Total low-cost fix range** | **$5–$50** | **$30–$200/yr** | |

**Source:** EPA WaterSense “Fix a Leak Week” and “Identifying Household Leaks” (drop-test method, dye tablet guidance, and leak-cost calculator available at epa.gov/watersense/fixaleak).[1]

### Level 5: Full-Load Disciplines (Cost: $0)

These are behavioral adjustments that cost nothing but require consistency.

- **Dishwasher:** Always run full. Modern dishwashers use 4–6 gallons per load. Hand washing the same load uses 20–27 gallons under a running tap.
- **Clothes washer:** Set for full loads. A top-load machine uses 40 gallons regardless of load size. A full load uses that water efficiently; a half load wastes the same amount.
- **Washing machine:** Use cold water. Switching from hot to cold saves energy (not water), reducing your water-heating bill up to $30–$50/year (DOE estimates water heating at ~20% of home energy bills).[2]

## What to Do If You’ve Tried Everything and the Bill Is Still High

If you’ve worked through the actions above and your usage hasn’t dropped:

1. **Check the billing period length.** A 33-day billing period will always show more usage than a 28-day one. Compare daily consumption (usage ÷ days). See [billing cycles explained](/blog/23-utility-billing-cycle-explained/).
2. **Check for a meter error.** Read your meter yourself and compare it to the billed reading. Report a discrepancy to your utility.
3. **Check rate schedule changes.** Did the utility raise rates mid-cycle? Look for a notice on your bill.
4. **Look for a sewer charge mismatch.** If your sewer is based on summer consumption without winter averaging, switching to winter-averaged billing (where available) could lower your sewer charge. See [sewer charges](/blog/how-sewer-charges-work-on-your-water-bill/).
5. **Call the utility.** Ask for a bill review or a usage audit. Some utilities offer free home water audits.
