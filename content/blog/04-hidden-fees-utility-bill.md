## Quick Answer

**A quoted energy price is not the same as the final amount due.** A utility statement can include usage-based charges, customer or service charges, delivery-related charges, taxes, riders, credits, and one-time event fees. The only reliable way to understand a charge is to read the description on your bill, the current tariff or rate schedule that applies to your account, and the local or state rule that authorizes the charge.

This page is the launching pad for the site's fee-explainers. Use the table below to identify what a charge is, then follow the linked explainer for the full review process, the questions to ask your provider, and the documents to keep.

## A Bill Review Process

1. Identify the billing period and total usage.
2. Separate usage-based charges from fixed charges, taxes, riders, credits, and one-time items.
3. Compare the same categories with a similar prior billing period.
4. Read notices, footnotes, and the rate-plan name on the statement.
5. Ask the utility for the tariff, rate schedule, or customer-service explanation for an item you do not understand.

## The Charges You May See

{{< visual src="/images/articles/04-hidden-fees-utility-bill/fee-taxonomy-tree.webp" alt="Utility fee taxonomy tree separating usage-based, fixed, and event-driven charges." wide="true" >}}

The table below lists seven categories of charges that appear across U.S. residential electricity, gas, and water bills. Each row links to the site's full explainer for that charge. Amounts and structures vary by provider, state, service class, and rate plan — the table summarizes what published utility sources describe (DOE rate-design material, Con Edison's bill guide, and CFPB consumer guidance) and tells you what to look for on your own statement.

| Charge | What it is | How it typically appears | How to spot it on your bill | Read more |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Customer charge** (a.k.a. basic service charge, service charge, monthly minimum) | A fixed monthly amount the utility bills for being connected to the system, regardless of how much energy or water you used. DOE rate-design material describes residential rates as historically built around a fixed customer charge plus a volumetric rate; the amount, label, and applicability are set in the utility's tariff.[^1] | Flat dollar amount billed every cycle, even on near-zero usage months. | A line that does not vary with kWh, therms, CCF, or gallons — and is on the bill whether or not you used the service. | [Customer charge explainer](/blog/17-what-is-customer-charge-on-utility-bill/) |
| **Transmission / delivery rider** (a.k.a. transmission surcharge, distribution charge, delivery service charge) | A usage-based charge that covers the wires, pipes, substations, and the people who maintain them. Con Edison describes delivery as the part of the bill that funds the infrastructure; "customers who use more pay more of these shared costs."[^3] | A cents-per-kWh (or per-therm, per-CCF) line multiplied by your usage. May be split into "transmission" and "distribution" sub-rows on restructured bills. | Look for "delivery," "distribution," "transmission," "grid charge," "wires charge," or your utility's equivalent. Multiply the rate by your usage and confirm against the line total. | [Delivery vs supply charges](/blog/24-delivery-charge-vs-supply-charge-utility-bill/) |
| **Fuel / energy adjustment charge** (a.k.a. fuel cost recovery, energy cost adjustment, purchased-gas adjustment) | A usage-based charge that passes through the provider's wholesale fuel or energy cost for the period. DOE electricity-bill material notes that consumption charges can appear under different names and are billed at a rate determined by a contract.[^2] | Cents-per-kWh (or per-therm) line that changes month-to-month as the underlying fuel or wholesale energy price changes. | Look for "fuel adjustment," "fuel cost recovery," "energy cost adjustment," "purchased gas adjustment," or "wholesale supply." Compare this month's rate with last month's — a jump is normal; a structural change is not. | [Fuel adjustment explainer](/blog/18-fuel-adjustment-charge-on-utility-bill-explained/) |
| **Surcharges, riders, and policy riders** (environmental, renewable portfolio, energy efficiency, public benefits, system benefits charge) | Account-specific add-ons whose meaning, amount, purpose, and effective dates are defined in the tariff. DOE electricity-bill material notes that bills can be reproduced using the applicable rate structure and measured energy use; riders are one component in that structure.[^2] | Varies — can be a flat per-bill amount, a cents-per-kWh rate, or a percentage of another charge. | Look for "rider," "surcharge," "environmental," "renewable," "energy efficiency," "public benefits," or "policy adjustment" — and read the tariff section that defines each one. | [Surcharges and riders explainer](/blog/19-utility-bill-surcharges-and-riders-explained/) · [Taxes, fees, and franchise charges](/blog/25-utility-bill-taxes-fees-franchise-charges-explained/) |
| **Taxes and franchise / local-government charges** | Federal, state, and local taxes on utility service; franchise fees paid to a city or county for the right to operate in that jurisdiction; gross-receipts taxes. Their names, rates, and legal basis vary by jurisdiction.[^4] | Either a percentage of another line (subtotal, delivery, or full bill) or a flat per-bill amount, depending on the local rule. May be lumped into a single "taxes" line or split into "state tax," "city tax," "franchise fee," "public utility tax." | Identify the jurisdiction by ZIP code or service address; ask the utility which tax authority each line pays. Con Edison documents that local property taxes on energy infrastructure total more than $3.2 billion for customers in 2026, and notes that most of this is embedded in delivery rather than listed separately.[^3] | [Taxes, fees, and franchise charges](/blog/25-utility-bill-taxes-fees-franchise-charges-explained/) |
| **Minimum bill / minimum charge** | A floor below which the bill will not fall, even at zero usage. May be the same as the customer charge, or it may be a higher floor that also includes some usage.[^1] | When your usage × rate would compute to less than the minimum, the bill shows the minimum instead. | Look for "minimum charge," "minimum bill," "monthly floor," or any line that does not move down with usage. Compare with the [customer charge explainer](/blog/17-what-is-customer-charge-on-utility-bill/) to determine whether the two are the same item on your account. | [Minimum bill explainer](/blog/22-minimum-bill-utility-bill-explained/) |
| **One-time / event-driven fees** (late fee, deposit, reconnection, service-start / connection) | Charges tied to a specific account event — paying past the due date, opening an account, restoring service after disconnection, or starting service at a new address. The amount, refundability, and conditions are defined in the provider's tariff and account terms, not by a national rule.[^5] | Usually a single line item with a one-time dollar amount. May appear separately from monthly charges or be grouped under "account activity," "other charges," or "adjustments." | Look for "late fee," "delinquent charge," "deposit," "connection fee," "service start," "reconnection," or "restoration." Confirm each in writing against the provider's tariff before paying. | [Service-start / connection](/blog/27-utility-connection-fee-explained/) · [Reconnection fee](/blog/28-utility-reconnection-fee-explained/) · [Past-due balance](/blog/21-past-due-balance-utility-bill-explained/) |

[^1]: U.S. Department of Energy, *Rate Design Primer* (PDF). Notes that residential rates have historically included a fixed customer charge plus a volumetric component, but does not establish a national amount or rule.
[^2]: U.S. Department of Energy, *Understanding Your Electricity Bills* (PDF) and *Understanding Your Utility Bills — Electricity* (PDF). Notes that bills can be reproduced using the applicable rate structure and measured energy use and demand.
[^3]: Con Edison, *How to Read Your Bill*. Notes that supply and delivery charges are separate categories; that "customers who use more pay more of these shared costs" of delivery; and that local property taxes on energy infrastructure make up nearly a quarter of the delivery charge.
[^4]: Con Edison, *Market Supply Charge*. Documents supply charges as separate, per-kWh line items that vary by zone and service class.
[^5]: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, *Get help paying rent and bills*. Notes that reconnection, deposit, and service-start charges are provider- and account-specific, and directs consumers with payment difficulty to local and state resources including LIHEAP.

## Why This Page Does Not Give You a National Price Range

Every fee page on this site repeats the same caveat: the amount, label, calculation, and applicability of any charge depend on the **serving provider's tariff, the service class on your account, and the state or local rules that apply**.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5] Two customers on different utilities — or even two customers on different rate plans at the same utility — can see the same charge name at very different amounts. A "customer charge" of $8 in one territory and $24 in another is normal, not an error; what looks like a wrong number may be the right number for the wrong reason, or the wrong number under the wrong rule.

This is also why the table above describes the **structure** of each charge (flat dollar, per-kWh, percentage of subtotal, one-time event) rather than a dollar range. Confirm the amount on **your** bill against **your** utility's current tariff — not against an article on the internet.

## How to Use the Linked Explainers

For each charge you do not understand, the linked page gives you the same workflow used across this site:

1. Record the line as printed on the statement, including the tariff or rate-schedule reference.
2. Find the tariff, rate schedule, or local rule that defines the charge.
3. Confirm the calculation: fixed, usage-based, demand-based, percentage, credit, or one-time.
4. Compare with a similar prior billing period to isolate what changed.
5. If the line does not reconcile, request the account-specific calculation from the provider before assuming the charge is wrong.

## If You Cannot Pay a Bill

Contact the utility promptly and ask about payment arrangements, assistance programs, and any available protections. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau directs consumers seeking year-round utility-bill help to their local LIHEAP office or the National Energy Assistance Referral Hotline.[^5]
