## First, What a Faucet Aerator Actually Is

An aerator is the little mesh-and-baffle cartridge screwed into the tip of your faucet spout. Its original job is to mix air into the water stream — that is why a modern faucet pours a smooth, bead-free column instead of a chaotic splash — but that same air does something useful for your bill: it means the faucet delivers a satisfying stream at a much lower actual gallon-per-minute (GPM) flow than the bare pipe would.

Here is the part that matters for savings: the federal plumbing standard caps bathroom faucets at **2.2 gallons per minute**, and most faucets you grew up with run right at that number even when you do not need the full blast.[1] Brushing your teeth, rinsing your hands, shaving, filling a glass — none of those need 2.2 GPM, yet the faucet happily pours it every second the tap is open. A WaterSense-labeled faucet or aerator cuts that to **1.5 GPM or less** — a 30% or better reduction from the standard 2.2 GPM — and EPA certified those aerators specifically so the flow still feels normal for handwashing and shaving.[1] Aerators that earn the WaterSense label are certified for both flow and performance, so you are not buying a dribble in exchange for a lower number.

What makes aerators the sleeper deal of the water world is not the percentage — it is the price and the effort. EPA notes a WaterSense aerator "can cost as little as a few dollars,"[1] and replacing one is a twist, not a plumbing project. That is the cheapest per-gallon-saved upgrade that exists. A water heater replacement is hundreds of dollars; a new toilet is a few hundred plus labor; an aerator is the price of a coffee and requires exactly one wrench you can borrow from a drawer.

## What to Look For Before You Buy

Two specs decide whether an aerator will actually help you, and both are usually printed on the box or listing.

**The GPM number.** This is the flow at full open. Lower is more water-efficient but can feel weak if you actually fill pots from the tap. For a bathroom sink, 1.0–1.5 GPM is the sweet spot; for a kitchen where you fill tall pots and rinse big dishes, you want the flexibility to get more flow on demand rather than a permanent trickle.

**Thread size and direction.** Aerators come in a few thread standards (most commonly **15/16-inch** for kitchen spouts and **55/64-inch** for bathroom spouts), and some faucets use male threads where you might expect female. This is the single most common reason an aerator "doesn't fit" — the adapter kit matters as much as the flow rate. A good listing either includes the adapter or tells you which standard the unit is built for.

The reason an **adjustable aerator** is worth hunting for is that it lets you escape the fixed-flow trade-off entirely. A fixed 1.0 GPM aerator is right for a bathroom but annoying in a kitchen; a fixed 1.5 GPM aerator saves less than it could in a bathroom. A dial solves both at once — and the Tri-Max below is the honest, well-reviewed way to get that dial without paying for it.

## The Math: What 2.2 GPM Down to 1.0 GPM Is Actually Worth

Let us put real numbers on it, with the assumptions stated up front so you can redo it for your own house. We will use a combined **water-plus-sewer rate of roughly $0.01 per gallon** (about $10 per 1,000 gallons), which is a common national average when you fold sewer charges in with water — EPA's figures put a typical family-of-four water and sewer spend around $1,000 a year, which works out to that same ballpark per gallon. Your utility's tiered rate may differ; that is exactly why [tiered water rates](/blog/tiered-water-rates-explained/) are worth a read first.

Start with one bathroom faucet. Assume a household uses that sink for about **4 minutes a day** combined (handwashing, teeth, shaving) across everyone:

- Old flow: 2.2 GPM × 4 min = **8.8 gallons/day**
- Tri-Max at 1.0 GPM: 1.0 GPM × 4 min = **4.0 gallons/day**
- Saved per faucet: 8.8 − 4.0 = **4.8 gallons/day**
- Per year: 4.8 × 365 = **1,752 gallons/year**
- In dollars: 1,752 × $0.01 = **~$17.50/year** for one bathroom faucet

Now scale it to a normal house. Two bathroom sinks plus a kitchen sink, each cut by roughly the same proportion:

- 3 faucets × 1,752 gallons ≈ **5,256 gallons/year**
- At $0.01/gal: **~$52/year**, for about $17–26 of aerators (two Tri-Maxes and the kitchen swivel)

Payback lands in **about four months**, and the units themselves are near-indestructible — aerators are not a consumable you replace annually. EPA's own national figure is more conservative (the average family saves about **700 gallons/year** from swapping to a standard WaterSense-labeled faucet or aerator),[1] and that is the right anchor for a typical 1.5 GPM swap. The 1.0 GPM dial on the Tri-Max does better than that because it lets you actually live at the lower flow. An aerator is a force multiplier on those habits, not a replacement for them. Buy the dial if you want the control; build the habit if you want the whole number.
