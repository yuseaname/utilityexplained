## Quick Answer

The three strongest duct-leak signs: **(1) uneven room temperatures**, **(2) weak airflow at registers far from the furnace**, and **(3) runtime and utility bills climbing while your thermostat settings never change**.

Together they deserve a closer look — but they do not *prove* a duct problem. Equipment sizing, airflow balance, insulation, solar gain, and control settings can all mimic leakage. The reliable confirmation is a professional duct-leakage test, not a guess.[1]

## Symptom List: What Duct Leaks Actually Look Like

Eight signs most often reported when ducts leak, each with its one-line cause:

1. **Uneven room temperatures** — Supply air escapes through leaks before reaching distant registers, so some rooms never catch up.
2. **Weak airflow at far registers** — Low pressure at the end of a leaky run means less air arrives where you need it.
3. **Dust streaks at register edges and joints** — Leaking air pulls dust through gaps and deposits it around the opening.
4. **Runtime and bills climbing at the same thermostat settings** — The system works longer to deliver the same conditioned air because part of it is lost before it reaches the room.
5. **Stuffy rooms that never settle** — Leaky returns rob the system of balanced airflow, so fresh-air exchange stalls.
6. **Visible kinks or tears in accessible attic flex runs** — Flex duct that is crushed, pinched, or torn leaks at every break.
7. **Whistling at return grilles** — High velocity through an undersized or restricted return path makes noise and signals pressure problems.
8. **Rooms that never reach setpoint in extreme weather** — When outdoor loads peak, even small supply losses become obvious.

## 15-Minute DIY Observation Checklist

Check the safe, visible parts of your duct system with no tools beyond a flashlight. Do **not** enter a crawlspace or an attic beyond a clear walkway — that is a professional job.

- **Registers:** Remove one vent cover. Look for dust streaks or daylight around the boot where it meets the floor or ceiling.
- **Filter:** Inspect the filter. A dirty or incorrectly sized filter makes every duct problem worse.
- **Visible return flex:** Look at the return duct in a closet or utility room. Squeeze gently — if crushed or disconnected, that is a clear defect.
- **Attic runs from the walkway only:** Shine a flashlight along accessible flex runs. Note kinks, tears, or disconnected collars. Do **not** step off the walkway onto ceiling joists you cannot see.
- **Safety:** Never touch electrical equipment, and do not disturb insulation beyond what you can see from the walkway. If anything looks unsafe, stop and call a professional. This is observation only — do not attempt to seal or modify ducts in hard-to-reach spaces.

## What a Professional Measures: Duct Blaster and Blower Door

A qualified energy rater or HVAC contractor uses a **duct blaster** — a calibrated fan that pressurizes the duct system — to measure leakage. The key number is **CFM25**: the cubic feet per minute that leaks when the system is held at 25 Pascals. Lower CFM25 means tighter ducts. DOE's Building Science Education material describes duct leakage testing as a way to measure leakage in HVAC duct systems, distinguishing total leakage from leakage to the outdoors.[1]

A **blower door** test pressurizes the whole house. Combined with the duct blaster, it tells the rater whether leaks dump conditioned air into the attic or crawlspace versus leaking between rooms.[1] Those two numbers — total leakage and leakage to outdoors — are what a pro uses to decide whether sealing is needed and where.

**The published statistic worth knowing:** ENERGY STAR states that "in a typical house, about 20 to 30 percent of the air that moves through the duct system is lost due to leaks, holes, and poorly connected ducts," which can cut heating and cooling efficiency by as much as 20%.[2]

## Sealing Options: What Works and What Fails

For accessible leaks, the durable fixes are **mastic** (a paint-on paste that hardens) and **metal tape** rated for HVAC use.

**Ordinary cloth duct tape fails.** ENERGY STAR's rule is direct: "seal with mastic sealant or metal tape — not duct tape" because cloth duct tape does not last.[2] If a pro proposes ordinary duct tape, that is a red flag.

## Runtime Math Table — Illustrative Arithmetic, Not Utility Data

The table below is **labeled arithmetic only** — not a prediction of your bill.

| Supply air lost to leaks | Air delivered | Added runtime | Example: 12-hour day |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0% (baseline) | 100% | — | 12.0 hours |
| 15% | 85% | ~1.18× | 14.2 hours |
| 30% | 70% | ~1.43× | 17.1 hours |

*How to read it:* If 15% escapes, the system runs ~18% longer to deliver the same useful conditioning; at 30%, ~43% longer. This is simple division (100 ÷ 85 ≈ 1.18; 100 ÷ 70 ≈ 1.43), not a utility estimate.

## FAQ

{{< faq "Can I seal ducts myself?" >}}
Only the *visible, accessible* sections — for example, a disconnected boot in a basement or a torn flex run on an attic walkway — using mastic or metal tape, never ordinary duct tape. If you cannot see the duct safely, or if the system serves combustion appliances, leave it to a professional. Improper sealing can backdraft gas appliances.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How much does a duct test cost?" >}}
Published cost figures vary widely by region, house size, and contractor, so this site does not quote an unverified number. Expect the total to include the duct blaster test plus any sealing work the test reveals. Ask two or three local energy raters for quotes before committing.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Will sealing lower my bill?" >}}
Sealing leaks reduces wasted airflow, which usually shortens runtime — but the actual savings depend on how much was leaking and how the system is sized. A duct test tells you the starting point. If your runtime is climbing at the same thermostat settings, sealing is worth investigating; it is not a guaranteed bill cut.
{{< /faq >}}
