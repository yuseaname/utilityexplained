## Quick Answer

**Pipes don't freeze because a house is cold — they freeze because the water in them sits still in a pipe that heat can't reach, long enough for the temperature to drop below freezing.** Every fix on this page is one of two things: keep water moving, or get heat to the pipe. The American Red Cross playbook fits on a sticky note: keep the thermostat at **55°F or higher** (even when you're away), **open kitchen and bathroom cabinet doors** so warm room air reaches under-sink plumbing, and **let cold water drip** from the faucet fed by the pipe most at risk — moving water freezes far slower than standing water.

None of those requires the heating system to be working. That's the point of this page: the ranked ladder below works during a heating outage, in a vacant house, or in a crawl space the furnace has never touched — and every rung is cheaper than the average burst-pipe insurance claim, which IBHS puts at **more than $5,000 per incident**. For what a small drip actually costs on your bill, see [how much a dripping faucet adds to your water bill](/blog/how-much-does-dripping-faucet-add-to-water-bill/); for the catastrophe a burst can become on your water bill, start with [why is my water bill so high](/blog/06-water-bill-too-high/).

## Why pipes freeze (the 30-second physics)

{{< visual src="/images/articles/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/why-pipes-freeze.webp" alt="Diagram of a pipe in cross-section: flowing water on the left, an ice plug in the middle pushing outward against the wall, the pipe bulging and cracking between the plug and a closed tap on the right." wide="true" >}}

Water expands when it freezes — the only common liquid that does. Inside a pipe, that expansion doesn't push outward into open air; it pushes against the pipe wall between the ice plug and a closed faucet, and the pressure can reach thousands of pounds per square inch. The pipe doesn't usually burst where the ice is; it bursts downstream, between the ice and the tap. That's why a "frozen pipe" and a "burst pipe" are two events separated by luck and hours.

Three conditions line up before it happens:

1. **The pipe is exposed** — in a crawl space, on an outside wall, in an unheated garage, under a mobile home, or in a wall cavity with poor insulation.
2. **The water is still** — nobody has run a tap in hours (overnight, or a vacant house).
3. **Cold has hours to work** — sustained temperatures in the 20s°F or below, especially with wind, which strips heat from exposed runs fast.

Remove any one of the three and the pipe survives the night. The ladder below removes them in order of cost.

## The prevention ladder (ranked by cost, tonight-first)

### Rung 1 — Free, tonight

{{< visual src="/images/articles/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/ladder.webp" alt="The prevention ladder in three tiers: free tonight (drip the faucet, open cabinets), cheap this weekend (insulation sleeves and heat cable), and the upgrades (smart monitoring and shutoff)." wide="true" >}}

**Drip the right faucet.** Pick the faucet fed by the most at-risk pipe (outside wall, crawl space) and let cold water run at pencil-thin width. Moving water is harder to freeze, and an open tap gives any ice that does form somewhere to push. Cost: fractions of a cent per hour on your water bill — a pencil-width drip is roughly 40 gallons a day, on the order of the cost of one load of laundry. If you're on a municipal bill measured in CCF, [what CCF means on your water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/) shows the conversion.

**Open the cabinets.** Kitchen and bathroom base cabinets trap cold air around plumbing that shares a wall with the outdoors. Opening them lets 68°F room air do what the missing insulation should.

**Keep the thermostat at 55°F minimum, day and night.** The Red Cross rule, and the one people break for a $2 setback saving. If you're away for the season, 55°F is the floor, not the suggestion.

**Shut and drain outdoor supply.** Close the indoor shutoff valve feeding outdoor faucets, then open the spigot outside to drain the line. A spigot that holds no water cannot burst. (Faucet covers help — we cover them below — but draining beats covering.)

### Rung 2 — Cheap, this weekend ($10–$50)

{{< visual src="/images/articles/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/heat-tape.webp" alt="A crawl-space pipe wrapped in foam insulation with a self-regulating heat cable spiraled along its length, plugged into a grounded outlet." >}}

**Foam pipe insulation sleeves** on every accessible exposed run — crawl space, basement rim joists, garage. Pre-slit polyethylene tubes, a knife to cut, no tools to speak of. This is the highest-value $30 in home plumbing.

**Heat tape or cable on the chronic problem pipe.** Every house has one run that freezes no matter what — the well line, the crawl-space feed, the line under the addition. Self-regulating heat cable is the targeted fix: it draws power only as the pipe cools, so it's cheap to run, and it keeps a pipe above freezing even when the space around it is well below. Run cost math: a 5W/ft cable on a 20-foot run is 100W when fully on — about 2 cents an hour at the US average rate, and self-regulating cable is rarely fully on. (For the full watts-to-dollars method, see [space heater running cost](/blog/space-heater-running-cost/) — same formula.)

**Insulate the crawl-space or garage wall, not just the pipe.** A pipe sleeve protects inches; a bale of fiberglass or a rigid-foam windbreak protects the whole space. If the cavity is truly unheatable, the goal is to slow heat loss, not to heat it.

## If the pipe is already frozen (do this, not that)

{{< visual src="/images/articles/how-to-keep-pipes-from-freezing/thaw-safely.webp" alt="Thawing a frozen pipe safely: hair dryer on the exposed run with the tap open, and a crossed-out torch showing never to use open flame." >}}

1. **Find the freeze, don't guess.** Open the tap. No flow at all says the freeze is upstream of that faucet's branch; slow trickle says it's partially open. Work from the faucet back toward the meter, checking accessible runs.
2. **Keep the tap open while you work.** Melting water needs somewhere to go; the open faucet is the pressure release that keeps a thawing pipe from becoming a burst pipe.
3. **Heat the pipe, gently, from the faucet side toward the freeze.** Hair dryer on high, heat gun on low, heating pad wrapped around the run, hot towels. You're moving a plug, not blasting ice.
4. **Never** an open flame — torch, propane, cigarette lighter. This is how frozen-pipe season produces house fires. Also never use a space heater pointed at a wall cavity unattended (see [space heater running cost](/blog/space-heater-running-cost/) for what that costs to run, and keep any heater 3 feet from combustibles).
5. **Cannot find it, cannot reach it, or the pipe has already split?** Shut the main valve — usually where the line enters the house (the shutoff test in [who's responsible for a water leak](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/) shows how to isolate your side) — and call a plumber. Water damage compounds hourly.

If it burst: shut the main first, then document everything for the insurer — most homeowner policies cover sudden accidental discharge from a burst pipe but not the pipe repair itself (the "hidden damage" nuance; verify with your carrier). And when the bill arrives, a burst pipe's wasted water is disputable — the [water bill leak-adjustment walkthrough](/blog/water-bill-leak-adjustment-how-to-get-credit/) covers the credit request.

## Who pays: you, the utility, or the insurer?

The dividing line is the same one used for [leak responsibility before and after the water meter](/blog/who-responsible-water-leak-before-after-meter/): **you own everything past the meter** (in most US arrangements, the customer-side service line and all house plumbing). A freeze in your crawl space is your pipe, your repair, your insurance claim. The utility's side of the meter is theirs — but a frozen meter pit is rarer, because meters sit below frost depth. The insurer pays for sudden water damage, subject to your deductible and the maintenance exclusion — a pipe you knew was vulnerable and ignored is arguable; a cold snap you couldn't foresee is a clean claim. Water wasted through the burst runs through your meter and onto your bill — that part is your consumption, though many utilities grant a leak credit (see the adjustment page above).

## FAQs

{{< faq "At what temperature do pipes freeze?" >}}
There's no single number — it's exposure plus time. Pipes in heated walls rarely freeze even below 0°F outdoors; pipes in uninsulated crawl spaces have frozen during a night in the low 20s°F with wind. The Red Cross treats sustained 20s°F-and-below cold snaps as the risk window. Treat "below freezing overnight, below the mid-20s for hours" as the trigger for the free rung of the ladder.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Should I drip hot or cold water?" >}}
Cold, from the faucet fed by the at-risk pipe — cold lines freeze first because they're the ones running to outdoor spigots and through cold spaces. If your hot lines are also exposed, a pencil-thin drip on hot too; hot water lines do freeze. The cost is trivial next to the claim — see [dripping faucet cost math](/blog/how-much-does-dripping-faucet-add-to-water-bill/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Does heat tape use a lot of electricity?" >}}
Self-regulating tape in the 3–5W-per-foot class draws roughly 100W fully on for a 20-foot run — a couple of cents an hour at average US rates, and it self-throttles as the pipe warms. It's a rounding error next to a $5,000+ burst (IBHS average). Full watts-to-dollars method: [space heater running cost](/blog/space-heater-running-cost/).
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How do I keep pipes from freezing in a crawl space or mobile home?" >}}
Same ladder, harsher space: drip the faucet fed by that run, insulate every accessible foot of it, seal the wind paths (crawl vents in deep cold are contested — ask your local building department), and put self-regulating heat cable on any run insulation alone has failed to protect. Mobile-home supply lines under the floor are the classic case for heat cable — they sit in open air all winter.
{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What if I have no power (heating outage)?" >}}
The free rung still works: drip the faucet, open cabinets, and add the shutoff-and-drain move — if you expect a multi-day outage in hard freezing weather, shutting the main and draining the system (run the lowest faucet until empty) is the certain protection. Fill containers before you shut off.
{{< /faq >}}
