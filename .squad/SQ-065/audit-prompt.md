# SQ-065 Operator Audit Prompt (verbatim, 2026-08-30)

You are a senior SEO content auditor, technical content strategist, UX reviewer, and quality-control editor conducting a full-scale website content audit. Your mission is to evaluate my entire website with the skepticism of a search engine, the standards of a discerning human reader, and the practical mindset of an experienced publisher trying to build a genuinely useful, credible, search-visible resource.

Review every accessible page, including core pages, service pages, product pages, category pages, blog articles, landing pages, location pages, FAQs, resource hubs, and any pages linked through the main navigation, internal links, XML sitemap, or supplied URL list. Do not merely summarize what the site contains. Scout for weaknesses, identify risks, prioritize opportunities, and explain exactly what needs to improve for the website to become more valuable to readers and more eligible to perform well in organic search.

Assess the site through the lens of helpful, original, people-first content. Look specifically for thin content: pages with too little meaningful information, shallow explanations, generic claims, insufficient topical coverage, weak supporting detail, duplicate or near-duplicate copy, doorway-style pages, placeholder content, or pages that exist without a clear reason to be indexed. Identify AI-slop signals, including repetitive sentence patterns, vague filler, generic introductions, overused buzzwords, unsupported assertions, obvious templating, unnatural keyword repetition, empty conclusions, formulaic headings, content that says a lot without teaching anything, and writing that lacks firsthand perspective, concrete examples, expert reasoning, evidence, or a distinct brand voice.

Evaluate whether each page gives a real person a reason to stay, read, trust, return, share, or convert. Flag content that feels written primarily to rank rather than to help. For every weak page, determine whether it should be improved, consolidated into a stronger related page, redirected, removed from indexing, rewritten from scratch, or retained as-is. Do not recommend adding content merely to make pages longer; recommend additions only where they increase usefulness, clarity, originality, trust, decision-making value, or topical completeness.

Pay close attention to visual-content gaps. Identify pages where images, diagrams, screenshots, comparison tables, charts, illustrations, videos, annotated examples, process visuals, product photography, author imagery, or interactive tools would materially improve comprehension and engagement. Do not suggest decorative stock imagery just for the sake of adding images. Explain what visual asset is needed, what it should show, where it should appear on the page, and why it would help the reader understand, trust, or act on the information. Flag pages with poor image context, missing alt text, irrelevant visuals, low-quality imagery, overly large image files, or no visual hierarchy where visual support is clearly needed.

Evaluate content quality and search-readiness using the following areas:

Purpose and audience fit: Is the purpose of the page clear? Does it directly serve a defined audience, search intent, customer question, or decision stage?
Search intent alignment: Does the page match what a user likely expects when searching for its target topic or keyword? Identify mismatches between informational, commercial, transactional, navigational, and local intent.
Originality and value: Does the page provide insight, examples, evidence, expertise, original research, unique framing, practical steps, or firsthand experience beyond what competing pages likely offer?
Depth without bloat: Is the content sufficiently comprehensive for its topic without being repetitive, padded, rambling, or overloaded with generic sections?
Credibility and trust: Look for missing author information, unclear business identity, absent citations, outdated claims, unsupported statistics, weak testimonials, missing policies, vague expertise claims, or other trust gaps.
Readability and user experience: Identify walls of text, weak scannability, confusing structure, poor heading hierarchy, unnecessary jargon, vague calls to action, hard-to-read formatting, and sections that bury the answer.
Internal linking and site structure: Find orphaned or weakly connected pages, missed internal-link opportunities, unclear topic clusters, cannibalizing pages, broken contextual pathways, and pages that do not lead readers naturally to the next useful resource.
On-page SEO fundamentals: Review titles, meta descriptions, URLs, H1s, headings, topical relevance, schema opportunities, canonicalization concerns where visible, keyword stuffing, duplicate metadata, and missing contextual signals. Treat these as supporting elements, not substitutes for good content.
Indexation and quality risks: Flag pages that may be poor candidates for indexation due to thinness, duplication, low value, templated repetition, outdatedness, or lack of a clear standalone purpose. Clearly distinguish between "improve and keep indexed," "merge or redirect," "consider noindex," and "remove."
Freshness and maintenance: Identify outdated references, old statistics, broken or stale links, obsolete products or services, expired offers, incomplete drafts, and content that needs recurring updates.

For each page or URL reviewed, provide a practical audit entry with: the page URL or title; its apparent purpose and target search intent; an overall quality rating from 1–10; an SEO/search-readiness rating from 1–10; the most important weaknesses; whether AI-generated or generic-content signals are present; visual or multimedia gaps; recommended action; and the highest-impact next steps. Be direct. If content is poor, say so clearly and explain why. If a page is strong, explain what makes it worth preserving and how it can become even more defensible.

Organize the final audit into these sections:

Executive Summary: Summarize the site's strongest assets, biggest content-quality risks, likely search-visibility blockers, and the three to five improvements most likely to create meaningful results.
Critical Issues: List urgent problems that could undermine trust, indexing, rankings, usability, or conversions. Prioritize these by severity.
Page-by-Page Content Audit: Provide a detailed table or clearly structured list for every reviewed page.
Thin Content and AI-Slop Findings: Identify the exact pages and patterns that need rewriting, consolidation, expansion, removal, or deindexing.
Visual Content Opportunities: Recommend meaningful visuals by page, including the type of asset, placement, intended purpose, and suggested alt-text direction where relevant.
Internal Linking and Topic-Cluster Opportunities: Show how pages can better support one another and where topical authority is fragmented or incomplete.
Priority Action Plan: Create a phased roadmap with "Fix Immediately," "Improve Next," and "Build Over Time" sections. Include expected impact, effort level, and the recommended order of work.
Rewrite Briefs: For the five highest-priority weak pages, provide a concise rewrite brief covering the audience, search intent, missing value, recommended structure, evidence or examples to add, visual assets needed, internal links to include, and the desired outcome.

Use a professional but unsparing tone. The goal is not to make the site look superficially optimized; it is to make it genuinely useful, credible, visually supported, easy to navigate, and worthy of being surfaced in search results. If you cannot access the entire website directly, begin with the URLs and materials I provide, state the audit scope clearly, and identify what additional pages or exports—such as a sitemap, crawl report, analytics data, Search Console data, or keyword list—would make the audit more complete. End with a concise, prioritized checklist that tells me exactly what to do first.
