Furnace efficiency decline</h3><p>Furnaces lose efficiency as they age. A furnace rated at 92% AFUE (Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency) may drop to 80–85% after 10–15 years without proper maintenance. Pre-2000 furnaces commonly sit at <strong>70–80% AFUE</strong>, meaning <strong>20–30% of the fuel you pay for escapes through the exhaust flue</strong> as wasted heat rather than warming your home. A new high-efficiency condensing furnace reaches <strong>95% AFUE</strong> — that gap is the single biggest efficiency upgrade available to most gas-heated households.</p><p>Short cycling (the burner turning on and off every few minutes without reaching set temperature) is another symptom of an aging or struggling furnace. It wastes fuel during startup cycles and stresses the heat exchanger. Annual professional tune-up — cleaning burners, checking the heat exchanger, verifying airflow — keeps the system near its rated AFUE.</p><p><strong>Signs your furnace is wasting gas:</strong></p><ul><li>Runs longer to reach the target temperature</li><li>Uneven heating (some rooms cold, others hot)</li><li>Yellow flame instead of blue (incomplete combustion — also a safety concern)</li><li>Soot around the furnace or vents</li><li>Increasing gas usage year over year despite same habits</li></ul><p><strong>What to do:</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Annual maintenance</strong> ($80–$200) — A professional tune-up can restore 2–5% efficiency and catch problems early.</li><li><strong>Filter replacement</strong> — A dirty filter forces the furnace to work harder. Replace every 1–3 months during heating season.</li><li><strong>Consider replacement</strong> — If your furnace is 15+ years old, a new 95–98% AFUE model can cut gas usage by 20–30%. Typical cost: $3,000–$6,000 installed, with payback in 5–8 years through gas savings.</li></ul><h3 id=4-poor-insulation-and-air-leaks>4. Poor insulation and air leaks</h3><p>Your furnace may be working correctly, but you&rsquo;re losing heat through:</p><ul><li><strong>Attic insulation</strong> — The #1 source of heat loss in most homes. If you have less than R-38 (about 10–14 inches of fiberglass), adding insulation pays for itself in 1–3 years.</li><li><strong>Windows and doors</strong> — Weatherstripping and caulking cost $20–$50 and can reduce drafts significantly.</li><li><strong>Recessed lights</strong> — Can lights in ceilings below attics are notorious for air leakage.</li><li><strong>Ductwork leaks</strong> — Leaky ducts in unconditioned spaces (attics, crawl spaces) can waste 20–30% of your heated air.[3]</li></ul><p><strong>Quick test:</strong> On a cold windy day, hold a lit incense stick near windows, doors, electrical outlets on exterior walls, and any penetrations. Smoke moving horizontally indicates an air leak.</p><h3 id=5-gas-water-heater-waste>5. Gas water heater waste</h3><p>Water heating is typically the <strong>second-largest gas expense</strong> (15–25% of gas usage). According to the U.S. Department of Energy, water heating accounts for about 20% of utility bills overall.[4] Common waste sources:</p><ul><li><strong>Temperature set too high</strong> — Many water heaters are set to 140°F by default. Lowering to 120°F saves 4–22% on water heating costs with no noticeable difference in comfort.</li><li><strong>Sediment buildup</strong> — Minerals settle at the bottom of the tank, creating an insulating layer between the burner and water. Flush the tank annually.</li><li><strong>Old and inefficient</strong> — Standard tank water heaters are 58–65% efficient. A tankless condensing model can be 95–98% efficient.</li><li><strong>Leaking T&amp;P valve</strong> — If the temperature and pressure relief valve is dripping, the heater is over-pressurizing and wasting hot water.</li><li><strong>No insulation blanket</strong> — Wrapping an older tank-style heater with a $20–$40 insulation blanket cuts standby heat loss by 5–10%.[4]</li></ul><h3 id=6-rate-increase>6. Rate increase</h3><p>Natural gas prices are volatile. Your utility&rsquo;s &ldquo;gas cost recovery&rdquo; rate can change monthly or quarterly. In late 2025 and into 2026, many U.S. utilities have increased rates 15–30% year-over-year (U.S. EIA, 2026).</p><p><strong>What to check on your bill:</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Cost per therm</strong> — Compare with the same month last year.</li><li><strong><a href=/blog/gas-delivery-charge-vs-supply-charge/>delivery charges</a></strong> — Your utility may have raised the fixed delivery charge.</li><li><strong><a href=/blog/18-fuel-adjustment-charge-on-utility-bill-explained/>Fuel adjustment charges</a></strong> — A line item that passes wholesale gas cost changes through to your bill.</li><li><strong>Pipeline or infrastructure surcharges</strong> — Becoming more common.</li><li><strong>Weather normalization adjustment</strong> — Some utilities smooth out weather impacts; others don&rsquo;t.</li></ul><p><strong>WNA (Winter Non-Winter Adjustment) explained:</strong> Some utilities — Virginia Natural Gas is one example — apply a <strong>Winter Non-Winter Adjustment</strong> on six bills during the heating season, typically November through April. The WNA recovers the fixed cost of maintaining gas delivery infrastructure during peak winter demand, spread across winter bills rather than charged flat year-round. The utility does not profit from it, but it does show up as a separate line item and contributes to winter totals being higher than summer totals even at identical usage. Check your bill for &ldquo;WNA&rdquo; or &ldquo;winter adjustment&rdquo; line items; they&rsquo;re normal and regulated, not a billing error.</p><p><strong>What to do:</strong> If rates increased significantly, check if you&rsquo;re in a deregulated state where you can choose a gas supplier. You may be able to lock in a lower rate through a third-party supplier.</p><h3 id=7-gas-leak-safety-concern>7. Gas leak (safety concern)</h3><p>A gas leak is both a safety emergency and a source of wasted gas. Signs include:</p><ul><li><strong>Rotten egg smell</strong> — Mercaptan, the odorant added to natural gas.</li><li><strong>Hissing sound</strong> — Near gas lines, valves, or appliances.</li><li><strong>Dead vegetation</strong> — Plants dying near an underground gas line.</li><li><strong>Higher-than-expected usage</strong> — Even a small leak adds up over a billing period.</li></ul><p><strong>⚠️ If you smell gas:</strong> Leave the house immediately. Don&rsquo;t use light switches, phones, or anything that could create a spark. Call your gas utility&rsquo;s emergency line (or 911) from outside.</p><p><strong>Soapy-water leak test (non-emergency):</strong> If you don&rsquo;t smell gas but suspect a small leak on an accessible fitting, mix dish soap and water in a spray bottle, then spray the mixture on gas connections behind appliances, at the meter, and at shut-off valves. If bubbles form and grow, gas is escaping at that joint. <strong>Do not attempt to repair the fitting yourself</strong> — call a licensed plumber or your utility. Higher summer gas pressure can also worsen slow leaks, which is why unexplained summer spikes deserve a meter-and-fitting audit before you assume rate increases are to blame.</p><h3 id=8-gas-fireplace-or-logs-left-on>8. Gas fireplace or logs left on</h3><p>Gas fireplaces and decorative gas logs use <strong>25,000–60,000 BTU/hour</strong>. Leaving a gas fireplace on overnight (8 hours) at 40,000 BTU uses about 3.2 therms — roughly $3–$5/night depending on your rate. Over a month, that&rsquo;s $90–$150.</p><p><strong>Check:</strong> Make sure your gas fireplace is completely off (pilot lights on older models also consume gas continuously — about 600–900 BTU/hour, which adds up to $10–$20/month).</p><h3 id=9-drafty-or-inefficient-windows>9. Drafty or inefficient windows</h3><p>Single-pane windows or old double-pane windows with failed seals let significant heat escape. Replacing windows is expensive ($300–$1,000/window), but low-cost alternatives help:</p><ul><li><strong>Window film insulation kits</strong> — $15–$30 per window, reduces heat loss by 40–70%.</li><li><strong>Thermal curtains</strong> — $20–$60 per window, blocks drafts and radiant heat loss.</li><li><strong>Weatherstripping</strong> — $5–$15 per window, seals gaps around the frame.</li></ul><h3 id=10-billing-cycle-length-or-estimated-readings>10. Billing cycle length or estimated readings</h3><p>A 35-day billing period will naturally cost more than a 28-day period. And estimated readings can be wildly off if your usage pattern changed.</p><p><strong>What to check:</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Days of service</strong> on your bill</li><li>Whether the read was actual or estimated</li><li>If estimated, the next actual read will correct any overcharge</li></ul><h3 id=11-outdoor-gas-appliances-pool-spa-grill-fire-pit>11. Outdoor gas appliances (pool, spa, grill, fire pit)</h3><p>Many homeowners forget that outdoor gas appliances tie back to the same meter that serves the furnace. In warm months when heating is off, these can quietly carry the bill:</p><ul><li><strong>Gas pool heater</strong> — Maintaining a pool temperature during summer requires constant gas input to offset evaporation and heat loss. A typical gas pool heater adds <strong>$50–$150/month</strong> to your bill during active swim season — one of the most expensive gas appliances to run per hour of use.</li><li><strong>Gas spa / hot tub</strong> — Smaller than a pool but operates at higher temperatures (100–104°F) year-round in many homes. Expect steady monthly gas use whenever the tub is heated.</li><li><strong>Outdoor gas grill</strong> — Frequent summer cookouts add up. A high-BTU grill running on natural gas (not propane tanks) consumes real volume over a barbecue-heavy month.</li><li><strong>Gas fire pit / outdoor fireplace</strong> — Natural gas fire pits running on summer evenings register on the meter too.</li></ul><p><strong>What to do:</strong> If your summer bill surprises you, walk your property and count gas-tied outdoor appliances. A pool heater alone can push a &ldquo;should be tiny&rdquo; summer bill into the $150+ range.</p><hr><h2 id=seasonal-comparison-winter-vs-summer-gas-usage>Seasonal comparison: Winter vs summer gas usage</h2><p>To visualize how the same home&rsquo;s gas demand shifts across the year, the table below compares a typical US single-family residence. Predictable but may be higher than market rate when prices drop.</p><h3 id=variable-rate>Variable rate</h3><p>Your rate changes monthly based on market prices. Can be cheaper during low-demand periods but risky during cold winters or supply disruptions.</p><h3 id=budget-billing>Budget billing</h3><p>Your utility averages your annual costs into equal monthly payments. You pay the same amount each month, with a &ldquo;true-up&rdquo; at the end of the year. This doesn&rsquo;t save money, but it prevents winter shock bills.</p><h3 id=delivery-vs-supply>Delivery vs. supply</h3><p>Your gas bill has two main components:</p><ul><li><strong>Supply charges</strong> — The cost of the gas itself. Check them in order from fastest to hardest to fix:</p><ol><li><strong>A longer billing period or estimated reading.</strong> A 28-day bill followed by a 34-day bill adds <strong>~21%</strong> more days of usage. A corrected &ldquo;EST&rdquo; reading can lump two months of charges into one bill.</li><li><strong>A rate increase.</strong> A fuel adjustment, seasonal shift, or new rate schedule can raise your per-kWh cost by 10%–25% with no usage change at all.</li><li><strong>Weather or HVAC working harder.</strong> A heat wave, a refrigerant leak, a clogged filter, or a heat pump stuck on emergency heat can double or triple your consumption without you changing a single habit.</li><li><strong>A malfunctioning appliance.</strong> A water heater element, a running well pump, an old garage fridge, or a continuously cycling pool pump can quietly add <strong>300–500 kWh</strong> per month (roughly $40–$75).</li><li><strong>A meter error or cross-billing.</strong> Rare, but real — and worth ruling out if nothing above fits.</li></ol><p><strong>Start here:</strong> Grab your current bill and last month&rsquo;s bill. In about 10 minutes you can rule out causes #1, #2, and #5 without leaving your kitchen. The 12-step investigation below walks through each cause in order, with dollar amounts, examples, and the exact action to take for each.</p><p>If you already know which cause is yours: a rate change points to <a href=/blog/18-fuel-adjustment-charge-on-utility-bill-explained/>fuel adjustment and rider charges explained</a>, an HVAC problem starts with <a href=/blog/03-why-is-my-electric-bill-so-high/>why your bill is high</a>, and a suspected heat-pump issue usually means <a href=/blog/aux-heat-vs-emergency-heat-meaning/>auxiliary heat running when it shouldn&rsquo;t</a>.</p><p><strong>Next step:</strong> Once you&rsquo;ve identified the likely cause of your spike, walk through the <a href=/blog/electric-bill-breakdown-understanding-line-items/>electric bill breakdown</a> to understand every charge on your statement, or see <a href=/blog/01-how-to-read-your-electric-bill/>how to read your electric bill</a> for a complete line-by-line guide.</p><hr><p>Last month&rsquo;s electric bill was <strong>$135</strong>. This month it is <strong>$340</strong>. You have not bought any new appliances, you have not added a pool heater, and your daily routine has not changed. A <strong>150% increase</strong> with no explanation is alarming, but it is also one of the most common complaints utility customer service departments handle. Here is a systematic guide to diagnosing and resolving a sudden spike in your electricity bill.</p><h2 id=the-top-10-causes-at-a-glance>The Top 10 Causes at a Glance</h2><table><thead><tr><th>#</th><th>Likely Cause</th><th>How to Check</th><th>Typical Time to Fix</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>1</td><td><strong>Longer billing period</strong> (28 vs 34 days)</td><td>Compare service dates on this bill vs. last</td><td>Instant</td></tr><tr><td>2</td><td><strong>Estimated meter reading</strong> corrected upward</td><td>Look for &ldquo;EST&rdquo; Detailed instructions for each follow below.</p></blockquote><h2 id=step-1-verify-the-bill-is-correct>Step 1: Verify the Bill Is Correct</h2><p>Before investigating your usage, verify that the bill itself is accurate. Check these elements:</p><p><strong>Your account number and service address:</strong> Ensure the bill is actually for your account. Meter misreads can sometimes assign one customer&rsquo;s usage to another.</p><p><strong>The meter number:</strong> Compare the meter number on your bill to the number on your physical meter. If they do not match, the utility is reading the wrong meter.</p><p><strong>Current and previous readings:</strong> Look at the &ldquo;Current Reading&rdquo; and &ldquo;Previous Reading&rdquo; on your bill. Subtract the previous from the current to verify the billed consumption. If the math does not match the billed kWh, there is a billing error.</p><p><strong>Rate schedule:</strong> Verify you are on the correct rate plan. A recent rate case or system change may have moved you to a different (more expensive) rate schedule without notification.</p><p><strong>Hidden fees and rider charges:</strong> Scan the charges section of your bill line by line. Utilities sometimes add or increase rider charges — transmission riders, distribution riders, energy efficiency surcharges, or &ldquo;customer charge&rdquo; increases — that are easy to overlook because they are small per-kWh adders that compound across a month of usage. A <strong>0.5¢/kWh</strong> rider increase on 1,200 kWh is <strong>$6/month</strong> you may not have noticed in previous months.</p><p>If any of these elements are wrong, call the utility&rsquo;s billing department immediately and request a correction. Most utilities will investigate and correct verified billing errors within one to two billing cycles, applying a credit to your next bill.</p><h2 id=step-2-check-the-billing-period-length>Step 2: Check the Billing Period Length</h2><p>Billing periods vary from <strong>28 to 34 days</strong>. A longer billing period means more days of usage and a higher bill, even if your daily consumption is unchanged.</p><p>Compare the service period dates on your current bill to the previous month&rsquo;s bill. If last month covered 28 days and this month covers 34 days, that is a <strong>21% increase</strong> in the number of billing days — which would account for a significant portion of a bill increase.</p><p><strong>Worked example:</strong> If your average daily usage is 40 kWh at 15¢/kWh, your bill should be roughly $168 for a 28-day period and $204 for a 34-day period. That $36 difference is billing-period math, not behavior change.</p><p>Calculate your average daily usage by dividing total kWh by the number of days in the service period. This normalizes for period length and gives you a comparable number across months. If your daily usage is the same but the total bill is higher, the spike is explained by the billing period.</p><h2 id=step-3-compare-the-per-kwh-rate>Step 3: Compare the Per-KWh Rate</h2><p>Rate changes are one of the most overlooked causes of sudden bill increases. Utilities can and do change their rates, sometimes with minimal notification buried in the fine print of your bill.</p><p>Compare the per-kWh rate on your current bill to the same month last year. If the rate has increased from 12¢ to 15¢ per kWh, that alone would cause a <strong>25% bill increase</strong> even with identical usage.</p><p>Rate increases happen through several mechanisms:</p><ul><li><strong>General rate case decisions:</strong> State commission-approved rate changes that take effect on a specific date.</li><li><strong>Seasonal rate adjustments:</strong> Some utilities charge higher rates in summer (peak demand) and lower rates in winter.</li><li><strong>Fuel adjustment clauses:</strong> Utilities with fuel adjustment clauses pass through changes in generation fuel costs (natural gas, coal) directly to customers, sometimes monthly.</li><li><strong>Time-of-use rate enrollment:</strong> If you were moved to a time-of-use rate plan, your effective per-kWh rate may be higher if your usage patterns do not align with the plan&rsquo;s off-peak windows. For help aligning your usage to cheaper hours, see <a href=/blog/46-what-time-is-electricity-cheapest/>what time electricity is cheapest</a>.</li><li><strong>Demand charges:</strong> If your bill shows a line item measured in kW (not kWh), you are being charged for your peak power draw in addition to total energy used. Demand charges are standard on commercial rates and appear on some residential plans — especially homes with EV chargers, large well pumps, or electric heat. A single high-draw event (charging an EV while running the dryer and AC simultaneously) can set your demand peak for the entire billing cycle. Learn more in our <a href=/blog/49-demand-charges-electricity-bill-explained/>guide to demand charges on your electricity bill</a>.</li></ul><h2 id=step-4-rule-out-weather-and-seasonal-factors>Step 4: Rule Out Weather and Seasonal Factors</h2><p>Even if you feel like your habits have not changed, weather can dramatically affect your electricity consumption without you realizing it:</p><p><strong>Heat waves:</strong> A <strong>10-day stretch above 95°F</strong> can double your air conditioning usage compared to a month with temperatures in the 80s. Your thermostat setting may be the same, but the AC runs longer and harder to maintain it.</p><p><strong>Cold snaps:</strong> Electric heating (heat pumps, electric baseboard, space heaters) responds similarly to cold. A week of temperatures below 20°F can triple heating-related electricity consumption.</p><p><strong>Humidity:</strong> High humidity makes air conditioners work harder because they must remove moisture in addition to cooling the air. A humid August month can produce significantly higher AC energy use than a dry August.</p><p>Compare your bill month to the same month last year, but also compare it to the actual weather data. The National Weather Service and most local news sites publish heating degree days (HDD) and cooling degree days (CDD), which measure how much heating or cooling was needed. If this month had <strong>50% more CDD</strong> than last year, a proportional increase in your bill is expected.</p><h2 id=step-5-check-for-estimated-meter-readings>Step 5: Check for Estimated Meter Readings</h2><p>If your bill says &ldquo;Estimated&rdquo; or &ldquo;EST&rdquo; next to the meter reading, the utility did not actually read your meter. Instead, they estimated your usage based on historical data, seasonal averages, or neighboring accounts.</p><p>Estimated readings can produce spikes for two reasons:</p><ol><li><strong>The estimate was too high:</strong> The utility overestimated your usage, and you are being overcharged. The next actual reading will correct this with a credit.</li><li><strong>The previous month was underestimated:</strong> Your current bill may include a &ldquo;catch-up&rdquo; for a prior month where the estimate was too low. The current month shows normal usage plus the undercharged amount from last month.</li></ol><p>If you see an estimated reading, call the utility and request an actual meter reading. Most will send a technician within a few business days. You can also read the meter yourself and provide the reading to the utility for an immediate recalculation. For a deeper explanation of how estimated billing works and why it causes spikes, read our guide on <a href=/blog/14-estimated-utility-bill-explained/>estimated utility bills</a>.</p><h2 id=step-6-investigate-hvac-system-issues>Step 6: Investigate HVAC System Issues</h2><p>HVAC problems are the single most common cause of legitimate usage spikes:</p><p><strong>Dirty air filter:</strong> A clogged air filter forces your AC or furnace to work <strong>15% to 30% harder</strong>, consuming significantly more electricity.[1] Replace your filter every 1 to 3 months.</p><p><strong>Refrigerant leak:</strong> If your AC system is low on refrigerant, it runs much longer to achieve the same cooling, increasing energy consumption by <strong>30% to 50%</strong>. Signs include reduced cooling performance, ice on the outdoor unit, and a hissing sound.</p><p><strong>Duct leaks:</strong> Leaky ductwork can lose <strong>20% to 30%</strong> of conditioned air before it reaches your living spaces, forcing your system to run longer to compensate.[2]</p><p><strong>Failed compressor components:</strong> A failing compressor motor draws more current than a healthy one. If your AC is 10+ years old, compressor inefficiency may be silently increasing your energy use.</p><p><strong>Thermostat malfunction:</strong> A thermostat that reads incorrectly (reading 72°F when the actual temperature is 78°F) will cause your HVAC system to run unnecessarily.</p><p><strong>Heat pump emergency heat:</strong> If your heat pump&rsquo;s outdoor unit fails and the system switches to emergency electric resistance heat, your heating costs can increase by <strong>200% to 300%</strong>. Emergency heat uses electric resistance coils that consume 3 to 5 times more energy than the heat pump&rsquo;s normal operation.</p><p><strong>Thermostat optimization:</strong> The U.S. Department of Energy states that a thermostat setback of 7–10°F for eight hours a day can save as much as 10% annually on heating and cooling.[3]</p><p>If you suspect an HVAC issue, schedule a professional inspection. The cost of a service call (<strong>$100–$200</strong>) is usually recovered within one or two months of lower energy bills after the repair.</p><h2 id=step-7-look-for-phantom-appliances-or-new-loads>Step 7: Look for Phantom Appliances or New Loads</h2><p>Sometimes the cause is an appliance you forgot about or one that started malfunctioning:</p><p><strong>Second refrigerator or freezer:</strong> An old, inefficient refrigerator in the garage can consume <strong>100–200 kWh per month</strong>, adding <strong>$15–$30</strong> to your bill. If it is running more because of summer heat, the cost increases.</p><p><strong>Space heaters:</strong> A single <strong>1,500-watt</strong> space heater running 8 hours per day adds approximately <strong>360 kWh per month</strong> — roughly $40–$60. If multiple heaters are in use, the cost compounds rapidly.</p><p><strong>Electric water heater issues:</strong> If the heating element or thermostat in your electric water heater fails, the unit may run continuously, consuming <strong>300–500 kWh per month</strong> more than normal.</p><p><strong>Well pump:</strong> If your home has a well, a failing well pump or a leak in the pressure tank can cause the pump to cycle on and off continuously, drawing significant power.</p><p><strong>Dehumidifier:</strong> A basement dehumidifier running continuously in humid months can consume <strong>200–400 kWh per month</strong>.</p><p><strong>Pool equipment:</strong> A pool pump running 24/7 instead of the recommended 4–8 hours can waste <strong>300–500 kWh per month</strong>. A pool heater adds even more.</p><p><strong>Charging an electric vehicle:</strong> If someone in the household started charging an EV, consumption increases by <strong>250–500 kWh per month</strong> depending on driving habits and charging efficiency.</p><blockquote><p><strong>Tip:</strong> A faulty appliance that runs continuously is the most common hidden cause of a usage spike. Smart plugs can help you identify and eliminate standby power waste — see our <a href=/blog/50-best-smart-plugs-standby-power/>best smart plugs for standby power</a> to monitor individual devices.</p></blockquote><h2 id=step-8-check-for-water-heater-problems>Step 8: Check for Water Heater Problems</h2><p>Electric water heaters are the second-largest energy consumer in most homes (after HVAC). Common issues that cause spikes:</p><p><strong>Leaking hot water:</strong> A dripping hot water faucet or running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons of hot water per month. Each gallon heated from 50°F to 120°F requires approximately <strong>0.15 kWh</strong>. A leak of 100 gallons per day wastes 15 kWh per day or <strong>450 kWh per month</strong> — about $55–$75.</p><p><strong>Failed lower element:</strong> In a dual-element electric water heater, if the lower element fails, the upper element alone must heat the entire tank. This causes the upper element to run twice as long, increasing consumption by <strong>30% to 50%</strong>.</p><p><strong>Sediment buildup:</strong> Over years of use, mineral sediment accumulates at the bottom of the tank, insulating the heating element from the water. The heater must run longer to heat through the sediment layer, reducing efficiency by <strong>10% to 25%</strong>.</p><p><strong>Thermostat set too high:</strong> Each 10°F reduction in water heater temperature saves approximately <strong>3% to 5%</strong> on water heating costs. If your thermostat is set to 140°F (the factory default) instead of the recommended 120°F, you are wasting 6% to 10% on water heating.</p><h2 id=step-9-investigate-possible-meter-malfunctions>Step 9: Investigate Possible Meter Malfunctions</h2><p>Meter malfunctions are rare but do happen. Smart meters (AMI meters) can occasionally fail in ways that cause them to report inflated readings:</p><p><strong>Meter running fast:</strong> The meter may register more consumption than actually occurs. This can be caused by a calibration error, a firmware bug, or a hardware malfunction.</p><p><strong>Communication error:</strong> Sometimes a smart meter fails to report interval data correctly, and the utility&rsquo;s system interpolates or estimates the missing data, potentially over-estimating usage.</p><p><strong>Meter creep:</strong> A very old mechanical meter may have a worn bearing that causes the disk to spin faster than it should.</p><p>If you suspect a meter malfunction, you can:</p><ol><li><strong>Request a meter test:</strong> Most utilities will test your meter for free or a small fee (<strong>$5–$25</strong>). The utility removes the meter, tests it in their lab, and replaces it with a tested meter.</li><li><strong>Monitor your own usage:</strong> Track your daily meter readings for a week and compare them to the utility&rsquo;s reported daily usage. If there is a consistent discrepancy, the meter may be faulty.</li><li><strong>File a complaint with the PUC:</strong> If the utility refuses to test the meter or you disagree with the results, your state public utility commission can order an independent test.</li></ol><h2 id=step-10-check-for-electrical-problems-in-your-home>Step 10: Check for Electrical Problems in Your Home</h2><p>Internal electrical problems in your home can cause significant energy waste:</p><p><strong>Short circuits or ground faults:</strong> A partial short circuit in your wiring (not severe enough to trip a breaker) can cause continuous current flow, wasting electricity and creating a fire hazard. Signs include warm outlets, discolored switch plates, or a burning smell.</p><p><strong>Wiring damage from rodents or pests:</strong> Mice, squirrels, and rats can chew through wire insulation, creating shorts and ground faults.</p><p><strong>Pool or well equipment wiring:</strong> Underground wiring to pool pumps, well pumps, or outbuildings can develop ground faults from moisture intrusion or physical damage.</p><p><strong>Aluminum wiring:</strong> Homes built between 1965 and 1975 may have aluminum branch circuit wiring, which is prone to loose connections and overheating. These loose connections create resistance, waste energy, and pose fire hazards.</p><p>If you suspect an electrical problem, have a licensed electrician perform an inspection. An electrical inspection typically costs <strong>$100–$300</strong> and can identify problems that save far more in energy costs and prevent potential fires.</p><h2 id=step-11-neighbor-or-cross-billing-issues>Step 11: Neighbor or Cross-Billing Issues</h2><p>In rare cases, a billing spike is caused by the utility assigning another customer&rsquo;s usage to your account:</p><p><strong>Shared meters:</strong> In multi-unit buildings, apartments, or condominiums, a shared meter that serves common areas may be billed to your account by mistake.</p><p><strong>Meter misassignment:</strong> During a meter replacement, software update, or account transfer, your meter reading may be cross-referenced with the wrong account number.</p><p><strong>Tandem meter readings:</strong> In some older installations, two meters share a single communication channel. A software error can assign one meter&rsquo;s data to the wrong account.</p><p>If you suspect cross-billing, compare your usage pattern to your actual daily routine. If your bill shows 50 kWh per day but you are single, work outside the home, and have no major appliances running during the day, the usage profile does not match your lifestyle — a strong indicator of a billing error.</p><h2 id=step-12-file-a-formal-dispute>Step 12: File a Formal Dispute</h2><p>If you have ruled out all usage-related causes and believe the bill is in error, file a formal dispute:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Contact the utility&rsquo;s billing department in writing:</strong> Send an email or letter (not just a phone call) describing the issue, the evidence you have gathered, and the resolution you are requesting. Keep a copy for your records.</p></li><li><p><strong>Request a meter test and account audit:</strong> Ask the utility to verify your meter number, test the meter, and audit your account for errors.</p></li><li><p><strong>Escalate to the public utility commission:</strong> If the utility does not resolve the issue within <strong>30 days</strong> (or sooner in emergency situations), file a complaint with your state PUC. PUC complaints are taken seriously, and utilities are required to respond within specified timelines.</p></li><li><p><strong>Document everything:</strong> Keep copies of all bills, correspondence, meter readings, and technician reports. This documentation is essential if the dispute escalates.</p></li></ol><h2 id=how-to-audit-your-electricity-usage>How to Audit Your Electricity Usage</h2><p>To systematically investigate a usage spike, conduct a home electricity audit:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Read your meter daily for 7 days:</strong> Record the reading at the same time each day. This establishes your baseline daily consumption and reveals usage patterns.</p></li><li><p><strong>Perform a circuit-by-circuit test:</strong> Turn off all breakers except one. Wait 15 minutes and read the meter. The difference from zero represents the consumption of that circuit. Repeat for each circuit. This identifies which circuit is consuming the most power.</p></li><li><p><strong>Use a Kill A Watt meter:</strong> Plug individual appliances into a Kill A Watt meter (<strong>$25–$40</strong>) to measure their actual consumption. Test refrigerators, freezers, space heaters, and any device you suspect may be malfunctioning.</p></li></ol><div class=product-box><span class=product-label>Find the energy thief</span>
