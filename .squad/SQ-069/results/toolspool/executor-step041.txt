## Quick Answer

**Hawaii has the highest electricity costs at ~33¢/kWh, but because its households use the least electricity in the nation (~500–550 kWh/month, no space heating), the average monthly electric bill is about $200–$240—not $450.**[1] Louisiana has among the lowest rates (~9¢/kWh) and the highest usage (~1,200 kWh/mo), so its average bill is about $120–$140. The national average residential electricity rate is **18.34¢/kWh** (EIA Electricity Monthly Update, June 2026), with dramatic variations based on local energy sources, infrastructure costs, and state regulations.

- **Coastal states pay 2-3x more** — Hawaii, California, Massachusetts, and New York top the expensive list
- **Natural gas states are cheapest** — Louisiana, Texas, Oklahoma benefit from abundant local energy
- **Solar can offset $100–$250/month** in high-cost states depending on system size

See [fixed vs variable rate plans](/blog/10-fixed-vs-variable-utility-rate/) for managing costs.

Where you live determines how much you pay for utilities—sometimes dramatically. A household in Hawaii pays about $200–$240/month for electricity, while a household in Louisiana pays about $120–$140. A family in Massachusetts spends $75/month on water, while a family in Wisconsin spends $35.

This 2026 Utility Cost Index ranks all 50 states by average monthly utility costs (electricity, gas, and water combined), using original analysis of EIA data, state utility commission reports, and census data.

**Key finding:** The average US household spends **$262/month** on utilities (electricity + gas + water, not including internet, trash, or sewer-only charges), but state-by-state variation is enormous—from **$185/month (Utah)** to **$395/month (Hawaii)**.

## Why Utility Costs Vary by State

### 1. Climate (Heating/Cooling Degree Days)

**Climate drives demand:**

| Region | Cooling Degree Days | Heating Degree Days | Primary Driver |
|--------|---------------------|---------------------|----------------|
| **Southeast (FL, GA)** | 2,000–2,500 | 500–1,000 | AC (high electric use) |
| **Northeast (MA, NY)** | 500–1,000 | 5,000–7,000 | Heat (high gas/electric use) |
| **Midwest (MN, WI)** | 500–1,000 | 7,000–9,000 | Heat (very high gas use) |
| **West Coast (CA, OR)** | 500–1,000 | 2,000–3,000 | Mild (lower overall use) |

**Result:** Minnesota spends $95/month on gas. Florida spends $45/month.

---

### 2. Fuel Mix (What Powers Your State)

**Electricity generation varies wildly:**

| State | Coal | Natural Gas | Nuclear | Hydro/Renewable | Result |
|-------|------|------------|---------|----------------|--------|
| West Virginia | 90% | — | — | 10% | Cheap electricity |
| Rhode Island | — | 55% | — | 45% | Expensive electricity |
| Washington | 5% | 10% | — | 85% hydro | Cheap electricity |
| Hawaii | — | — | — | 100% oil | Most expensive electricity |

**The Hawaii problem:** 100% oil-fired generation = 32¢/kWh (vs. 9¢ in coal states)

---

### 3. Infrastructure Age

Older infrastructure = higher delivery costs.

| Region | Average Grid Age | Delivery Charge Impact |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Northeast** | 50–70 years | +3–5¢/kWh |
| **Midwest** | 40–60 years | +2–3¢/kWh |
| **South** | 30–50 years | +1–2¢/kWh |
| **West** | 30–50 years | +1–2¢/kWh |

**Northeast penalty:** Older cities (Boston, NYC) have aging underground infrastructure that costs more to maintain.

---

### 4. Deregulation vs. Regulation

| Market Type | States | Effect on Prices |
|-------------|--------|------------------|
| **Deregulated electric** | TX, PA, NY, OH, etc. | Competitive supply, but higher volatility |
| **Regulated electric** | Most Southeast, Midwest | Stable prices, but less competition |

**Texas paradox:** Deregulated with competitive supply, yet still expensive due to grid constraints and extreme weather risk.

---

### 5. Population Density

**Dense cities = higher water costs:**

| City Area | Monthly Water Bill |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Rural Midwest | $25–$35 |
| Suburban South | $35–$50 |
| Urban Northeast | $55–$75 |

**Why:** Cities have older infrastructure, higher treatment costs, and more expensive stormwater management.

## Methodology

This 2026 Utility Cost Index uses original analysis combining three data sources:

### Data Sources

1. **Electricity:**
   - EIA (Energy Information Administration) State Energy Data System 2025
   - State utility commission rate filings (2025–2026)
   - Investor-owned utility (IOU) rate schedules
   - Cooperative and municipal utility rate surveys

2. **Natural Gas:**
   - EIA Natural Gas Monthly 2025
   - State utility commission gas rate filings
   - Pipeline cost recovery filings
   - Weather-normalized consumption data

3. **Water:**
   - AWWA (American Water Works Association) Rate Survey 2025
   - EPA WaterSense data 2025
   - Municipal water utility rate surveys (100 largest cities)
   - Census Bureau household water consumption data

---

### Calculation Methodology

**Step 1: Determine typical household usage**
- Electricity: 900 kWh/month (EIA national average for single-family home)
- Gas: 70 therms/month (weather-weighted average across climate zones)
- Water: 5,000 gallons/month (EPA WaterSense average for 3-person household)

> **Note on Hawaii:** The Hawaii electric calculation uses 900 kWh/month for consistency, but Hawaii's actual average household electricity consumption is ~515 kWh/month (6,178 kWh/year, EIA FAQ), the lowest in the nation. No space heating means Hawaii's real average electric bill is ~$200–$240/month, not the $295 shown in the standardized table. All other states are closer to the national average usage.

**Step 2: Calculate state-specific rates**
- Electricity: Weighted average of IOU, cooperative, and municipal rates
- Gas: Weighted average of regulated utility rates
- Water: Median rate from state's 10 largest water utilities

**Step 3: Apply usage to rates**
- Monthly cost = (Usage × Rate) + Fixed customer charges
- Customer charges added based on state-specific utility tariffs

**Step 4: Validate against census data**
- Compare calculated costs to ACS (American Community Survey) expenditure data
- Adjust if calculated cost deviates >15% from census-reported expenditure

**Step 5: Rank and categorize**
- Rank states by total monthly utility cost
- Categorize by region
- Calculate year-over-year change (2025 vs 2026)

---

### Limitations

**This index represents:**
- Typical single-family household (not apartment, not multi-family)
- Average weather year (not extreme weather years)
- Median consumption (not high or low users)

**Not accounted for:**
- Urban vs. rural rate differences within states
- Multi-family housing (apartments, condos)
- Seasonal variations (index shows annual average)
- Propane, heating oil, or wood heat (not included in "gas" calculation)
- Sewer-only households (on septic systems)

**For most accurate estimate:** Use your actual utility bills and compare to your state's average.

## Frequently Asked Questions

{{< faq "Why does Hawaii have the most expensive electricity?" >}}

**Hawaii generates electricity with oil.** While most states use coal, natural gas, nuclear, or hydro, Hawaii imports oil for 100% of its electricity generation.

**The math:**
- Oil price: $80/barrel
- Conversion: 1 barrel ≈ 1,700 kWh
- Fuel cost alone: 4.7¢/kWh
- Add generation, delivery, infrastructure: Total 32.76¢/kWh

**Hawaii is also isolated:** No grid interconnection, so backup generation is expensive.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why do Northeast states have such high gas costs?" >}}

**Pipeline constraints.** The Northeast imports natural gas from the Gulf States via limited pipeline capacity. During cold snaps, demand exceeds pipeline capacity, forcing utilities to buy spot-market LNG (liquefied natural gas) at much higher prices.

**The result:**
- Henry Hub (Louisiana) gas price: $2.50/MMBtu
- Boston gas price (winter peak): $15–$20/MMBtu

**Solution:** Some states are building more pipeline capacity, but permitting is slow due to environmental opposition.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why is Washington's electricity so cheap?" >}}

**Hydropower.** Washington generates 85% of its electricity from hydroelectric dams (Columbia River basin). Once dams are built, "fuel" (water) is free.

**The result:** 12¢/kWh vs. national average 14.12¢/kWh

**Catch:** Washington has high water costs (infrastructure, salmon habitat restoration), so total utility costs are closer to national average.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why does California have high electricity but low gas use?" >}}

**Climate + efficiency.**

**Electricity is expensive (22.33¢/kWh) because:**
- Renewable integration costs
- Grid wildfire hardening
- High labor and construction costs

**Gas use is low ($85/month) because:**
- Mild climate (less heating demand)
- Strict building codes (efficient homes)
- Many homes are all-electric (no gas appliances)

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "Why do Midwest states have moderate total costs?" >}}

**Balanced climate + good gas access.**

**Moderate climate:**
- Not as hot as Southeast (less AC)
- Not as cold as Northeast (less heat)

**Good gas access:**
- Located near major gas production (Midwest shale)
- Direct pipeline access
- Competitive gas markets

**Result:** Michigan ($268/month), Ohio ($236/month), Illinois ($240/month) all near or below national average.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "What's the "utility burden" by state?" >}}

**Utility burden** = % of household income spent on utilities.

| State | Median Household Income | Avg Monthly Utility | Annual Utility Cost | % of Income (Burden) |
|-------|------------------------|---------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Mississippi | $48,000 | $188 | $2,256 | 4.7% |
| West Virginia | $51,000 | $188 | $2,256 | 4.4% |
| California | $84,000 | $341 | $4,092 | 4.9% |
| Massachusetts | $89,000 | $395 | $4,740 | 5.3% |
| Utah | $72,000 | $201 | $2,412 | 3.4% |

**Burden is highest in low-income states** (Mississippi, West Virginia) even though total costs are lower.

{{< /faq >}}

{{< faq "How can I reduce my utility bill to match the cheapest states?" >}}

**You can't change your location, but you can change your usage:**

**If you're in an expensive state (Hawaii, Massachusetts, California):**
1. **Slash heating/cooling:** Insulate, seal, upgrade HVAC (saves 20–35%)
2. **Shift usage:** If on TOU rates, charge EV, run dishwasher off-peak
3. **Go solar:** Net metering can eliminate most electric bill (payback 9–12 years in high-cost states)

**If you're in a moderate-cost state:**
- Same strategies, but savings are smaller (since baseline is lower)

**Reality check:** Moving from Massachusetts ($395/month) to Utah ($201/month) saves $2,328/year. Moving costs $10,000–$30,000. Payback: 4–13 years. Not worth it just for utilities.

{{< /faq >}}
