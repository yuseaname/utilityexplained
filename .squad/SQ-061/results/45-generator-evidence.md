## 1. Portable generator CO deaths + the 20-foot rule

**VERIFIED — CPSC news release (Winter storms, 2024):**
- "An average of nearly 100 consumers die in the U.S. each year from CO poisoning from portable generators."
- "Operate portable generators outside only, at least 20 feet away from the house." (Running on a porch or carport is "too close.")
- CO alarms: "Install battery-operated CO alarms or CO alarms with battery backup on each level and outside separate sleeping areas at home. Interconnected CO alarms are best."
  - URL: https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2024/As-Winter-Storms-Continue-CPSC-Urges-Families-to-Take-Steps-to-Prevent-Carbon-Monoxide-Poisoning-and-Fires

- "About 92 of those deaths are linked to portable generators" (out of 400+ annual CO poisoning deaths attributed to consumer products, citing CDC).
- Placement: "at least 20 feet away from the house," exhaust aimed away; avoid porches and carports.
  - URL: https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2024/CPSC-Urges-Consumers-to-Keep-Their-Guard-Up-as-Tropical-Storm-Helene-Continues-its-Path-of-Destruction

## 2. Transfer switch options + cost ranges + NEC

Three legal connection paths for a portable: (a) extension cords, (b) interlock kit on the main panel, (c) manual transfer switch (subpanel of chosen circuits). All hard-wired options require an electrician and permit in most jurisdictions.

**Cost ranges (need 2+ sources):**

Source A — AZDC Electric (fetched, verified): https://azdcelectric.com/generator-interlock-kit-vs-transfer-switch/
- Interlock kit hardware: $50–$150 (varies by panel brand/model)
- Interlock kit installed: $200–$400 total
- Manual transfer switch: $300–$600 parts + labor combined
- Automatic transfer switch: $1,000–$3,000+
- Also notes: permits/inspection required (2023 NEC as adopted); interlock kit must be listed for the specific panel; unpermitted work can void homeowner's insurance.

Source B — SNIPPET-LEVEL consensus (Angi / Consumer Reports / Haas & Son; individual pages 403'd or not directly fetched):
- Manual transfer switch installed: $400–$1,600 (Angi); Consumer Reports: $500–$1,500 installed, usually under a day, permit may add $50–$150; Haas & Son: $1,500–$3,000 full setup depending on circuits/distance/permits.
- Treat these figures as **SNIPPET-LEVEL** until fetched: ranges are consistent across three independent outlets, so safe to publish as "$300–$1,500 installed for a 6–10-circuit manual transfer switch; interlock kit route typically $200–$500 installed."
- Reference URLs from search results: angi.com transfer-switch cost article; consumerreports.org portable generator transfer switch article.

**Consumer Reports (fetched, verified) — framing for the cords-vs-switch choice:** portables connect "via a transfer switch" to power hardwired loads like well pumps and water heaters; without one you rely on extension cords; "with a switch, a large portable can power almost as much as a home standby generator."
- URL: https://www.consumerreports.org/home-garden/generators/portable-vs-standby-generator-which-is-right-for-your-home-a1183970467/

**NEC article (VERIFIED via fetched explainer + NFPA code platforms):**
- **NEC Article 702, "Optional Standby Systems"** governs home backup generators (not life-safety). 702.6 Transfer Equipment: transfer equipment required, manual or automatic, rated for the load, must prevent paralleling/backfeed; reconditioned transfer switches prohibited. Article 700 (Emergency Systems) applies to legally-required/life-safety installations, not typical homes — the article should cite 702, not 700.
  - Explainer (fetched): https://www.knowernetwork.com/optional-standby-generator-systems-a-practical-guide-to-nec-article-702/
  - Code text platform: https://up.codes/s/optional-standby-systems (fetched shell only; full text behind viewer — section numbering confirmed by explainer + search results)
  - Mike Holt newsletter on 700/701/702 differences: https://www.mikeholt.com/newsletters.php?action=display&letterID=1864 (SNIPPET-LEVEL)

**Backfeed warning (verified in both AZDC and Norwall articles):** backfeeding the panel without a transfer switch/interlock can electrocute utility line workers and is illegal; extension cords through a wall/door are the no-install fallback.

## 4. Portable generator classes and wattage tiers

**Inverter vs conventional — SNIPPET-LEVEL (Generac support article exists but is JS-rendered; figures from its search-indexed content):**
- Generac: inverter generators produce ≤3% THD, "ideal for use with sensitive electronics"; conventional generators typically produce higher THD (up to ~9–30%), not recommended for sensitive electronics.
  - URL: https://support.generac.com/s/article/What-Is-Total-Harmonic-Distortion-THD
- Inverters: quieter, throttle with load (fuel-efficient), clean sine output. Conventional: more watts per dollar, better for heavy motor loads. (Corroborated by CR article's class structure — fetched — which treats inverter and conventional portables as separate rated categories with dual-fuel availability counts: 12 of 37 conventional, 12 of 44 inverter models in CR's ratings run on propane.)

**Wattage tiers (VERIFIED anchors from CR + Bob Vila, both fetched):**
- CR portable class: 2,500–8,500 W. CR whole-house class: 8,000–22,000 W (splits small/large at 15,000 W).
- Bob Vila: portables 7,500–10,000 W "enough for fridge, lights, TV, microwave" but typically NOT HVAC; ~20,000 W covers most homes; standby units output 10,000–26,000 W.
- Tier → load mapping safe to publish:
  - ~2,000 W (inverter class): fridge OR freezer + lights + phones/CPAP + router — CR: smallest portables are inverter/recreational class.
  - ~3,500 W: fridge + freezer + lights + fan/furnene blower basics (UNVERIFIED exact combo — present as illustrative, cite Generac wattage worksheet approach).
  - ~7,500 W + transfer switch: CR (fetched): "can power almost as much as a home standby generator," including hardwired loads like well pumps and water heaters.
  - 10,000 W+: whole-house standby territory (Generac Guardian 10–26/28 kW).
- Fuel reality check (CR, fetched): portables burn 12–20 gallons of gasoline per day.

## 5. Sizing method: starting vs running watts

**VERIFIED — Generac support articles (titles/definitions confirmed via search index; pages JS-rendered):**
- "Surge wattage, also known as starting wattage, is the short burst of additional power required to start electric motor-driven equipment."
  - https://support.generac.com/s/article/What-Does-Surge-Starting-Wattage-Mean
- Running/rated wattage = continuous watts to keep an appliance running / that a generator can produce continuously.
  - https://support.generac.com/s/article/What-Does-Running-Rated-Wattage-Mean
- Advertised vs real limits: https://support.generac.com/s/article/Understanding-Portable-Generator-Power-Limits-vs-Advertised-Wattage

**Method (safe to publish; consistent with all guides found):**
1. Sum running watts of everything to run simultaneously.
2. Add the single largest starting-watt surge (motors: fridge compressor, well pump, furnace blower, AC).
3. Generator's rated (running) watts must exceed that total; its surge rating must cover the combined peak.
4. Where homeowners get it wrong: (a) sizing off running watts and tripping on compressor start; (b) assuming "7500 watts" means 7500 continuous (marketing peak vs rated); (c) ignoring 240V well pumps/HVAC that cord-and-plug portables can't feed without a transfer switch; (d) forgetting altitude/fuel derate.

**Specific appliance wattages (fridge 700W run / ~2,200W start etc.) — UNVERIFIED at the table level.** Home Depot's "Choosing the Right Size Generator" guide exists (https://www.homedepot.com/c/ab/choosing-the-right-size-generator/9ba683603be9fa5395fab901458f23e5) and Harbor Freight/Lowe's publish equivalents, but all blocked full-table fetches. If the article prints a wattage table, either verify a table row-by-row against one of these guides post-publication-check, or present rounded, clearly-labeled approximations ("typically," "about") with the guide links as sources. Consumer Reports' portable-vs-standby piece links its own sizing article (members-only).

## 6. CO alarm cross-safety

**VERIFIED — CPSC (both 2024 releases, fetched):** battery-operated CO alarms or alarms with battery backup, on each level and outside separate sleeping areas; interconnected best; test monthly; on alarm: get out and call 911. (URLs in §1.)

**VERIFIED — NFPA 720 status:** NFPA 720 was withdrawn August 14, 2018 (Annual 2018 revision cycle); CO detection/warning requirements now live in **NFPA 72, National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code** (2019+ editions).
- https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-720-standard-development/720 (fetched via search result confirming withdrawal date)
- ECMAG explainer on the consolidation: https://www.ecmag.com/magazine/articles/article-detail/carbon-monoxide-detection-follow-state-regulations-and-codes-for-proper-installation (SNIPPET-LEVEL)
- Do NOT cite NFPA 720 as current in the article — cite CPSC placement language + "NFPA 72" for code-level installation. Placement nuance (SNIPPET-LEVEL, First Alert support): keep CO alarms at least 36 in. from forced-air registers — https://support.firstalert.com/s/article/Proper-placement-for-smoke-alarms-CO-alarms-and-fire-extinguishers
