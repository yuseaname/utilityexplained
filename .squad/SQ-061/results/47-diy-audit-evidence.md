## 1. DOE/EPA DIY home energy audit guidance (the official step list)

**Source (DOE DIY page, live):** https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/do-it-yourself-home-energy-assessments?nrg_redirect=370169
(The clean URL without `nrg_redirect` returns 404 — keep the redirect parameter in the article's source list, or link exactly as the current article does.)

Verified content of the current DOE DIY page:

- Framing quote: "While not as thorough as a professional home energy audit, a simple do-it-yourself walk-through can help you identify and prioritize some energy efficient upgrades."
- **Step 1 — Locate air leaks:** make "a list of obvious air leaks" — gaps at baseboards, door and window junctions, electrical outlets, switch plates, wall/window-mounted AC units. Also check for gaps around pipes and wires, electrical service penetration, foundation seals, and mail slots. Check storm windows and exterior siding. "The potential energy savings from reducing drafts may range from 5% to 30% per year for comfort" and the same fixes "could also result in a 5%-30% reduction of your total annual energy bill." (Exact current wording, DOE DIY page.)
- **Step 2 — Seal the leaks:** plug and caulk holes/penetrations for faucets, pipes, electric outlets, wiring; seal door and window frames with weatherstripping. Seal leaks in the attic knee walls, dropped-soffit areas over cabinets, and furnace/water-heater closet.
- **Step 3 — Check ventilation:** "Adequate ventilation is as important as air sealing" — attics need proper venting; never cover attic soffit vents with insulation; check for appropriate air exchange in a well-sealed house.
- **Step 4 — Check insulation:** home heat loss is often highest through attics/roofs; attic hatch/door should be insulated; R-value ratings are printed on insulation batts; R-38 is the DOE-recommended minimum for attics in most climates (verify climate zone before citing).
- **Step 5 — HVAC:** check filters monthly, especially during high-use seasons; change as needed. Consider professional cleaning/servicing of heating/cooling systems. "A professional technician should inspect, clean, and service your home's heating and/or cooling system at least once a year."
- **Step 6 — Lighting/appliances/electronics:**
  - Bulb output/efficiency: replace inefficient bulbs. Fluorescent tubes: "The most efficient tubes are T8 and T5"; replace T12 tubes with T8s.
  - Lighting dimmers and timers save money. CFLs in frequently used fixtures; LED upgrades.
  - Refrigerator door seals: place a piece of paper in the door jamb and check resistance when pulling out — if it slides out easily, "you may need new seals."
  - Estimate appliance age and check ENERGY STAR-qualified replacements.
- The page includes a **"likely savings" summary table** with total estimated savings percentages by improvement (air sealing, attic insulation top-up, duct sealing, equipment replacement, solar PV). Each range is described as an estimate.
- The page also points to printable **DOE Home Energy Audit checklist resources** (PDF download).

**Other DOE pages verified:**
- **Professional assessments page (live):** https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/professional-home-energy-assessments?nrg_redirect=308459 — describes the walk-through, the "blower door test" (variable-speed fan depressurizes the home to locate leaks), and a "puffer test"/duct leakage check with a "duct blast" machine for forced-air systems; includes combustion-safety-related checks.
- **Air Sealing Your Home (live):** https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/air-sealing-your-home — this page, NOT the DIY page, carries the classic leak-hunting techniques (see §3).

## 3. The DIY leak-hunt toolkit (verified methods)

**Source: DOE Air Sealing Your Home** https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/air-sealing-your-home

- **Leak-first rule:** "The most effective strategy is to reduce air leakage as much as possible" before adding insulation.
- **Windy-day note:** leaks are easiest to find "on a windy day" (the page recommends pressurizing the house — see below — to amplify small leaks).
- **Building pressurization test (step list, verbatim steps from the page):**
  1. First, close all exterior doors, windows, and fireplace flues.
  2. Turn off all combustion appliances such as gas burning furnaces and water heaters. (Remember to turn them back on when you're done, including re-lighting pilot lights.)
  3. Turn on all exhaust fans (generally located in the kitchen and bathrooms) or use a large window fan to suck the air out of one room.
  4. This increases infiltration — air drawn in through remaining gaps — making them easier to detect with the smoke test.
- **Smoke/incense test:** passing smoke (incense stick or smoke pencil) past suspected leak sites "small enough to fit into tight spaces" reveals leaks when the smoke wavers or is sucked out of or blown into the room.
- **Where to check (DOE list):** penetrations/knocks through the attic floor; dropped ceilings; soffit over cabinets; recessed lighting; attic hatch; rim joists, sill plates, and the top of concrete foundations in the basement; water and furnace flues; electrical service entrance; foundation and siding junctions; chimney flashing; window and door frames before old caulk and weatherstrip are removed.
- **Door/window "paper" test:** the DOE DIY page describes the paper-in-the-doorjamb resistance test specifically for **refrigerator/freezer door gaskets** (if paper slides out easily, seals need replacing). This Old House applies the same paper/dollar test to exterior doors — that broader use is competitor practice, not DOE wording. (Flag in article: attribute the gasket version to DOE.)
- **Outlet gaskets:** the DOE Air Sealing page lists electrical outlets and switch plates on exterior walls among the first places to air-seal — use pre-formed foam gaskets behind cover plates on exterior-wall outlets and switches. (This Old House cites foam outlet gaskets at roughly 10 cents each — UNVERIFIED price on primary pages; do not cite a price.)
- **Attic hatch:** DOE DIY page — attic hatch/door should be insulated and weatherstripped like any exterior door. (This Old House cites the "Attic Tent" product from ~$200 — manufacturer claim, not DOE; treat as product info, not savings fact.)
- **Material selection rule (DOE weatherstripping guidance):** choose weatherstripping based on the location and friction/wear expected at each joint; weatherstrip movable joints (operable windows and doors), caulk stationary joints. The article's "match the fix to the location" framing matches DOE guidance — good.
- **Payback:** DOE-classic guidance repeated on the Air Sealing page: weatherstripping and caulking "generally offer a payback period of one year or less" — verify wording survives on the live page before quoting; the live page does carry a one-year-or-less payback statement for basic sealing materials.

## 4. Professional audit context

**What a professional audit adds (DOE professional page, live):** https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/professional-home-energy-assessments?nrg_redirect=308459
- **Blower door test:** a variable-speed fan mounted in an exterior door frame depressurizes the house; the auditor can then locate leaks and gauge the overall "tightness" of the envelope. (Infrared thermography may be used simultaneously.)
- **Puffer/duct leakage test:** for forced-air systems — "duct blaster" style machine pressurizes ducts to measure leakage.
- **Thermographic scan** (see DOE's thermographic-inspections topic — clean URL 404'd during this session; cite via the professional page or search "energy.gov thermographic inspections" before linking).
- **Combustion safety:** professional assessments include checks on combustion appliances and venting (professional page framing; detailed combustion-analyzer tool list corroborated by Energy Harbor's auditor-tool list below).

**Blower-door / audit cost range (secondary sources — no single authoritative .gov figure):**
- Standalone blower door test: ~**$200-$500** (HomeGuide aggregates $200-$250 basic; $350-$450 with thermography; SCS Foam cites $250-$500 + travel) — HomeGuide: https://homeguide.com/costs/blower-door-test-cost ; SCS Foam: https://www.scsfoam.com/ (landing page; figure as reported in search aggregate) — UNVERIFIED by direct fetch, treat ranges as "commonly quoted."
- Full professional audit: typically **$200-$700**, average ~$416-$437 (HomeAdvisor data cited by Energy Harbor https://www.energyharbor.com/energy-resources/energy-savings-tips/diy-home-energy-audit ; Pearl Certification average $437 https://www.pearlcertification.com/ ; This Old House cites ~$400, sometimes free from utilities https://www.thisoldhouse.com/inspections/slash-your-utility-bill-with-a-diy-energy-audit ). Recommend phrasing: "commonly $200-$700 depending on size and market; some utilities offer free or discounted audits."

**DOE Home Energy Score — VERIFIED:**
- DOE announcement: 250,000th Home Energy Score recorded August 2024; score is "an estimate of a home's energy use on a 1 to 10 scale" (10 = uses less energy than ~90% of homes). https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/articles/home-energy-scores-surpass-250000
- Assessment by a certified assessor takes about an hour, collects 40+ data points, and typically costs **$150-$350** (Thurston County WA program page: https://www.thurstoncountywa.gov/climate/home-energy-score ). Required at time-of-sale in Portland, OR; Thurston County, WA (2026). Program FAQ: https://betterbuildingssolutioncenter.energy.gov/home-energy-score/home-energy-score-faqs

**Honest limits (what they can/cannot diagnose):**
- CAN: surface temperature patterns that *suggest* missing insulation, air-infiltration paths at surfaces, moisture anomalies (evaporative cooling), overheating breakers/connections — best with a large indoor/outdoor temperature difference and ideally while a blower door is running.
- CANNOT: measure R-value directly. A thermal image shows *surface* temperatures, not heat flow or material R-value; interpreting wall temperature as "insulation level" requires assumptions about conditions. Low-resolution sensors (80×60, 160×120) cannot resolve narrow framing/stud patterns from a distance. Phone-attached units are screening tools, not calibrated instruments — a finding is a prompt for a professional audit, not a diagnosis.
- UNVERIFIED: the DOE "20°F indoor/outdoor difference needed" rule of thumb for thermography — the DOE thermographic-inspections page 404'd this session; if used, re-verify or attribute to auditor practice.

## 6. Phantom loads / standby power

- **Kill A Watt usage method (site already wires ASIN B00009MDBU = P3 International P4400):** plug the meter into the outlet, plug the appliance into the meter; read instantaneous watts, volts, amps, Hz, power factor, and cumulative kWh over a measured interval (e.g., 24 h); kWh × electric rate = cost. Specs (Circuit Specialists spec sheet, https://www.circuitspecialists.com/p4400_kill_a_watt_energy_meter ): 115 VAC, 15 A / 1,875 W max, wattage accuracy 0.2% (0.2%-2.0% across quantities), large LCD. Street price ~$15-$20.
- **Standby-power estimates:**
  - LBNL Standby Power site (https://standby.lbl.gov/): most products draw **less than 0.5 W** in standby; collectively standby power "amount[s] to 5-10% of residential electricity use." Two decades ago typical standby draws were 1-3 W vs. ~0.5 W today.
  - LBNL whole-house measurement study (OSTI, https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/793739 ): measured total standby per home **14-169 W, average ~67 W**, i.e., 5-26% of those homes' annual electricity use.
  - DOE/FEMP measurement protocol page (for context, commercial framing): https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/measuring-standby-power
- This Old House (competitor) cites standby losses ~10% of electricity costs — roughly consistent with LBNL's 5-10%.
