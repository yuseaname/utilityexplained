Water Meter Running When No Water Is Being Used: Causes and Fixes

Margaret Harrington May 27, 2026 12 min read

If your water meter is still spinning with no water running, you have a leak. Find the cause — usually a running toilet or irrigation line — and stop wasting up to 36,000 gallons a month.

Quick Answer

Yes — if your water meter moves when every faucet and appliance is off, water is flowing somewhere and you have a leak. The most common cause by far is a running toilet (bad flapper), followed by an irrigation line leak — a single running toilet can waste 200+ gallons per day (U.S. EPA WaterSense, 2026).

What to do right now (in this order)

  1. Confirm the leak dial. Learn what the triangle/star spins mean on the [water meter leak indicator](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/) guide.
  2. Test the toilet first. It causes the majority of indoor leaks. Run the [2-minute toilet leak test](/blog/is-your-toilet-running-leak-test/).
  3. Read the meter. Use the [how to read a water meter](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/) walkthrough to quantify your loss in gallons per day.
  4. Check the bill impact. See how leaks drive up your [sewer charges and water bill](/blog/how-sewer-charges-work-on-your-water-bill/).
  5. Stop the waste. Once the leak is fixed, follow the [how to lower your water bill](/blog/44-how-to-lower-water-bill/) guide.

Already see the leak indicator spinning? Skip ahead to the fix:

  • [Running toilet — the #1 cause](/blog/is-your-toilet-running-leak-test/): a 2-minute dye test confirms it. A flapper costs $10–$20.
  • [Confirm you're reading the right dial](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/): the small triangle or star is the leak indicator, not the sweep hand.
  • [Read the numbers](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/): quantify how many gallons you're actually losing per day.

Why Is My Water Meter Running When Nothing Is On?

Your water meter should be completely still when every faucet, appliance, and irrigation zone is off. Water meters are precision instruments required by code; the utility uses them to bill you for every gallon that crosses the line into your home.

If the meter keeps moving, water is flowing through your service line right now — even with no fixture open. That is the definition of a leak, and it will show up on your next [water bill](/blog/why-did-my-water-bill-suddenly-increase/) as a spike.

There are two dials to watch on a standard analog meter (for a full walkthrough of every dial type plus digital and AMI smart meters, see [how to read a water meter](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/)):

  1. The sweep hand — a large hand that rotates to measure gallons. Slow movement means a significant leak, often hundreds of gallons per hour.
  2. The leak indicator (low-flow dial) — a small triangle or star that spins with even a trickle. If this one moves while everything is off, you have a small leak — usually a toilet flapper or a dripping faucet.

Common Causes of a Running Water Meter

When your meter keeps moving with everything off, the cause usually falls into one of three buckets: indoor plumbing leaks, outdoor and irrigation leaks, or the meter itself. Indoor plumbing — especially toilets — drives the majority of residential cases we see.

1. Toilet Flapper Leaks (Most Common)

A faulty flapper is by far the most common reason a meter won't stop. Over time the rubber flapper warps, gets coated in mineral deposits, or simply cracks. Water then trickles from the tank into the bowl continuously, and the fill valve keeps refilling the tank — every drop registers on the meter.

  • Signs: A faint hissing or phantom-flush sound — or nothing at all. Many flapper leaks are completely silent.
  • Impact: A single flapper leak can waste 200 to 400 gallons per day (U.S. EPA WaterSense).

2. Irrigation and Outdoor Systems

Sprinkler systems and pool autofill lines are the second most common culprit. A cracked solenoid valve or a stuck zone valve can let water flow into the lateral lines even when the controller is off. A pool autofill stuck in the open position is one of the most expensive leaks per square foot of property.

  • Signs: Wet spots in the yard, soggy grass, a pool that constantly needs topping off, or a noticeably greener stripe of lawn.
  • Impact: A stuck irrigation valve can waste thousands of gallons per month, especially in summer when tiered rates apply.

3. Hidden Pipe Leaks (Highest Dollar Damage)

Leaks inside walls, under slabs, or behind water heaters are the most dangerous because the water is doing structural damage long before you ever see it. Slab leaks in particular can run for months before detection.

  • Signs: Unexplained damp spots on walls or ceilings, warm spots on the floor (a hot-side slab leak), a sudden drop in water pressure, or a water heater that runs constantly.
  • Impact: High-volume leaks that can add hundreds of dollars to a single bill, plus the cost of foundation or drywall repair.

4. Meter Malfunction or Air in Lines

Sometimes the meter itself is the problem.

  • Air trapped in the line — After municipal main repairs or a long period of non-use, air can get trapped in the meter. The compress/expand cycle makes the dial jerk and registers false consumption.
  • Pressure spikes — Sudden pressure swings from the municipal supply can cause mechanical meters to register phantom movement.
  • Wear and tear — Old mechanical meters can develop internal friction that lets the dial creep even with no real flow.

How to Diagnose the Problem Step-by-Step

Before calling a plumber or your water utility, run these four tests in order. Each one narrows the source down so you don't pay for a full-house repipe when the real issue is a $10 flapper.

Step 1: The All-Off Test

Turn off every water-using appliance and fixture in your home:

  • All faucets and showers (hot and cold)
  • Dishwasher and washing machine
  • Refrigerator ice maker — close the supply valve behind the fridge
  • Hot water heater, if practical
  • Irrigation controller and any outdoor hose bibs

Wait 15 minutes so any residual water in the pipes settles. Then check the meter again. If the leak-indicator triangle is spinning, water is flowing somewhere. For photos of what to look for, see the [water meter leak indicator guide](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/).

Step 2: Isolate Indoor vs. Outdoor

Find your home's main shut-off valve — typically where the water line enters the house, sometimes in a meter box at the property line.

  1. Turn the main supply to the house fully off.
  2. Go back to the meter and watch the dial for 1–2 minutes.
  3. If the meter stops: the leak is on your side — indoors or in your irrigation system.
  4. If the meter keeps moving: the leak is in the service line between the meter and your house, or the meter itself is faulty. Call the utility.

Step 3: Check the Toilets

Toilets are the easiest indoor source to test. Put a few drops of food coloring in the toilet tank. Wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking.

Toilets cause the majority of indoor "mystery" leaks — for the full dye-test walkthrough plus listening tests, see [how to test if your toilet is running](/blog/is-your-toilet-running-leak-test/). A replacement flapper costs $10–$20 and takes about 10 minutes.

Step 4: Check the Meter for Air or Debris

Look closely at the meter face while the All-Off Test is still running.

  • Irregular movement (forward, backward, jerking): likely air in the line or a damaged meter — not a real leak.
  • Smooth, continuous movement: a real, steady flow of water somewhere in the system.
  • Cloudy water or bubbles in the meter glass: debris or air from a recent main break on the municipal side.

When to Call the Water Utility Company

There are specific scenarios where the water company — not a plumber — should be your first call.

1. Meter Testing

If you've turned off the main house valve and the meter is still spinning, the problem is either on the service line between the meter and the street, or the meter itself is defective. Contact your utility to request a meter accuracy test.

  • Fee note: Some utilities charge for the test, refunding the fee only if the meter is found defective. Ask up front whether the test is no-cost when a malfunction is suspected (state public utility commission rules vary).

2. Billing Adjustment for a Confirmed Leak

If you confirm a leak, repair it, and the bill still hasn't been corrected, many utilities allow a one-time billing adjustment when the high usage is tied to a documented, repaired leak.

  • Documentation needed: Plumber receipt, before/after photos of the meter, and the date the leak started and stopped.
  • Don't confuse this with hidden base-rate hikes on your statement — usage spikes from leaks and gradual rate increases are separate issues, even when they hit the same month.

3. Supply Line Responsibility

The leak's location determines who pays. Leaks in the public right-of-way (between the street and your property line) are usually the utility's responsibility. Leaks on your side of the meter are yours. Knowing that boundary matters before anyone digs.

Fixing the Leak: DIY vs. Professional Help

Once you've isolated the source, decide how to fix it.

DIY Fixes

  • Toilet flapper — Inexpensive ($10 – $20) and beginner-friendly. Turn off the supply valve, drain the tank, unhook the old flapper, install the new one. Most hardware stores carry universal flappers.
  • Faucet washer — A slow drip is almost always a worn washer or cartridge. Shut off the supply under the sink, disassemble the handle, and swap the part.
  • Irrigation solenoid — A simple stuck solenoid on a single zone can often be swapped without calling a pro. Cost: $15 – $40 for the part.

When to Call a Pro

  • Slab leaks — Don't try to jackhammer the floor yourself. Pros use acoustic listening devices and thermal imaging to pinpoint the leak with minimal demolition.
  • Main line leaks — The line entering the house may need excavation and partial repipe. Permit requirements vary by jurisdiction.
  • Water heater leaks — If the meter is moving and you see pooling near the heater, the tank is likely failing. Tank replacement typically runs $1,200 – $3,500 installed.

How to Prevent Future Water Meter Leaks

Prevention is cheaper than repair. Once the current issue is fixed, build these habits so the meter stays still when it should.

  1. Monthly meter check — Run the All-Off Test once a month. It takes five minutes and can save hundreds of dollars per avoided leak.
  2. Watch the pressure — Residential water pressure above 80 PSI stresses pipes, valves, and appliance supply lines. Install or service a pressure-reducing valve if your home runs high.
  3. Replace parts at the first drip — A $2 washer today prevents a $200 leak in six months.
  4. Ask about smart meters — Many utilities now offer AMI/smart meters that flag continuous-flow events in real time. Catching a leak the same day it starts is the single best way to limit damage.

FAQ: Water Meter Troubleshooting

Here are the most common questions homeowners ask when their meter starts moving unexpectedly.

Should my water meter be moving when no water is running?

No. In a properly functioning home, the meter should be completely stationary when every water source is shut off. Any movement — even a slow spin of the small leak-indicator triangle — means water is flowing through the system, either from a leak or a meter malfunction. Start with the water meter leak indicator guide to confirm what you’re seeing.

How do I know if the leak is in the toilet?

Run the food coloring test: add a few drops of dye to the toilet tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. If the color seeps into the bowl, the flapper is leaking. Toilets are the #1 cause of a running meter indoors — see the full toilet leak test walkthrough.

What is the leak indicator dial on a water meter?

It is a small triangle or star on the meter face, designed to move with very low flow rates. If this dial is spinning while all water is off, you have a leak — even if the main sweep hand isn’t moving yet. For photos of different dial styles, see the water meter leak indicator guide.

Can air in the pipes cause the meter to run when nothing is on?

Yes. Air trapped in the lines after repairs or long periods of non-use can compress and expand, which makes the dial spin forward and backward irregularly. That movement can register as false consumption on a mechanical meter.

How much water does a dripping tap waste?

A slow drip can waste up to 3,000 gallons per year. That is small compared to a slab leak, but it still adds up to real money on the bill every month — and a dripping tap often points to a washer that will only get worse.

Will the water company fix or test the meter for free?

Most utilities will test the meter, but there may be a fee. If the meter is defective, they typically replace it and refund the test fee. If the meter is accurate, the fee is usually yours to pay. Ask your utility up front whether the test is no-cost when you suspect a malfunction.
