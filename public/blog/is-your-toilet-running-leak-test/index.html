Is Your Toilet Running? A Simple Leak Test (and How It Shows Up on Your Water Bill)

Margaret Harrington December 25, 2025 8 min read

A running toilet can waste 200–400 gallons a day and add $50–$100+ a month to your water bill. Test it in 10 minutes with the food-coloring dye test.

Quick Answer: Is Your Toilet Running?

A running toilet can waste 200–400 gallons a day and add $50–$100+ a month to your water bill (U.S. EPA WaterSense estimate). Test it in 10 minutes: drop 10 drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 10–15 minutes without flushing; if color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking.

Run the 10-Minute Dye Test

The dye test is the fastest way to confirm a toilet leak without any tools. It isolates the flapper, which causes the majority of running-toilet problems.

  1. Remove the tank lid and set it somewhere safe (they crack if dropped).
  2. Add 10 drops of food coloring (or one dye tablet) to the tank water.
  3. Wait 10–15 minutes. Do not flush during this window.
  4. Check the bowl. If any color shows up in the bowl water, water is leaking from the tank into the bowl — the flapper is the prime suspect.
  5. Replace the flapper. A universal flapper is a $10–$15 part at any hardware store, and the swap takes about 15 minutes.

Want a second confirmation? Turn off every faucet and appliance, then watch the [leak indicator dial](/blog/water-meter-leak-indicator-explained/) on your water meter. If it spins, water is moving somewhere. If the dye test was clean but the meter is still moving, you likely have a [hidden leak elsewhere](/blog/water-meter-running-when-no-water-used/).

Why Running Toilets Are Easy to Miss

A running toilet is uniquely good at hiding. The leak is inside a closed tank, the sound is often masked by a noisy house, and the bowl refills on its own — so nothing looks "broken" to the eye.

What you do see is the bill. A flapper that lets a thin stream seep from tank to bowl can run 24 hours a day for weeks before anyone notices. By the time a higher bill arrives, the leak has already cost you.

If you want a refresher on what every line on that bill means, start with [how to read your water bill](/blog/complete-guide-understanding-utility-bill/). If you are in "why did this just spike?" mode, jump to [why water bills suddenly increase](/blog/why-did-my-water-bill-suddenly-increase/).

The meter test (confirms any continuous flow)

Turn off every faucet, ice maker, and appliance that uses water. Then watch your water meter. Most meters have a small leak indicator — a triangle, star, or tiny spinning disk — that moves with very small flows.

  • If the indicator moves: water is flowing somewhere in the house.
  • If the indicator is still: no continuous leak right now.

Need help reading the dial? See [how to read a water meter](/blog/how-to-read-a-water-meter/).

How a Running Toilet Raises Your Water and Sewer Bill

Every gallon that leaks out of the tank is a gallon your meter counts — and in most U.S. cities, the sewer charge is tied directly to your metered water usage. So one toilet leak can quietly double-dip your bill: once on the water side, and again on the sewer side.

Worked example: one stuck flapper, one month

A flapper stuck open at roughly 250 gallons/day:

  • Monthly water use added: ~7,500 gallons (about 10 CCF).
  • Water charge at $4/1,000 gal: ~$30.
  • Sewer charge at $4/1,000 gal: ~$30 (because sewer is usually calculated from water use).
  • Total monthly hit: ~$60, or roughly $700+ a year from a single toilet.

If a number like 10 CCF appears on your bill and you are not sure what it means, see [what CCF means on your water bill](/blog/what-is-ccf-on-a-water-bill/). And to understand the sewer portion specifically, see [how sewer charges work on your water bill](/blog/how-sewer-charges-work-on-your-water-bill/).

How to spot it in your own usage

Compare your current month's usage to the [average water usage per person](/blog/average-water-usage-per-person/) for your household size. If you are running 30–50% higher than expected with no lifestyle change, a running toilet or silent leak is the most common reason.

Common Causes of a Running Toilet

Most running-toilet problems come from three parts inside the tank. You do not need to be a plumber to recognize the categories.

  • Flapper valve (most common): a worn, warped, or mis-seated flapper lets tank water seep into the bowl. The dye test is designed to catch exactly this.
  • Fill valve: the valve that refills the tank can stick partially open or fail to shut off cleanly, causing the tank to top off every few minutes.
  • Tank water level too high: if the float is set above the overflow tube, water trickles down the tube continuously.

All three are inexpensive fixes — usually a $10–$25 parts swap — but only after you have confirmed a leak with the dye test or meter test. Once you have, you are no longer guessing.

What to Do After You Confirm a Leak

Once the dye test or meter test says "yes, water is moving," you have a few safe next steps.

  • Replace the flapper yourself. A universal flapper costs $10–$15 and takes about 15 minutes. No tools required.
  • Re-test the same day. Repeat the dye test after any change. If the bowl stays clear for 15 minutes, the leak is fixed.
  • Verify with a meter reading. Take a meter photo at night (no water use) and again in the morning. If the numbers match, you have a true zero.
  • Ask your utility about a leak adjustment. Many utilities have a one-time leak adjustment policy if you can document the repair. Policies vary by state and provider (state public utility commission).

Not comfortable working inside the tank? A licensed plumber typically charges $100–$200 for a flapper or fill-valve service call — still a fraction of what a year of leaking costs you.

Common Misconceptions About Running Toilets

  • "If it is silent, it is fine." Silent leaks are the most common kind. Many flappers seep without making a sound.
  • "It is only a little water." A slow, continuous flow adds up to thousands of gallons a month. The cost table above shows the math.
  • "My bill would show it right away." Bills are delayed. The leak may have been running for 3–4 weeks before it appears on a statement.
  • "A new toilet can't leak." Even brand-new flappers can misseat or wear out within a few years, especially with hard water.

Next Step: Confirm the Fix on Your Bill

After the repair, your next bill should drop by roughly the amounts in the cost table above. If it does not, the leak was either not fully fixed, or there is a second leak somewhere. Can a running toilet really cause a high water bill?

Yes. A running toilet can waste 200–400 gallons per day, and in many cities the sewer charge is calculated from your water use — so a single leak can raise both the water and sewer portions of your bill at the same time.

How do I know if my toilet is leaking?

Two quick checks: the dye test (add 10 drops of food coloring to the tank, wait 10–15 minutes without flushing, then look for color in the bowl) and the meter test (turn off all water and watch the leak indicator dial on your water meter).

How much does a running toilet add to a water bill per month?

It depends on the leak size. A slow drip adds under $2 a month, a visible running toilet can add $20–$60, and a flapper stuck open can add $30–$90+ per month — often another $20–$60 on top in sewer charges (U.S. EPA WaterSense).

Will a running toilet increase my sewer charge too?

Often, yes. In most U.S. cities, the sewer portion of your bill is based on your metered water usage, so extra metered water from a leak raises both the water and the sewer line items. See how sewer charges work for the details.

What is the most common cause of a running toilet?

A worn or mis-seated flapper valve is the most common cause. Fill-valve issues and a tank water level set too high are the next two most frequent culprits — all three are inexpensive DIY fixes.

Can a running toilet stop on its own?

Rarely. A flapper may temporarily reseat, but the underlying wear usually returns within days. Treat a confirmed leak as a fix-it-now item — the cost table above shows how fast the dollars add up.
