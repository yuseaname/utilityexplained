Why Is My Electricity Bill So High in Summer? (7 Causes + Fixes)

Margaret Harrington December 26, 2025 8 min read

Summer bill shock? Learn how AC, humidity, and peak rates spike your costs--and 7 ways to lower your bill without sweating.

On this page
  • Quick Answer
  • Summer Bill Increase Causes
    • AC Temperature Setting Cost Impact

Quick Answer

Summer electric bills spike because air conditioning drives 40–50% of home energy use during hot months. The biggest cost-cutting levers: raise the thermostat above 78°F, shift laundry and dishes to off-peak hours, and seal duct leaks. Together these can cut cooling costs by 15–30%.

AC is the #1 summer bill driver

Air conditioning is the single largest driver of summer electric bills. A central AC can draw 2,000 to 5,000 watts while running (U.S. EIA, 2025). Run it several hours a day and you can add hundreds of kWh to the bill — which is the most common reason your electric bill is so high in summer.

Humidity makes this worse because the system must remove moisture as well as cool the air. That extra latent-load work increases runtime even if the thermostat setting stays the same.

Window units and portable ACs also add significant kWh, especially if multiple units run at once. Each unit can draw 500 to 1,500 watts, and older units often run at low SEER efficiency.

If your AC runs continuously but the home still feels warm, the system may be undersized, low on refrigerant, or short-cycling. See our guide on AC short-cycling causes and fixes to rule that out first.

Thermostat settings: the cheapest AC control

A lower thermostat setting increases AC runtime. Setting the thermostat to 72°F instead of 76°F can raise energy use by 10–20% depending on your home and climate.

Sunlight and heat gain also matter. South- and west-facing windows can add significant heat in the afternoon. Closing blinds or using light curtains can reduce the load.

Cooking indoors adds heat too. Using the oven frequently in summer can cause the AC to run more to counteract the extra heat.

Using the microwave or outdoor grill for some meals reduces the indoor heat load on very hot days.

AC maintenance keeps efficiency high

Dirty filters and blocked vents make the AC work harder. Replace filters monthly during peak season or as the manufacturer recommends. Keep outdoor condenser coils clear of debris so the system stays near its rated SEER efficiency.

If the system is older or low on refrigerant, it may run longer than normal. A maintenance check can identify issues that quietly raise power use.

Also check that supply vents are not blocked by furniture or curtains. Poor airflow forces longer runtimes to reach the same temperature.

Time-of-use rates can double peak-hour costs

Many utilities charge higher rates during late afternoon and early evening in summer. That is when AC use often peaks. Even if kWh stays the same, the cost per kWh can be 2–3x higher during those hours.

Time-of-use rate plans reward pre-cooling your home earlier in the day and shifting laundry, dishwashing, and pool pumps to off-peak hours.

Tiered rate plans work differently — they charge more as you cross kWh thresholds within a billing cycle, so any month you cross into a higher tier the marginal kWh cost jumps.

Humidity makes the AC run longer

High humidity makes your home feel warmer, so the AC runs longer to reach the same comfort level. In very humid climates, a dehumidifier can improve comfort but it also uses electricity. The combined load can raise kWh by 20–40% if both run frequently.

If you use a dehumidifier, run it during off-peak hours if your rate plan allows. Empty the tank regularly to keep it running efficiently.

Appliances that add hidden heat

Refrigerators, dryers, and ovens release heat into your home. When it is hot outside, that extra heat makes the AC work harder. Running the dryer at night or air-drying clothing can reduce both heat and kWh.

Lighting also adds a small amount of heat. Switching to LEDs reduces both energy use and heat output.

Billing cycle length skews summer comparisons

A longer billing cycle can make a summer bill look worse than it is. Compare kWh per day before drawing conclusions — a 33-day cycle will always be higher than a 28-day cycle even at the same daily usage.

Also check for any seasonal rate adjustments, which are common in summer when demand is higher. If your bill includes a fuel or supply adjustment line, that can shift the total even if kWh stays the same.

Utility programs that lower summer bills

Some utilities offer summer demand-response programs that reward you for reducing usage during peak hours. These programs can lower costs or provide bill credits, depending on the provider (state public utility commission).

If you qualify, time-of-use education programs can help you shift energy use to cheaper hours. Check your utility website for available programs and eligibility.

If your utility offers a smart thermostat rebate, it can be a low-cost way to automate higher settings when you are away without sacrificing comfort when you are home.

Use daily usage data to find AC spikes

Daily or hourly usage charts make it easier to see when the AC runs the most. If the spikes align with afternoon hours, shading windows or pre-cooling earlier in the day may help.

If usage is high overnight, check whether the thermostat is set too low or if the AC runs continuously because the home is not sealing properly.

A quick experiment is to raise the thermostat by one degree for a week and compare daily kWh. Even small changes reveal how sensitive your usage is to temperature settings.

Insulation stops attic heat from reaching you

Attics can reach 130–150°F in summer. If insulation is thin, that heat transfers into your living space and increases cooling demand by 15–25% in many homes. Sealing attic access points and adding insulation reduces heat gain.

For renters, using insulated curtains or reflective window film can reduce heat gain without permanent changes.

If you notice rooms that are consistently warmer, check for missing weatherstripping around doors or gaps around window frames.

Low-effort fixes to cut summer bills

  • Set the thermostat a few degrees higher when away.
  • Use ceiling fans to feel cooler without lowering the AC.
  • Seal air leaks around doors and windows.
  • Clean or replace AC filters regularly.
  • Use blinds on sunny windows in the afternoon.

Small changes can cut cooling runtime, which is the biggest source of summer kWh. For the full playbook, see our complete guide to lowering your electric bill.

If you have rooms you rarely use, close the vents and keep the doors shut so the system focuses on the spaces you actually occupy.

Next step: Pull up your last three bills and divide kWh by days in the cycle. If your summer daily usage is more than 50% above your spring daily usage, the AC is the prime suspect — start with the thermostat, filter, and duct-sealing fixes above.

Is it cheaper to leave the AC running all day?

Usually no. It is more efficient to raise the thermostat when you are away and cool the home when you return. A constant-on strategy usually costs $30–$80/month more than a setback strategy.

Do window units cost less than central AC?

It depends. Window units can be cheaper if you only cool a small area, but they are less efficient for whole-home cooling. A 10,000 BTU window unit at 10 EER costs more per kWh cooled than a modern central system at 16 SEER.

Why is my bill higher even with the same temperature setting?

Hotter weather, higher humidity, and longer daylight hours can increase runtime. Rate changes, a longer billing cycle, or crossing into a higher tier can also raise the total even if kWh stays flat.

How much can raising the thermostat by 1 degree save?

About 3–5% of cooling cost per degree in most homes. Raising from 72°F to 78°F typically saves $36–$72/month during peak summer.

Why does my AC run but the house still feels warm?

Common causes: undersized system, low refrigerant, dirty coils, blocked ducts, or the system is short-cycling. A service tech can confirm which one it is. See our guide on AC short-cycling causes and fixes.

Does closing vents in unused rooms save energy?

Usually no. Central AC systems are balanced for full airflow. Closing vents raises duct pressure, can leak air at joints, and in some systems makes the AC run longer — costing more, not less.
