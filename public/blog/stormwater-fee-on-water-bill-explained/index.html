Stormwater Fee on Your Water Bill: What You

Margaret Harrington May 27, 2026 14 min read

Most people don't know they're paying a stormwater fee on their water bill. Here's what this charge funds, how it's calculated, and if you can get a discount.

Quick Answer

A stormwater fee covers the cost of managing rainwater runoff from streets and properties. It is usually a flat monthly charge on your water or utility bill, separate from your water usage charges. You typically cannot reduce it by using less water, but some utilities offer discounts for installing permeable surfaces or rain barrels.

What Is a Stormwater Fee Exactly?

To understand the stormwater fee on water bill, you must first understand the difference between a utility service and a tax. Many municipalities explicitly state that this charge is a user fee, not a tax. For example, the City of Alexandria, Virginia, clarifies on its official website that the Stormwater Utility Fee is included as a separate line item on the real estate tax bill but is "not a tax." It is a utility user fee modeled after how residents are billed for other services like water and sewer.

This distinction is important for legal and financial reasons. Taxes are generally revenue sources for general government operations, whereas utility fees are tied to a specific service provided to the user. In the case of stormwater, the "service" is the maintenance of the drainage system that prevents your property from flooding and keeps local waterways clean.

The concept of charging property owners directly for stormwater management has gained significant traction. Unlike temporary [drought surcharges](/blog/drought-surcharge-on-water-bill-explained/) that may come and go, stormwater fees are typically permanent charges. in recent years. A notable legislative precedent was set by the Maryland General Assembly, which enacted a stormwater management fee program in House Bill 987 (April 2012). Signed into law by then-governor Martin O'Malley, this law applied to the largest urban jurisdictions in Maryland, including nine counties and the City of Baltimore. The goal was to create a dedicated funding stream to meet environmental mandates and infrastructure needs.

Before these fees were introduced, stormwater management was often funded through general taxes or water rates that didn't account for the actual volume of runoff a property generated. This led to a situation where a small home with a small roof paid the same for drainage as a large commercial lot with acres of concrete. Modern stormwater fees aim to correct this by charging based on the property's contribution to the runoff problem.

Why Are You Being Charged? The Problem with Runoff

You might wonder why you need to pay to manage rain. After all, water falls from the sky for free. The issue lies in what happens when that water hits your property. When rain falls on natural ground, it soaks into the soil. However, when it hits impervious surfaces like asphalt driveways, concrete sidewalks, and metal roofs, it flows rapidly into storm drains.

This rapid flow, known as stormwater runoff, carries with it a cocktail of pollutants. As it travels across your property, it picks up oil, grease, fertilizers, pesticides, pet waste, and sediment. This contaminated water flows directly into local creeks, rivers, and oceans without being treated at a water facility. This pollutant loading degrades water quality and harms aquatic ecosystems.

the sheer volume of runoff can overwhelm municipal drainage systems. During heavy storms, pipes and inlets that convey stormwater may not be able to handle the flow, leading to street flooding and basement backups.

Municipalities like Philadelphia have been transparent about where this revenue goes. The Philadelphia Water Department states that revenue generated from stormwater billing funds a variety of activities. These include the maintenance of the city's network of pipes and inlets. Crucially, it also funds stormwater management and stream restoration projects designed to reduce combined sewer overflows and pollutant loading to the city's streams and waterways.

The cost of maintaining this infrastructure is rising. As climate change leads to more frequent and intense precipitation events, the strain on these systems increases. To pay for these increasing costs, municipalities have adopted stormwater utility fees because they are modeled after the way cities have historically billed residents for other utility services. This ensures a stable revenue stream that isn't subject to the fluctuations of general tax revenues.

How Is the Stormwater Fee Calculated?

One of the most common questions homeowners ask is how the number on their bill is determined. Unlike your standard water bill, which is based on how many gallons you use, the stormwater fee on water bill is typically based on the size of your property's impervious surface area.

The Concept of Impervious Surface

Impervious surface refers to any area on your property where water cannot soak into the ground. This includes:

  • Roofs
  • Driveways
  • Walkways
  • Patios
  • Concrete foundations

The logic is straightforward: the more hard surface you have, the more runoff you generate. Therefore, you are charged more.

Single Family Equivalent Unit (SFEU)

To simplify billing, many cities use a standard unit of measurement called a Single Family Equivalent Unit (SFEU). This allows the utility to standardize the charge across different property types.

For instance, the City of Raleigh, North Carolina, defines 1 SFEU as 2,260 square feet of impervious surface. Their approved stormwater fee rate per SFEU is **$8.22**. If you have a typical single-family home, you might be billed for one SFEU. However, if your property is larger or has extensive concrete coverage, the fee scales up.

Commercial properties are calculated differently due to their larger footprint. Using the Raleigh example, a commercial property with 47,303 square feet of impervious surface would be assessed based on how many SFEUs that area represents, resulting in a significantly higher bill than a residential home.

Billing Methods Vary by City

Not every municipality adds this charge to your monthly water check. The billing method depends on local policy:

  1. Water Bill: Many cities bundle the fee into the monthly water and sewer statement for convenience.
  2. Property Tax Bill: Some jurisdictions, like Alexandria, Virginia, and Charleston, South Carolina, bill this annually through the tax assessor's office.
  3. Separate Statement: In rarer cases, it may arrive as a standalone bill.

In Charleston, South Carolina, the Stormwater fee is billed on an annual basis through the County Tax Offices. The County Tax Bill received in late 2025, for example, will cover the stormwater fee service period of calendar year 2026. This timing difference can confuse homeowners who expect to see the charge on their monthly utility statements.

How Much Does a Stormwater Fee Cost?

The cost of a stormwater utility fee varies significantly depending on your location, the size of your property, and the local infrastructure needs. However, having a baseline understanding of average costs helps you budget effectively.

According to industry data from Rainplan, the average stormwater fee in the United States is approximately **$12** billed quarterly. This translates to roughly **$48 per year** for a standard residential property. However, this is just an average. Actual fees can range from **$5 to over $50** per month depending on the municipality.

The Impact of Fee Increases

Stormwater fees are not static. As infrastructure needs grow, cities vote to increase these rates. For example, Fort Worth, Texas, proposed a FY26 Stormwater Utility Fee Increase. Their documentation notes that until January 1, 2026, the current rates apply, but the increased fee would bring a full annual increase of **$3.4 million** during fiscal year 2027.

Similarly, Portland, Oregon, announced a new Flood Safety Benefit Fee that Portlanders will see on their utility bills starting July 1, 2026. This fee is specifically designated to pay for flood safety infrastructure along the Columbia River. Collecting this fee helps the City of Portland make required payments to the Urban Flood Safety and Water Quality District.

When you look at your stormwater fee on water bill, compare it to these benchmarks. If your fee seems disproportionately high, it may be due to a larger impervious surface area or a specific local infrastructure project requiring funding.

Why It's Often Bundled with Water

Municipalities prefer to bill stormwater fees alongside water bills for efficiency, similar to how [water service charges](/blog/water-service-charge-explained/) are often grouped on your statement.. The water utility already collects property data, manages billing cycles, and has established customer service channels. Adding a stormwater line item requires minimal additional administrative cost compared to setting up a separate billing system.

However, this bundling can blur the lines between "using water" and "managing runoff." If you see a line item that says "Stormwater Management" or "SWMS Charge" (Stormwater Management System), know that this is a separate calculation from your water meter reading. Even if you move into a house and reduce your water usage to zero, you will likely still owe the stormwater fee because your roof and driveway still exist and still generate runoff.

Can You Reduce or Exempt Your Stormwater Fee?

This is the most actionable part of understanding this charge. Since the fee is often based on impervious surface area, you can potentially lower it by reducing that area or improving drainage on your property.

Permeable Surfaces

The most effective way to lower your fee is to convert impervious surfaces into permeable ones. If you replace a concrete driveway with permeable pavers or gravel, water can soak into the ground rather than flowing into the storm drain. Some cities offer credits for this.

Green Infrastructure

Installing rain gardens, rain barrels, or cisterns can capture runoff on-site. By storing rainwater for irrigation or allowing it to infiltrate the soil slowly, you reduce the volume of water entering the municipal system.

Exemption Applications

Some cities allow property owners to apply for an exemption or a reduced rate if they can prove they manage their stormwater effectively.

  1. Document the Work: Keep records of any landscaping changes that reduce runoff.
  2. Check Local Policy: Visit your city's public works or water department website. Look for "Stormwater Fee Exemptions" or "Credits."
  3. Apply Annually: In many cases, these exemptions are not automatic. You must submit an application proving your property meets the criteria.

While stormwater fees are specific, they contribute to your overall utility burden. Future Trends: What to Expect in 2026 and Beyond

Looking ahead, the landscape of stormwater billing is changing. Climate change is driving more extreme weather events, which necessitates more robust infrastructure. Consequently, expect to see more municipalities implementing or increasing these fees.

The trend is moving toward more specific, benefit-based fees. As seen in Portland, Oregon, new fees like the Flood Safety Benefit Fee are emerging to target specific risks like river flooding. This indicates that stormwater charges may become more granular, potentially varying by flood zone rather than just property size.

regulatory pressure is increasing. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and state environmental agencies enforce stricter water quality standards. Municipalities must meet these standards to avoid federal fines. The revenue from stormwater fees is often the only reliable way to fund the massive capital projects required to upgrade aging pipe networks and build green infrastructure.

For homeowners, this means budgeting for potential increases. If your city is in a growth phase or facing aging infrastructure issues, a fee adjustment is likely. Keep an eye on city council meeting minutes regarding utility rates. Most cities are required to hold public hearings before increasing utility fees, giving you a chance to understand the justification before the new rates hit your bill.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is the stormwater fee considered a tax?

No. Most jurisdictions explicitly classify this as a utility user fee, not a tax. For example, the City of Alexandria states that the Stormwater Utility Fee is not a tax, but a charge for a specific service (runoff management). This distinction matters because fees are tied to the service provided and the property’s impact on the system, whereas taxes are broad revenue sources.

Why is the fee on my water bill if I don't use water for it?

The fee is bundled with the water bill for administrative efficiency. The water utility already has your billing information and collection systems. However, the charge itself is not based on water consumption. It is based on the size of your property’s impervious surface, which generates runoff regardless of whether you use indoor water.

Can I get a discount if I have a small roof?

Yes, in many cases. Since the fee is calculated based on impervious surface area (like the Single Family Equivalent Unit used in Raleigh), a property with less hard surface area will be billed less. If your property is smaller than the standard unit definition, you may qualify for a reduced rate. You must check with your local utility provider to see if they offer pro-rated billing for smaller properties.

What happens if I don't pay the stormwater fee?

Failure to pay this fee is treated similarly to non-payment of water or sewer charges. Depending on the municipality, late fees may be applied, or the amount could be added to your property tax lien. In some cases, consistent non-payment can lead to service interruption or legal action, similar to other utility defaults.

Does the fee cover water main breaks or leaks?

Generally, no. Stormwater fees fund the drainage system (pipes, inlets, catch basins) that handles rainwater. They do not typically fund the potable water distribution system or wastewater treatment. However, in combined sewer systems (common in older cities like Philadelphia), stormwater and wastewater pipes are connected, so the fee helps maintain infrastructure that handles both flows during heavy rain.

Why did my stormwater fee increase suddenly?

Sudden increases are often due to scheduled rate hikes approved by the city council to fund new infrastructure projects or to catch up with inflation. For instance, Fort Worth proposed a significant increase to generate $3.4 million in additional revenue. Always check utility announcements or city council meeting summaries to understand the specific reason for a rate change in your area.

Can I appeal my stormwater assessment?

Yes. If you believe your impervious surface area was miscalculated, you can usually appeal the assessment. You may need to provide a survey or a site plan showing the actual square footage of your driveway and roof. Contact your local water department or tax assessor’s office for the specific appeal process and deadlines.

Conclusion

The stormwater fee on water bill is a legitimate charge designed to fund the critical infrastructure that manages rainwater runoff, prevents flooding, and protects local waterways. While it may feel like an extra cost, it represents a shift toward more equitable billing where property owners pay based on their contribution to the runoff problem rather than a flat tax.

Understanding how this fee is calculated—whether by impervious surface area or a standard SFEU—allows you to verify its accuracy. Exploring options for permeable surfaces or green infrastructure may offer a path to reduce this cost in the long term. As cities like Portland, Fort Worth, and Raleigh continue to update their systems and rates, staying informed about your local utility policies is the best way to manage your household budget.

By recognizing the difference between this fee and your standard water consumption charges, you can better plan for your utility expenses. Remember to review your bill annually, check for exemptions, and keep an eye on local legislative changes that might affect your rate. This fee is a small but necessary part of maintaining the safety and environmental health of your community.
