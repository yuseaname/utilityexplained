What Is CCF on a Water Bill? (Cubic Feet Explained in Plain English)

Margaret Harrington December 25, 2025 7 min read

CCF on a water bill means 'hundred cubic feet' — 1 CCF equals 748 gallons. Here's what your CCF number means, how to convert it, and how to tell if a hidden leak is driving up your bill.

Quick Answer

CCF means "hundred cubic feet" — 1 CCF equals 748 gallons of water. A typical household uses about 2–4 CCF per person per month; if your number is well above that range, a hidden leak (most often a running toilet) is the usual reason. Got the definition? Good — now the part most people miss: what your CCF number reveals about your bill. Once you know your CCF, you can tell in about 30 seconds whether your usage is normal or whether water (and money) is quietly leaking. Two things to know up front:

  • A typical household uses roughly 2–4 CCF per person per month. If yours is a lot higher, a hidden leak is the usual suspect.
  • Your CCF also drives your sewer charges — so one high number raises both bills.

Run the 30-second check in "Is your CCF normal?" below, or jump straight to finding a leak.

What does CCF mean on a water bill?

If you opened your water bill and saw "CCF," you are not alone. It is one of those utility terms that feels obvious to the utility and totally unclear to everyone else.

Here is the simple definition: CCF means "hundred cubic feet." The first "C" is the Roman numeral for 100, and "CF" means cubic feet. It is a unit of volume — how much water flowed through your meter during the billing period.

This article gives you a plain-English conversion you can trust, plus a quick way to connect your CCF number to what you actually pay. Since many sewer charges are calculated from your CCF usage, understanding this unit helps you track both your water and sewer costs.

For help understanding other parts of your bill beyond usage, check out our guide on water service charges. If you want the big picture first, start with our Water Service Explained pillar.

CCF vs cubic feet vs gallons (and why bills use different units)

Utilities measure water in volume. The most common units you'll see are gallons, cubic feet (ft³), and CCF.

  • 1 cubic foot = 7.48 gallons
  • 1 CCF = 100 cubic feet = 748 gallons

So if your bill says you used 6 CCF, that is about 4,488 gallons (6 × 748).

Some cities use kgal (thousand gallons) instead. The important thing is that the unit is just a way to count volume — it does not change the actual amount of water you used. However, some utilities apply tiered water rates, where the price per CCF increases as your usage goes up.

Quick conversions you can memorize

If you want a quick mental shortcut, these are the most useful:

Bill unit Roughly equals
1 cubic foot 7.5 gallons
1 CCF 750 gallons
2 CCF ~1,500 gallons
10 CCF ~7,500 gallons

The goal isn't perfect math. The goal is being able to look at your bill and think, "Is this number in the right ballpark?" If the gallons on your bill come out well above the typical range for your household size, that is a red flag — not because of the unit, but because of what the number is telling you.

How CCF turns into charges on your bill

Once your utility has a usage number, it applies your local rates. That rate structure can be simple or complicated depending on where you live.

Flat-rate pricing

With a flat rate, each CCF costs the same. Your usage charge is basically: CCF × rate. The dollar ranges in the table below show what 2–8 CCF typically costs a household — usage only, before fixed service fees, sewer, and stormwater are added.

Tiered pricing

With tiers (block pricing), the first chunk of usage is cheaper and later chunks are more expensive. That can make it look like your "price per unit" changed month to month, even though the tiers themselves are fixed. See our guide to tiered water rates for how the blocks work.

If your bill also includes sewer, your water usage may drive that charge too. See how sewer charges work for the plain-English model.

CCF only drives the usage part of your bill. Most bills also include a fixed water service charge, and many add a separate stormwater fee for runoff — neither of which depends on your CCF.

Is your CCF normal? A 30-second check

The fastest way to build confidence is to connect three things: (1) your meter, (2) your bill, and (3) what your household is doing.

1) Compare the meter to the bill

Your bill lists a previous and current meter reading. Subtract to get usage. If you want the step-by-step, see how to read a water meter.

2) Check whether the jump makes sense

If your usage suddenly spiked, your next step is usually to rule out leaks — especially toilets. A running toilet can quietly waste 1+ CCF per day (hundreds of gallons) without making a sound. Our troubleshooting guide is a simple toilet leak test (it takes minutes).

3) Use "normal" as context (not as a rule)

Households vary, but having a rough benchmark helps. See average water usage per person for a practical range. If your number is well above the typical band for your household size and nothing has changed about your routine, that is the point to start looking for a leak — not to assume the utility made a mistake.

Common misconceptions about CCF

  • "CCF is water pressure." No — pressure is measured in psi. CCF is strictly volume.
  • "CCF is a fee." CCF is the measurement. The fee comes from the rate applied to that measurement.
  • "The utility is hiding gallons." Usually it's just a standard unit that's convenient for billing. CCF and gallons describe the same water; CCF is simply the unit your meter records in.

How many gallons are in 1 CCF?

1 CCF (hundred cubic feet) is 100 cubic feet, which is about 748 gallons.

What does CCF stand for on a water bill?

CCF stands for “hundred cubic feet.” The first “C” is the Roman numeral for 100, so CCF means 100 cubic feet (CF). One CCF equals about 748 gallons, and it is the volume unit most water meters use to measure how much water you used during the billing period.

Is CCF the same as a cubic foot?

No. One CCF equals 100 cubic feet. A cubic foot is the smaller base unit; CCF is a larger bundled unit used for billing.

Why does my bill use CCF instead of gallons?

Some utilities use cubic feet/CCF because many water meters record in cubic feet. Billing in CCF is a convenient way to summarize volume.

Is a high CCF number a sign of a leak?

Often, yes. If your CCF is well above the typical 2–4 CCF per person per month and nothing has changed about your routine, a hidden leak — usually a running toilet — is the most common cause. A simple toilet dye test can confirm it in minutes.

Does CCF affect my sewer bill too?

In many cities, yes. Sewer charges are often calculated from your water usage (in CCF or gallons), so a high water bill can pull your sewer bill up with it. The exact rule varies by utility, so check your bill or your utility’s website for how they handle it.
