Tiered Electricity Rates Explained (Tier 1 vs Tier 2 on Your Bill)

Margaret Harrington December 26, 2025 8 min read

Tiered electricity rates charge a lower price per kWh up to a baseline allowance (often 400–500 kWh), then a higher price above it. Learn how tiers work, where they show up on your bill, and why a small usage increase can spike the total.

On this page
  • Quick Answer

Quick Answer

Tiered electricity rates charge a lower price per kWh up to a baseline allowance (often 400–500 kWh in summer), then a higher price above it. Tier 2 typically runs 10–30% over Tier 1, so crossing the threshold by 20–50 kWh can spike your bill — only the over-baseline kWh pay the higher price.

The problem: you used a little more, but the price jumped a lot

If your electric bill suddenly jumped and you did not change much, tiered pricing is often the hidden reason. You cross a threshold, and the next block of electricity costs more. It feels like a penalty, but it is just how the rate is structured.

This guide turns tiered rates into something you can see and predict. If you want the full electricity basics first, start with Electricity Explained, or jump straight to understanding kWh usage to make the numbers feel less abstract.

How tiered electricity rates work (simple example)

Tiered rates are built like a staircase. The first step — the baseline allowance — is priced lower per kWh. Each step above it is priced higher. You only pay the higher price for the kWh that land on that step, not your whole bill. Many U.S. utilities set a monthly baseline that ranges from about 300 kWh to 800 kWh depending on climate, household size, and whether you have electric heat (U.S. EIA, 2025).

Baseline allowance and tier thresholds

A common structure looks like this:

  • Tier 1 (Baseline): first 500 kWh at a lower price, e.g. $0.13/kWh
  • Tier 2 (Over Baseline): next 500 kWh at a higher price, e.g. $0.17/kWh
  • Tier 3 (Excess): usage above 1,000 kWh at the highest price, e.g. $0.22/kWh

If you used 520 kWh in a month, only the last 20 kWh are billed at the Tier 2 price. The first 500 kWh stay at the cheaper Tier 1 rate. That is why tiers feel like a "penalty" only when you push well past the baseline.

Why the next tier costs more

Utilities price the upper blocks higher to discourage peak demand and to cover the higher cost of generating or buying extra power during hot afternoons. The first block is meant to cover basic needs at an affordable rate; the higher tiers price "discretionary" usage — pool pumps, window AC units running all day, a second fridge in the garage — at a premium.

It is a pricing structure, not a personal judgment. Once you see the threshold, the bill becomes predictable.

Worked example. Say your utility charges $0.13/kWh for the first 500 kWh and $0.17/kWh above that. A household using 520 kWh pays:

  • Tier 1: 500 kWh × $0.13 = $65.00
  • Tier 2: 20 kWh × $0.17 = $3.40
  • Total usage charge: $68.40

Now increase usage to 700 kWh (only 200 kWh above baseline):

  • Tier 1: 500 kWh × $0.13 = $65.00
  • Tier 2: 200 kWh × $0.17 = $34.00
  • Total usage charge: $99.00

Same baseline, same Tier 1, but the bill jumped by $30.60 — a 45% increase in usage charges for about 35% more kWh. That math is exactly why a small usage bump can produce a big bill jump. To see the kWh side of this in plain terms, see understanding kWh usage.

Where tiered rates appear on your electric bill

Most bills list usage charges in separate lines. Look near the middle of the page, where the per-kWh rate and the quantity in kWh are broken out. You might see "Tier 1" and "Tier 2" or "Baseline" and "Over Baseline". Each line shows three things: the kWh in that block, the price per kWh, and the dollar amount.

A typical line block looks like this:

  • Tier 1 (Baseline): 500 kWh × $0.13 = $65.00
  • Tier 2 (Over Baseline): 120 kWh × $0.18 = $21.60

Multiply the kWh by the per-kWh price to verify the math yourself. If the two numbers on the line do not match, call your utility's billing line and ask for the rate schedule (state public utility commission consumer hotline can also help).

If your bill has many other lines beyond tiers — supply, delivery, riders, taxes — this walkthrough helps you find the right section: how to read electric bill line items.

Why your electric bill jumps with small usage changes

Two small shifts can cause a big total change:

  • You crossed the tier threshold. Even 20–50 kWh above baseline can move those kWh into Tier 2, raising the effective rate on the whole bill.
  • Your billing cycle was longer. A 33-day cycle vs a 28-day cycle is about 18% more days, so 18% more kWh for the same lifestyle.

Ask yourself: did this bill cover more days than last month? If so, compare kWh per day, not total kWh. The full checklist is in why your electric bill is so high and the bill-spike guide for any month.

Also note the difference between tiered rates and time-of-use pricing. Time-of-use is about when you use power, not how much. If you see peak and off-peak lines on the same bill, read time-of-use electricity rates. And if you want to know whether your plan is fixed or variable before you try to game the tiers, see fixed vs variable utility rates.

If your bill also lists a separate demand charge, that is a different concept based on your highest single hour of use, not total kWh. See demand charges explained.

Common misconceptions about tiered rates

  • "All my kWh are billed at the higher rate." No. Only the usage above the threshold is billed at the higher tier. The first block stays at the lower price.
  • "Tiered rates and time-of-use are the same." Tiered is based on total usage. Time-of-use is based on time of day. A bill can use both — a Tier 1 peak rate and a Tier 1 off-peak rate.
  • "Tiered rates mean the utility made a mistake." It is a common pricing structure, not an error. Tiered pricing is used by many utilities and is approved by state regulators.
  • "A longer bill cycle is 'free' usage." No. Each extra day adds kWh at whatever tier you land in. A 33-day cycle can cost $10–$30 more than a 28-day cycle even with the same daily habits.

If you also see separate supply and delivery charges, that is another layer of the bill. This guide keeps them clear: supply vs delivery charges.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are tiered electricity rates?

Tiered rates mean the price per kWh changes after you use a certain amount of electricity. The first block is cheaper, and the next block costs more. Only the kWh above the threshold pay the higher price.

Does a higher tier apply to my whole bill?

No. Only the usage above the tier threshold is billed at the higher price. The first block stays at the lower price, so a small over-baseline amount does not reprice your entire bill.

Why did my bill jump when my usage barely changed?

Small increases can push you into a higher tier, and longer billing cycles add days of usage. Compare kWh per day to see the real change, and check whether this bill covered more days than last month.

Where do tiers show up on an electric bill?

They usually appear as separate usage lines labeled Tier 1 and Tier 2, or Baseline and Over Baseline. Each line lists the kWh in that block, the per-kWh price, and the dollar amount.

Are tiered rates the same as time-of-use rates?

No. Tiered rates depend on total usage. Time-of-use rates depend on when you use electricity. A bill can use both at once — for example, a Tier 1 peak rate and a Tier 1 off-peak rate.
