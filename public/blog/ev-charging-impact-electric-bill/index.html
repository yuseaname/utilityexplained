How Much Does EV Charging Add to Your Electric Bill?

David Chen August 10, 2026 7 min read

EV charging adds roughly 320 kWh a month for a typical US driver. The real cost math, plus how off-peak rates cut the price of every charge.

Quick Answer

Expect an extra $30–$60 per month for a typical US driver. That's roughly 320 kWh of home charging (about 13,500 miles a year at 3–4 miles per kWh) at typical residential rates. Off-peak time-of-use charging can cut that meaningfully — while DC fast charging runs 2–4× home rates and should stay a road-trip tool, not a habit.

The bill comes down to three numbers.

  • Miles driven — the average US driver covers about 1,100 miles a month.
  • Efficiency — typical EVs run 3–4 miles per kWh; big trucks can be near 2.
  • Your rate and timing — flat vs time-of-use decides the multiplier.

Run the full math in the three numbers, or compare directly with gasoline costs. New to the units? Start with what a kWh actually is.

What to Expect on Your Bill

Charging an electric vehicle at home typically adds $30 to $60 to a monthly electric bill, with most drivers falling somewhere between $40 and $80 depending on mileage and local rates. That works out to roughly $0.04 to $0.06 per mile, well below what a comparable gas vehicle costs to fuel. The added draw is large enough to notice on the bill: most EV owners report a 15 to 30 percent jump in total household electricity consumption once they start charging regularly at home.

Over a full year, home charging runs about $500 to $800, compared with $1,200 to $1,800 for gasoline at typical prices and mileage for a similar driver. Even after accounting for the higher electric bill, most owners come out ahead by $50 to $100 per month on fuel costs alone. The exact number depends on how much you drive, what you pay for electricity, and when you plug in.

Those three variables — mileage, rate, and timing — are the numbers worth understanding before you commit to a charger.

The Three Numbers Behind Your Bill

Your electric bill comes down to three numbers: how many miles you drive each month, how efficiently your EV turns electricity into miles, and what you pay per kilowatt-hour. Monthly mileage sets the baseline. Light drivers cover around 500 miles, average drivers about 1,000, heavy drivers 1,500, and long commuters roughly 2,000. The typical American driver lands near 1,100 to 1,125 miles per month.

EV efficiency varies more than most people expect. A Tesla Model 3 delivers about 4.2 miles per kWh, while a Rivian R1T gets closer to 1.8. That gap nearly doubles your charging cost for the same miles driven. Pick the wrong vehicle for your rate plan and the savings you expected can shrink fast.

Your electricity rate matters just as much as the other two. Flat-rate plans run 12 to 15 cents per kWh, tiered plans span 10 to 20 cents, and time-of-use off-peak windows typically run 8 to 12 cents. The core formula is simple: miles driven divided by miles per kWh equals the kWh you’ll need to charge.

Off-Peak Charging and Time-of-Use Rates

Off-peak charging on a time-of-use (TOU) rate plan can cut your home charging cost by 70 to 80 percent compared with charging during weekday peak hours. Most TOU plans divide the day into three windows: off-peak from 9pm to 9am on weekdays and all weekend at roughly 8 to 12 cents per kWh, mid-peak from 9am to 4pm at 14 to 18 cents per kWh, and peak from 4pm to 9pm at 28 to 40 cents per kWh. For a Tesla Model 3 driven 1,000 miles a month, that translates to about $23.80 when you charge overnight, $38.08 during mid-peak, and $90.44 if you charge on peak rates — a swing of roughly $66 a month for the same electricity. Some utilities go further with a dedicated EV rate plan or a separate EV meter, which can push overnight rates even lower for households that charge after 9pm. The savings depend entirely on when you plug in, not just how much you drive. For the broader playbook, see what time electricity is cheapest.

EV vs Gasoline: Real Cost Comparison

The real cost gap between gas and electric shows up clearly in the monthly fuel bill. A 25 MPG gas car driven 1,000 miles at $3.50 per gallon costs about $140. The same miles in a Tesla Model 3 on a flat 14¢/kWh rate run roughly $33, since the car uses about 240 kWh at around 4 miles per kWh. If that owner shifts to a time-of-use plan and charges off-peak, the monthly bill falls to roughly $24, or about $288 for the year.

Against a 25 MPG gas baseline, EV owners typically save $93 to $116 each month, which adds up to more than $1,000 a year. The savings widen with more miles driven, since a gas car’s fuel cost scales with every gallon while an EV’s electricity cost stays comparatively low. Higher pump prices stretch the gap further, so heavier commuters see the largest dollar benefit — money that compounds alongside other moves in our guide to lowering utility bills.

Home Charging vs Public Stations

Home charging is almost always the cheapest way to fuel an EV, and the gap over public stations is wide enough that most owners reserve public stops for road trips or for apartment dwellers who lack a dedicated outlet. On a flat residential rate, adding 50 kWh at home runs roughly $6 to $7.50, and a time-of-use off-peak plan can pull that down to about $4 to $6. Public Level 2 stations typically price electricity at 20 to 30 cents per kWh, putting the same 50 kWh in the $10 to $15 range, while DC fast charging climbs to 30 to 50 cents per kWh, or $15 to $25 for 50 kWh. That makes DC fast charging roughly two to four times the average home rate, a premium that adds up quickly if it becomes a habit. For routine daily driving, plugging in at home wins on cost and convenience. Public networks still earn their place for road trips, homes without overnight charging, or renters with no outlet.

Charging Speeds, Panels, and Practical Concerns

Charging speed shapes the whole experience of owning an EV at home. Level 1 charging, which uses a standard 120V outlet, adds only 3–5 miles of range per hour — enough for a light driver to top off overnight, but painfully slow for anyone covering serious weekly mileage. Level 2 charging at 240V jumps that to roughly 20–40 miles per hour, covering an average driver’s daily use in just a few evening hours. The catch is your electrical panel. A 100A panel might handle a Level 2 charger if you have available headroom, a 150A panel usually can, and a 200A panel takes it without complaint. Before installing, ask your utility whether an EV-specific rate plan or a separate EV meter is available, since off-peak rates in the 8–12¢/kWh range can meaningfully trim your charging bill. The savings only materialize if your panel cooperates and your rate plan rewards overnight charging. Frequently Asked Questions

Will charging an EV increase my electric bill?

Yes, but less than you’d spend on gas.

Typical increase: $30–$80/month for average driving Gas savings: $80–$150/month

Net effect: Most EV owners save $50–$100/month on fuel costs.

Home charging is almost always cheaper.
